**The Whitefield Estates** are Pholtine-administered agricultural colonies established to receive cleared half-blood refugees from Mercygate and similar border registries. Officially, they provide lawful work, food, shelter, moral instruction, and civic supervision to persons who would otherwise become vagrants, bandits, or casualties of the road.

Residents are not slaves. No writ of ownership is held over them, and any adult may petition to depart with travel papers.

This distinction is recited often.

Usually by people who have never had to choose between signing the ledger and watching their children starve.

Among half-orcs, the Whitefields are bitterly described as “slavery with fewer whips and more lectures.” This is legally false and socially understandable. The food is real. The walls are real. The wages are real, though thin after deductions. So is the constant counting, the chapel discipline, the suspicion, and the quiet knowledge that leaving means stepping back into a kingdom that has not yet decided whether you are a person, a problem, or tomorrow’s corpse.

The Pholtines insist the estates are mercy given structure.

The Pelorites call them better than abandonment, and worse than welcome.

House Amadis tolerates them because fewer starving refugees become raiders.

And the half-orcs endure them because survival is still survival, even when it comes with a sermon before breakfast.

## What they actually are

A Whitefield Estate is a large, church-administered agricultural holding run under Pholtine legal authority. Cleared half-orcs and other half-blood refugees from Mercygate may be “offered placement” there.

On paper:

- They are free laborers.
- They receive food, shelter, work, religious instruction, and legal protection.
- They are paid, though much of that pay is offset by food, clothing, tools, lodging, and “debt of placement.”
- They may leave after signing out and receiving travel papers.

In practice:

- Leaving means no housing, no food, no references, no tools, and no guarantee the next village will tolerate you.
- Most arrive half-starved, traumatized, and without kin networks.
- The estate controls the ledgers, work assignments, chapel discipline, and travel passes.
- “Freedom” is real, but shaped like a hallway with one door and wolves outside.

So the half-orc view is understandable:

> “Slavery with fewer whips and more lectures.”

Not fully accurate. Not fully wrong. Which is where the good mud lives.

## Pholtine justification

The Pholtines would be insufferably sincere about it.

They are not trying to be cartoon villains. They believe chaos produces bandits, bandits produce corpses, and unstructured mercy is just negligence wearing a clean robe.

Their position:

> “We do not own them. We order them. There is a difference, and civilization depends upon it.”

Very Pholtus. Technically correct, morally brittle, and absolutely the sort of thing that makes Pelorites develop headaches.

## How daily life feels

The estates are clean. That almost makes it worse.

Rows straight. Ditches maintained. Dormitories whitewashed. Chapel bells at dawn. Work crews counted twice daily. Meals plain but reliable. Children schooled in Common, arithmetic, doctrine, and “proper civic conduct.” Adults attend evening lectures on law, obedience, temperance, hygiene, and the dangers of resentment.

There are fewer beatings than a serf estate. Less arbitrary cruelty. More paperwork. More sermons. More smug little clerks explaining why your hunger is a spiritual opportunity.

The overseers are not called overseers, of course. That would sound bad.

They are **Rectors of Labor**, **Field Wardens**, **Ledger Brothers**, or **White Stewards**.

## The ugly truth

These estates probably do save lives.

That is the annoying part.

A half-orc family released from Mercygate with nothing may die in a ditch, get lynched by a frightened village, be recruited by bandits, or drift to the Qazaq. A Whitefield placement gives them food, walls, papers, work, and a chance their children survive winter.

But it also teaches them, every day, that their freedom exists only inside someone else’s ledger.
