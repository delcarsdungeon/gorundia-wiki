# Hushwick

Hushwick is a small Hairfoot halfling village hidden within the Great Forest, enclosed by a modest wooden palisade and surrounded by old thorn, root, and watching silence.

The villagers call themselves small folk, though they are plainly Hairfoot halflings rather than Tallfellows. This has drawn comment from travelers familiar with woodland halfling communities, since Tallfellows are more commonly associated with deep forest settlements and old elven influence. Hushwick does not have that character. Its people are hearth-bound, close-kin, wary, and deeply rooted in their own closed customs.

The village is small by design. Locals speak of --nine hearths--, and the number appears to carry ritual or customary weight. The settlement does not sprawl beyond its palisade. No outer farms, cottages, or free-standing sheds were seen beyond the wall, and the villagers appeared uncomfortable with the idea of growth beyond the established bounds.

## Appearance and Condition

Hushwick is built low and tight, with thatched roofs, packed-earth paths, small gardens, drying racks, woven fences, and timber buildings sized for halfling use. The palisade is not large enough to resist a proper military force, but it is carefully maintained and treated as more than a simple defense.

The village feels enclosed.

The gates are watched. The walls are repaired with unusual care. Children are kept close. Outsiders are noticed immediately.

There is little visible metalwork. The villagers appear to keep --no iron--. Tools, fastenings, and household goods seem to be made from wood, bone, bronze, copper, stone, antler, or woven material. Iron weapons and armor draw visible discomfort.

## People

The people of Hushwick are Hairfoot halflings: round-faced, close-knit, practical, and family-centered. They are also isolated to an unusual degree.

Several families show signs of long intermarriage. Faces, names, and mannerisms repeat from house to house. Kinship appears dense and complicated, with elders holding strong authority over household matters.

The villagers are not openly hostile, but their hospitality is tense and formal. They speak carefully around outsiders and appear trained from childhood not to volunteer too much.

They are frightened of the forest.

They are also frightened of offending whatever watches from it.

## The Pact

Local people told the party that Hushwick survives because of --a pact--.

They did not explain its full terms.

They referred to unseen watchers or powers as --the Kindly Ones--.

The name was spoken with caution rather than warmth. The villagers did not describe the Kindly Ones clearly, nor did they treat them as gods in the ordinary halfling sense. Their attitude was closer to fearful gratitude: the pact protects the village, but must be honored.

The villagers implied that the pact is old, binding, and dangerous to neglect.

## The Wicker Man

The party witnessed a wicker man rite in or near Hushwick.

The rite was communal, solemn, and clearly part of the village’s pact observance. The villagers treated it as a necessary duty rather than an act of celebration. Food, song, preparation, and ritual order surrounded the event, but the mood was grave.

The wicker figure appeared to represent more than a simple execution or punishment. Household tokens, woven material, and ceremonial handling suggested that the rite carried meaning for the entire village.

The villagers did not speak of it casually afterward.

## Children Taken to the Forest

The party also heard local accounts that some children of Hushwick are taken into the forest and abandoned there.

These children are not seen again.

The practice is not spoken of plainly. Locals use softened language and avoid direct description. It appears to be connected to the pact, the village’s numbers, and the favor of the Kindly Ones.

Occasionally, after such a child is given over, the family receives a small favor.

These favors are modest and domestic rather than grand: a sickness passing, a lost animal returning, a household need unexpectedly met, or some minor misfortune avoided. The villagers seem to treat such favors as signs that the offering was accepted.

## Local Mood

Hushwick is not a free village in the ordinary sense.

It is protected.

It is confined.

It is watched.

The people keep their hearths, tend their gardens, raise their children, and maintain their wall. They observe old rules that outsiders are not trusted to know. They avoid iron. They keep their numbers small. They speak of the Kindly Ones with the careful respect of people who believe survival has a price.

Whatever the truth of the pact, Hushwick endures beneath it.
