**To:** His Lordship, the Lord Marshal  
**From:** Quartermaster Merrec Vane, Field Supply Office  
**Subject:** Effects of Vogel Coinage Collapse Upon Supply, Morale, Refugee Burden, and Border Trade  
**Seal:** Field Quartermaster’s Mark  
**Priority:** Immediate Practical Concern

My Lord Marshal,

I have begun collecting reports from road agents, sutlers, reeves, caravan masters, and baggage officers operating along the Grevandar border and within the contested districts.

The matter of Vogel coinage is no longer theoretical.

It is already damaging supply, pay discipline, market trust, and refugee management. Worse, the damage is uneven, which makes it harder to correct by simple proclamation. In one town a brass manticore still buys bread. In the next it buys laughter. In the third it buys a knife if offered to the wrong baker.

The old ducal system depended on Vogel law being present at both ends of the bargain. That law has now become uncertain. Therefore the coin has become uncertain with it.

## I. Immediate Effects Observed

Vogel lead pennies and brass manticores are being refused outright outside Grevandar, accepted only at steep discount in border markets, and taken reluctantly within Grevandar where ducal officers still stand close enough to punish refusal.

This has produced four immediate consequences.

First, refugees arriving from Vogel lands often possess what they believe to be savings, wages, or dowries, only to discover that outside the duchy those coins are treated as base scrap or tokens of doubtful promise. This is causing visible distress, especially among widows, laborers, discharged servants, and smallholders.

Second, merchants operating near the border are now demanding royal coin, silver by weight, grain, salt, livestock, or written credit backed by a known noble house. They will not take Vogel coin except at ruinous rates. Some refuse it entirely. Several have stated openly that they would rather be paid in oats than manticores.

Third, Vogel soldiers and local levies paid in ducal coin are growing unreliable. Many now understand that their purse is only useful if the duke’s officers remain victorious. Some have begun hoarding royal coin, looting portable goods, or demanding payment in food before obeying orders. This is especially true among toll guards, road wardens, and pressed local men.

Fourth, counterfeit Vogel coin has already appeared. Given the poor metal and poor confidence involved, I regret to report that false manticores are now nearly as trusted as true ones, which is to say not at all. The distinction matters only to clerks and dead men.

## II. Rates Currently Observed

The following rates are not official but are being used in practice:

- In loyal Vogel-held towns: 1 brass manticore is treated at face value by compulsion, though merchants quietly raise prices.    
- In uncertain border towns: 3 to 6 brass manticores are demanded for 1 royal copper’s worth of goods.    
- In Crown-held or Crown-sympathetic markets: 8 to 12 manticores to 1 royal copper, if accepted at all.    
- Lead pennies are often refused entirely unless taken by count and weight for melting.    

These rates change by the hour when soldiers arrive, rumors spread, or a royal patrol is seen on the road.

The common phrase among teamsters is now:

> “A manticore flies only where Vogel watches.”

Crude, but accurate.

## III. Damage to Supply

The baggage trains are affected in three ways.

First, local purchase has become slower. Quartermasters cannot simply buy hay, beans, lamp oil, nails, cart grease, and salt pork by offering coin unless the coin is accepted. Royal coin is accepted almost everywhere, but our stores of it are limited and must be guarded against false claims.

Second, merchants are charging a risk premium when selling into Grevandar. They fear being paid in Vogel coin, robbed by desperate soldiers, or later accused by one side or the other of provisioning the enemy.

Third, grain brokers with prior Vogel contracts are withholding stock until they learn whose writ will be honored after the fighting. They are cowards, but cowards with granaries, so I recommend courtesy until we can afford honesty.

## IV. Morale and Discipline

Among common soldiers, the issue is simple: men want pay they can spend.

Crown troops paid in royal coin remain steady and are increasingly favored by local sellers. This has become a quiet advantage. Our men can buy beer. Vogel men can argue about brass.

Vogel troops are beginning to notice.

Several captured men from minor garrisons carried purses containing almost entirely ducal coin. One had served three years and possessed what he claimed was full back-pay. Outside Grevandar it would barely purchase boots, assuming the cobbler was drunk, charitable, or threatened.

This has produced bitterness. Not yet organized mutiny, but the seed is planted.

A man who learns he has been paid in prison tokens does not become loyal. He becomes available.

## V. Refugee Burden

The largest human cost is among refugees.

A family leaving Vogel land often brings coin, tools, bedding, and small heirlooms. The tools and bedding retain value. The coin does not.

This creates ugly scenes at gates and markets. Refugees believe they are being cheated. Merchants insist they are not charities. Reeves lack clear authority to force acceptance. Priests intervene, which prevents riots but does not fill bellies.

I strongly recommend establishing official redemption tables at controlled locations, guarded and witnessed by Crown officers, reeves, and clergy. Even an unfavorable exchange, if publicly fair and consistent, will calm matters.

The rate should not pretend Vogel coin is sound. That would reward the system that caused this. But the Crown should be seen rescuing people from it.

Suggested emergency rate:

> Ten Vogel pennies or manticores by counted value to one royal copper, with improved relief exchange for soldiers’ widows, refugees with children, and men willing to swear service.

This will cost money. It will also buy loyalty more cheaply than another regiment.

## VI. Political Use

The ducal mint was not merely an economic privilege. It was an instrument of confinement.

The people were paid in coin that lost strength the moment they crossed the border. This kept laborers local, soldiers dependent, merchants licensed, and peasants tied to Vogel markets. It also allowed ducal officers to demand taxes, rents, fines, and debts in stronger terms than wages were paid.

In plain language:

Vogel paid weak and collected strong.

If proper ledgers can be seized, they will likely show:

- Wages paid in ducal brass or lead.    
- Taxes assessed in silver, grain, livestock, or labor.    
- Debts converted through ducal moneychangers.    
- Fines for refusal of lawful ducal tender.    
- Penalties for hoarding royal coin.    
- Exchange privileges granted to loyal houses, reeves, or toll officers.    

Such records would be useful not merely for prosecution, but for explaining to the people why reform is necessary.

A proclamation abolishing the ducal tender without relief would be seen as theft.

A proclamation redeeming it while exposing the abuse would be seen as justice.

## VII. The DeSalle Matter

This also explains, in practical terms, why Baron DeSalle’s so-called unity tavern never functioned as intended.

The tavern was built to display harmony between DeSalle’s people and those over the border. It failed because the men did not arrive as equals.

A DeSalle laborer or soldier paid in proper coin could cross into Vogel lands and spend like a minor lord. His ordinary wages became power the moment they entered a trapped economy where locals were paid in brass and lead.

Reports indicate DeSalle men would cross over, drink heavily, buy rounds, hire music, overtip serving girls, boast, gamble, and behave like temporary nobility. In their own villages they were common men. Across the border, with royal or good baronial coin in their purse, they became kings for a day.

The locals did not experience this as unity.

They experienced it as humiliation wearing boots.

The Vogel-side patrons saw outsiders casually spending what they themselves could not earn in usable money. The DeSalle men saw themselves as generous, worldly, and welcome. The tavern became a stage where one side felt rich and the other felt insulted.

No amount of shared beer corrects that. Beer is powerful, but it is not a miracle, despite what soldiers claim after the sixth cup.

Worse, any Vogel official watching the place would understand the danger. Good coin crossing into Grevandar undermines ducal control. Local girls being tipped in royal copper learn very quickly which coin is real. Local men watching outsiders throw good money around begin asking why their own wages are stamped with a manticore and a lie.

Thus the unity tavern did not fail because men hate each other.

It failed because the money told the truth.

## VIII. Recommendations

1. Establish Crown redemption tables at secured markets, refugee camps, and major road junctions.    
2. Publicly distinguish between innocent holders of Vogel coin and officials who profited from its forced use.    
3. Pay Crown troops visibly in royal coin when possible. Let locals see the difference.    
4. Require army buyers to carry scales, assay tools, and written rates posted openly.    
5. Seize Vogel minting dies, records, and moneychanger ledgers wherever found.    
6. Offer improved exchange to Vogel soldiers who surrender, swear lawful conduct, or enter supervised service.    
7. Forbid private exploitation of desperate coinholders. Unauthorized moneychanging will cause riots if left unchecked.    
8. Use clergy as witnesses. Pelorites for mercy, Pholtans for fair measure, Heironeans for public trust among soldiers.    
9. Investigate noble houses and reeves who demanded taxes in good coin while paying wages in ducal coin.    
10. Treat the currency issue as a weapon already loose in the field, not an accounting concern to be settled after victory.    

## IX. Final Assessment

The collapse of Vogel coinage is not a side effect of the war. It is one of the fronts.

The duke’s authority made the coin useful. The coin made his authority inescapable. Now that his authority is broken in places, the coin breaks with it.

Handled poorly, this will cause hunger, riots, desertion, and resentment against whichever army happens to be closest.

Handled well, it will show the common people that the Crown is not merely taking Vogel lands, but freeing them from a machine they already hated and did not yet know how to name.

Respectfully submitted,
