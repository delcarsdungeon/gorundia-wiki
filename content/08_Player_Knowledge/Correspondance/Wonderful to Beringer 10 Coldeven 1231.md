## Message to Sir Beringer — Rashgar Relay from Bard’s Gate


The spell does not arrive with proper form.

It does not knock politely against the wards.

It does not present credentials in the approved Gorundian sequence, because apparently nobody warned the channel that humans invented etiquette to keep themselves busy while Rome burned.

It shoves.

A sharp twist in the air, a pressure like two fingers pressing against the inside of the skull, and then a familiar voice comes through without waiting for permission.

Male.

Deep.

Amused with itself.

“Sir Beringer Amadis.”

A pause.

“No, do not answer yet. The channel is stable.”

Another pause.

“And before one of your crown-trained whisper clerks begins chewing parchment over custody forms, yes, you know me.”

A faint sound, like someone shifting comfortably into arrogance.

“Rashgar.”

A beat.

“Brother to Rykeena.”

He lets that one land first.

Practical.

Personal.

The part most likely to keep someone from reflexively cutting the spell.

“High Magus of Furyondy, though I understand that means less here than it ought to.”

A smaller pause.

“And apprentice to Count Blackheart.”

That one he enjoys saying.

You can hear it.

Not boastful exactly.

Worse.

Satisfied.

“I mention the last part because it is the most relevant, and because unlike half the titles people vomit into a room before doing useful work, it actually means I was trained by someone worth fearing.”

A beat.

“Also, Wonderful told me to remind you I am Rykeena’s brother, because she believes Gorundians trust blood before competence.”

Another pause.

“She is not entirely wrong.”

The channel hums faintly.

“I am assisting Wonderful.”

The word assisting carries the delicate strain of a man who believes he is performing civilization a favor.

“She asked for a message sent quickly. I am sending it quickly.”

A smaller pause.

“She also used the phrase ‘proper channels,’ then laughed, then said the proper channels were probably buried under three dead nobles and a tax ledger by now.”

His tone warms slightly.

“That part I liked.”

Then the arrogance smooths back into place.

“Message follows.”

A breath.

Then Rashgar’s delivery changes.

Not into Wonderful’s voice exactly.

He is not imitating her.

He is carrying her words with the faint theatrical disdain of someone who thinks transcription is beneath him and yet, annoyingly, is good at it.

“Beringer.”

A pause.

“She did not give title.”

Another pause.

“I suggested she should.”

A beat.

“She told me where I could put the title.”

He continues.

“Beringer, I am not sending this to the King because I do not know if this is a King problem, a Lord Marshal problem, a priest problem, a coin problem, or one of those problems where everybody waits for the correct seal while children starve in a ditch.”

A pause.

“I hate those problems.”

“I am sending it to you because people keep telling me you are the one they meant to put in charge when they still believed the house was not already on fire.”

A faint sound from Rashgar.

“That phrasing is hers. Mostly.”

Then he continues.

“The Grevandar border is cracked open.”

“Not strained.”

“Not troubled.”

“Cracked.”

“People are coming through in numbers now. Not just the clever ones, not just the ones with wagons and cousins and a place to land. Whole families. Bare feet. Bad carts. Sick old people. Mothers carrying children too quiet to be asleep.”

“They are coming with Vogel coin.”

“Lead pennies. Brass manticores.”

“They think it is money.”

“It is not.”

A pause.

Rashgar lets it sit.

Then he adds, in his own voice, mildly irritated:

“I have seen several of these coins. Calling them coin is generous. Calling them metal is also generous.”

Then back to Wonderful.

“They get laughed at in markets.”

“They get cheated by moneychangers.”

“They get told their purse is worth nothing outside the duke’s shadow.”

“Some of them get angry.”

“Some of them break.”

“And some of them get desperate enough that I am having to write this next part down because if I say it wrong, I am going to kill someone.”

The channel goes quiet.

Rashgar does not speak immediately.

For the first time, the smugness slips.

Just a little.

When he resumes, his voice is his own.

Lower.

Less ornamented.

“Some of the refugees tried to sell their children.”

A pause.

No cleverness.

No flourish.

Then, almost unwillingly:

“That is confirmed.”

Another pause.

“It was not phrased as abandonment. Not at first. They called it service. Apprenticeship. Fosterage. A chance for food. A chance to live.”

His voice tightens.

“They were selling them.”

Silence.

A longer one.

Then Rashgar exhales, sharp and angry, as if offended by his own reaction.

“Wonderful stopped the first group before it became a market.”

A beat.

“She may have broken a table.”

Another beat.

“Possibly a man.”

Now the relay resumes, but even Wonderful’s words come through heavier.

“I do not want to bother the King.”

“That sounds stupid when I say it, but I mean it. The King has the throne, the gods, the nobles, the gallows, the whole damn kingdom tilting under him.”

“I do not know if he needs me shouting from Bard’s Gate that the poor are poor.”

“But this is not ordinary poor.”

“This is Vogel poor.”

“This is people who thought they had wages finding out they had leash tokens.”

“This is mothers finding out their savings are decorations.”

“This is fathers staring at a moneychanger and realizing the duke stole from them every payday without even entering the room.”

A pause.

“And now they are moving.”

“Once enough people hear that the borders are not sealed, more will come.”

“Once enough people hear that Bard’s Gate has food, more will come.”

“Once enough people hear that Oxlight did not turn them away, more will come.”

“Once enough people hear that royal coin spends and Vogel coin doesn’t, they are going to run from every village where the duke’s men are not still holding the road.”

A beat.

“So I need to know who has the solution.”

“Do I send them to temples?”

“To Crown officers?”

“To the Lord Marshal?”

“To Oxlight?”

“To Castle Stormarrow?”

“To the nearest reeve with a spine?”

“To you?”

Another pause.

Her frustration sharpens.

“Because if nobody owns this problem, it will own the roads by nightfall.”

Rashgar mutters, softly but audibly:

“She paced while saying that. Deeply irritating. Effective, though.”

Then he continues.

“Rumors out of the free town of Blackberry are worse.”

“Bloodshed.”

“That is the word coming in.”

“Not a tavern scuffle. Not a few hungry men stealing bread.”

“Bloodshed.”

“I do not have clean counts yet. I do not know who struck first. I do not know if it was refugees, merchants, old Vogel loyalists, road men, or some enterprising bastard deciding a panic makes fine cover.”

“But Blackberry is already being named in the refugee lines.”

“They are saying people tried to pass Vogel coin there and were beaten.”

“They are saying a wagon was overturned.”

“They are saying children were separated.”

“They are saying three different things about a priest, which means either there was no priest or the priest is dead.”

A pause.

“I hate rumor. It breeds faster than rats and eats better.”

Another breath.

“So here is what I am doing.”

“I have people pulling children out of any bargain that smells wrong.”

“I have halflings feeding families before anyone asks what coin they carry.”

“I have watchers at the gates listening for Blackberry.”

“I have told the moneychangers that if they laugh at refugees, they can laugh without teeth.”

“I have asked the temples for visible hands and clean scales.”

“I am not waiting for permission to prevent a child market.”

Another pause.

“But I need someone above me to decide what this becomes.”

“Relief?”

“Exchange?”

“Royal order?”

“Military matter?”

“Criminal matter?”

“All of the above, probably, because apparently one kind of disaster was not festive enough.”

A faint hitch.

Not weakness.

Fury trying to become useful.

“Beringer, if the Crown redeems the coin, people will come.”

“If the Crown refuses, people will still come.”

“If nobody sets a rate, every bastard with a scale becomes a little duke.”

“If nobody guards the roads, the roads become hunting grounds.”

“If nobody gives the refugees a lawful way to live, some will steal, some will sell, some will die, and then every noble with clean gloves will ask how it happened.”

A pause.

“Do not let them ask that.”

Another pause.

Then, quieter:

“Tell me who to move through.”

“Tell me who is safe.”

“Tell me whose seal I can use without finding out tomorrow they have been arrested, married, assassinated, or made king by accident.”

A beat.

“And if the answer is that there is no answer yet, then say that.”

“I can work with honest chaos.”

“I cannot work with silence pretending to be order.”

The message ends there.

For a moment, Rashgar does not close the channel.

When he speaks again, the arrogance has returned.

Mostly.

“That was Wonderful.”

A pause.

“Delivered directly, because none of you have time for ceremonial throat-clearing.”

Another pause.

Then, more quietly:

“The part about the children is true.”

A breath.

“I do not shock easily.”

He lets that sit.

Then the mask slides back into place with audible contempt for vulnerability as a concept.

“I am holding the channel long enough for a reply. If you insist on naming every witness and seal before speaking, I will endure it with heroic suffering.”

A pause.

“Briefly.”
