## Message Re: Oxlight — Maester Mara Moffet Receiving

The signal reaches Orin after a delay.

Not a long one.

Long enough to make clear the message passed through hands before reaching him.

The connection opens with the faint pressure of a maintained spell-link, the slight metallic taste of distance, and then Mara Moffet’s voice.

Controlled.

Composed.

Trying very hard to stay that way.

“My lord Orin.”

A pause.

“This message originated at Castle Stormarrow from Baroness Braga Kharne, Warden of the West. It was transmitted by Magus Elowen Caerwyn, Crown Mage of Signals, assigned to your household by royal order.”

Another pause.

Mara exhales softly through her nose.

“Magus Caerwyn was very particular about the custody chain.”

A beat.

“Admirably particular.”

There is just enough frost on admirably to preserve the body until spring.

“She requested that I relay the message without embellishment.”

Another pause.

“I will honor the meaningful portions of that request.”

That is absolutely not the same thing.

Her voice shifts slightly as she begins the message proper.

“From Braga.”

A breath.

“She began: Orin.”

A faint pause.

“Then apparently corrected herself to: my lord husband.”

Mara lets that hang for half a heartbeat.

“I am told the correction did not survive long.”

Then she continues, carefully repeating Braga’s words.

“I miss you. I am starting there because if I do not, I will pretend this message is only business, and then you will know I am lying.”

A smaller pause.

“She added that you are not to look smug when hearing that.”

Mara’s tone stays dry.

“I assume you will disappoint her in this, but that is between husband and wife.”

Then the warmth leaves.

“The business is bad.”

“Oxlight has already seen the first break.”

“A refugee column out of Vogel lands reached the town this morning. Families. Old folk. Wounded men. Children. Several carts. Enough to clog the east road and draw every merchant eye in town.”

“They came with purses full of Vogel lead pennies and brass manticores.”

“They believed they had money.”

A pause.

“They did not.”

Mara stops there.

Not because she has lost the message.

Because she was there.

When she resumes, her voice is still controlled, but the effort shows.

“They tried to buy bread, blankets, stable space, lamp oil, and medicine.”

“The market laughed.”

Another pause.

“That was the spark.”

“First shouting. Then shoving. A baker’s stall went over. Three men set upon a moneychanger. After that, others joined in because apparently misery only needs permission to become arithmetic.”

A breath.

“No dead. Several hurt. The watch and my people contained it.”

Her voice tightens.

“Contained. Not solved.”

A moment passes.

Then Mara adds, quieter:

“She is right to be worried.”

A beat.

Then back to Braga’s message.

“These people were not poor when they left home. They became poor the moment someone told the truth about their money.”

“Their wages were a leash.”

“Their savings were a wall.”

“Their duke paid them in coin that only worked while his men stood close enough to punish refusal.”

“That is not poverty.”

“That is captivity with arithmetic.”

Mara goes silent after that.

A longer silence this time.

When she speaks again, it is her own voice.

“She said it better than I would have.”

A pause.

“And I dislike that.”

Then she continues the message.

“Oxlight needs food stores released under guard.”

“It needs a posted exchange rate before rumor sets the rate for us.”

“It needs clergy visible beside the scales, because if this is handled by merchants alone, someone will end up dead.”

“She recommends immediate relief exchange for refugees, witnessed by myself, town officers, and priests. Even a poor rate is better than mockery.”

“She also recommends warning every border market and road-town.”

“This will happen again.”

“Every refugee purse from Vogel land is a little riot waiting to be laughed at.”

A pause.

Then Mara’s voice softens despite herself.

“Her next words were for you.”

She resumes.

“Orin, this is not only a coin problem.”

“This is the sound of Vogel’s cage opening.”

“And the people inside are only just now realizing the bars were stamped like money.”

“Stand ready.”

“Keep your people warned.”

“Do not let anyone mistake this for common disorder.”

A pause.

“And come home alive.”

The link hums faintly.

Mara lets the silence sit long enough for the words to land.

Then:

“That was the end of Baroness Braga’s message.”

A breath.

“Magus Caerwyn also requested confirmation that her transmission had been relayed with urgency and without embellishment.”

A pause.

“I shall confirm urgency.”

Another pause, razor-thin.

“As for embellishment, I will report that the message reached you intact. That should satisfy all but the most spiritually upholstered of bureaucratic consciences.”

There is a faint sound, perhaps paper being set down.

Then Mara’s voice changes again.

No court polish now.

Just controlled alarm.

“Orin, I saw their faces.”

“They were not beggars. They were not vagrants. They came in ashamed to ask for help, but not broken. Some had decent cloaks. Tools. Wedding rings. A few still had little household ledgers wrapped in oilcloth.”

“They thought they had survived with something.”

A pause.

“Then a baker laughed at a woman trying to buy bread for her children.”

The silence that follows is colder than anger.

“That is when I understood.”

“Vogel did not merely tax them.”

“He taught them to trust a lie and then made the lie spendable only in his shadow.”

A breath.

“I have ordered temporary distribution from my own stores. Quietly. If I do it too loudly, half the town will call it charity and the other half will call it weakness. Both halves will be wrong, naturally, but wrong people still form crowds.”

Another pause.

“I am making arrangements now.”

“I have sent for the reeve, the senior grain factors, and the temple clerks.”

“There will be a posted emergency exchange by sundown, or there will be an explanation given in public by every man who thinks laughter is a monetary policy.”

“I have ordered the watch doubled at the market and the east road.”

“I have put our household men near the granaries, not because I expect theft, but because I expect fear, which is theft with better excuses.”

A colder pause.

“The moneychangers will be made useful. If they wish to keep their tables, they will weigh coin under witness and speak their rates aloud.”

“I am also sending notice to the nearest border towns. Not asking. Warning.”

Her voice steadies.

“I will hold Oxlight.”

A beat.

“But this is not an Oxlight problem.”

“This is coming down every road out of Grevandar.”

The link remains open a moment longer.

Then Mara adds, softer:

“She misses you.”

A faint pause.

“That part was plain.”

Then the formal tone returns, because Mara Moffet would rather die standing than end on naked sentiment.

“Awaiting instruction, my lord.”
