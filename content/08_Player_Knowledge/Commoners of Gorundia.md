## Common Folk of Gorundia Looking at the Other Peoples

## Common Folk Baseline

The common folk of Gorundia do not think like scholars, nobles, priests, or wandering adventurers.

Most of them judge peoples by simple questions:

- Will they pay fair?
- Will they rob us?
- Will they get us conscripted?
- Will they bring soldiers?
- Will they bring priests?
- Will they keep the road safe?
- Will they burn the fields?
- Will they eat what we serve without complaint?
- Will their trouble become our trouble?

Most common folk have never traveled more than a few days from home. Their opinions are local, practical, inherited, and often wildly unfair.

A peasant in Vogel lands, a mason in Blackvale, a fisherman near Wyrmlea, and a free ranch-hand out near Orenic or Amadis country may all be Gorundian commoners — but they do not see the world the same way.

The biggest divide is between:

- **Heartland folk** — especially in Vogel lands, where oppression is often understood as safety.
- **Frontier folk** — especially Orenic, Amadis, and western freeholder types, where order is respected but independence is sacred.

Their shared view of the world is:

> “Great folk make decisions. Small folk survive them.”

---

## Heartland Folk

Heartland folk live under stronger lords, older tax rolls, tighter watch, and more direct noble authority.

In Vogel lands especially, harsh rule is not always loved, but it is often understood. The roads are patrolled. Bandits vanish. Taxes hurt, but armies are fed. Thieves are flogged. Fields are measured. Granaries are counted. Doors are watched.

To outsiders this looks like oppression.

To many heartland commoners, it feels like the price of not being burned out, raided, or left to the wolves.

Their worldview is:

> “A hard hand is better than no hand when the dark comes.”

This does not mean they are happy.

It means they are used to choosing between bad and worse.

---

## Frontier Folk

Frontier folk live closer to danger and farther from court.

Orenic and Amadis lands, border farms, free ranches, fortified villages, and western holdings produce a different kind of commoner. These people still respect lords, oaths, and law, but they are much less impressed by distant authority.

They trust a baron who rides out.
They trust a bailiff who knows where the creek floods.
They trust a neighbor with a bow more than a court decree three counties away.

Harlan Vayne-style freeholders especially believe land is earned by holding it, improving it, and bleeding for it. They are not anti-noble by default, but they hate soft-handed interference.

Their worldview is:

> “Law’s good. Help’s better. A man who won’t mend fence has no business drawing borders.”

### Nobles

**Common private take:**  
Nobles are necessary, dangerous, expensive, and not always as smart as their horses.

Common folk respect good nobles. A good lord protects roads, settles disputes, stores grain, keeps soldiers disciplined, and does not take more than people can survive losing.

A bad lord is worse than a bandit because he brings paperwork.

**Heartland take:**  
A harsh noble may still be accepted if order holds.

**Heartland saying:**  
“A cruel lord is a curse. A weak lord is an invitation.”

**Frontier take:**  
A noble who does not show up when trouble comes is barely a noble at all.

**Frontier saying:**  
“His banner don’t mend my roof. His men might.”

**Common polite version:**  
“His lordship keeps the peace.”

**What they mean:**  
“May the gods keep him interested in doing so.”

---

### Humans

**Common private take:**  
Humans are just people. Which is to say: good, bad, greedy, kind, stupid, brave, and usually tired.

Most commoners do not think of “humans” as a category unless they are comparing humans to elves, dwarves, or halfbloods. Human ways are simply normal ways in most Gorundian villages.

**Heartland take:**  
Humans belong to their house, village, trade, parish, and lord before they belong to any grand idea of “humanity.”

**Frontier take:**  
Humans are whoever shows up to work, fight, trade, or steal.

**Common saying:**  
“Men are men. Some bring bread. Some bring trouble.”

---

### High Elves

**Common private take:**  
High elves are beautiful, old, rich in manners, and impossible to understand without a priest, a tutor, or three cups of wine.

Most common folk rarely meet true high elves. They know them from songs, noble courts, wandering envoys, old ruins, and half-remembered diaspora families.

They are admired, but not trusted in a simple way. Elves speak softly and somehow make simple things complicated.

**Heartland take:**  
High elves are above common life. Pretty, powerful, and probably trouble if they notice you.

**Heartland saying:**  
“If an elf asks your name twice, give him your brother’s.”

**Frontier take:**  
An elf who can shoot, heal, scout, or bargain fairly is welcome. An elf who only sighs at the trees can do it somewhere else.

**Frontier saying:**  
“Pointy ears don’t stack hay.”

**Common polite version:**  
“Elves are a graceful people.”

**What they mean:**  
“They are lovely, but I do not want one deciding whether my fence offends history.”

---

### Wood Elves

**Common private take:**  
Wood elves are the decent sort of elves, provided you are not cutting the wrong tree.

Common folk understand wood elves better than high elves. Wood elves hunt, gather, guide, heal, scout, trade herbs, and know weather. That makes sense.

They are still strange. They care deeply about groves, springs, old stones, animal paths, and things a farmer may not realize are sacred until the elf has gone very still.

**Heartland take:**  
Useful, but unsettling. Best treated respectfully and not invited into legal disputes.

**Heartland saying:**  
“A wood elf smiles at your orchard and frowns at your axe. Mind both.”

**Frontier take:**  
Good allies if you keep your word and do not foul the water.

**Frontier saying:**  
“Forest folk know where the deer went. That’s worth manners.”

**Common polite version:**  
“They know the old ways of the wild.”

**What they mean:**  
“They are helpful until you accidentally step on a holy fern.”

---

### Grugach / Wild Elves

**Common private take:**  
Wild elves are what mothers warn children about when the woods get too quiet.

Most common folk do not distinguish cleanly between wood elves and grugach unless they live near them. To heartland villagers, “wild elf” may mean anything from “forest scout” to “painted killer in the trees.”

Frontier folk have sharper opinions. They may fear grugach, but they also understand territorial logic: stay out, keep your bargain, do not trespass, do not boast.

**Heartland take:**  
Dangerous forest ghosts with bows.

**Heartland saying:**  
“If the woods go silent, turn around.”

**Frontier take:**  
Hard folk. Not friendly, not false.

**Frontier saying:**  
“A wild elf gives one warning. That’s more than a wolf gives.”

**Common polite version:**  
“They are very protective of their lands.”

**What they mean:**  
“They may shoot you before explaining the boundary.”

---

### Dwarves

**Common private take:**  
Dwarves are hard workers, hard bargainers, hard drinkers, and hard to move once they have decided anything.

Common folk respect dwarves. Dwarven tools, stonework, locks, bridges, weapons, and contracts are trusted. A village with dwarven-built walls sleeps better.

They are also thought expensive, proud, and easy to offend if you use the wrong hammer on the wrong nail.

**Heartland take:**  
Dwarves are good for mines, walls, money, and serious work. Let the reeve or guildmaster argue price.

**Heartland saying:**  
“A dwarf-built wall costs dear once. A cheap wall costs every winter.”

**Frontier take:**  
A dwarf neighbor is a blessing if you can survive the arguing.

**Frontier saying:**  
“If a dwarf says the bridge will hold, cross. If he says it won’t, swim upstream.”

**Common polite version:**  
“Dwarves know their craft.”

**What they mean:**  
“They will tell you that before breakfast, during breakfast, and again while fixing your roof.”

---

### Halflings

**Common private take:**  
Halflings are good neighbors until they are not, and by the time they are not, they already know where you keep the key.

Common folk usually like halflings. They are cooks, innkeepers, farmers, carters, shopkeepers, herbalists, midwives, brewers, and gossip engines. They make communities feel safer and warmer.

But everyone knows some halflings are thieves, smugglers, card-sharks, and little smiling devils with ledgers.

**Heartland take:**  
Good household folk, good food, good trade — keep one eye on your purse near the lively ones.

**Heartland saying:**  
“A halfling will feed you, fleece you, or marry your cousin. Sometimes in that order.”

**Frontier take:**  
Small folk who pull their weight are just folk. A halfling who can cook, shoot, hide, or keep books is worth having.

**Frontier saying:**  
“Small hands still mend harness.”

**Common polite version:**  
“Halflings are cheerful folk.”

**What they mean:**  
“They know more than they should.”

---

### Gnomes

**Common private take:**  
Gnomes are clever little folk who can fix a clock, curse a lock, and explain both until you wish you were dead.

Commoners like useful gnomes: tinkers, scribes, menders, tutors, alchemists, mapmakers, and minor wizards. But gnomes feel risky. Their inventions whistle. Their questions pry. Their explanations breed more explanations.

**Heartland take:**  
Useful under supervision.

**Heartland saying:**  
“If a gnome says it is safe, ask safe for who.”

**Frontier take:**  
A gnome who can repair gear, make powder, read maps, or spot a scam is worth the noise.

**Frontier saying:**  
“A gnome talks too much until the wheel comes off. Then he talks just enough.”

**Common polite version:**  
“Gnomes are very learned.”

**What they mean:**  
“Do not let him near the mill unless the miller is sober.”

---

### Halfbloods

**Common private take:**  
Halfbloods are people you judge twice: once by fear, once by what they actually do.

This varies hard by place.

Some commoners fear half-orcs and other halfbloods because of war stories, noble prejudice, church warnings, and old raids. Others live beside them, work with them, drink with them, or owe them their lives.

Common folk are often less ideological than nobles. A half-orc who guards the road, pulls a cart from mud, pays fair, and does not threaten children may become “our half-orc” very quickly.

That does not erase prejudice. It just makes it personal and inconsistent.

**Heartland take:**  
Useful if vouched for. Dangerous if unknown. Best if attached to a lord, reeve, temple, or company.

**Heartland saying:**  
“A tusked man with a badge is law. A tusked stranger is weather.”

**Frontier take:**  
Can you work? Can you fight? Can you keep your word? Good. Stand there.

**Frontier saying:**  
“Blood don’t mend fence. Hands do.”

**Common polite version:**  
“He seems a decent sort.”

**What they mean:**  
“We are still watching, but less loudly.”

---

### Orcs

**Common private take:**  
Orcs are not another kind of neighbor. Orcs are ruin.

Full-blooded orcs are remembered in Gorundian common speech as destroyers: raiders, burners, defilers of fields, slaughterers of livestock, spoilers of wells, and servants of old dark powers. They do not come to bargain. They come to take, kill, burn, or break.

And if they flee, they do not merely run. They spoil what they cannot carry. They burn barns, hamstring animals, poison wells, break millstones, foul grain, cut fruit trees, and leave traps behind for whoever comes after them.

To common folk, orcs are the reason walls exist. They are the reason old women keep children indoors when horns sound. They are the reason a village bell can make every adult go silent at once.

**Heartland take:**  
Orcs are what hard lords, high walls, watchtowers, and taxes are for.

Heartland folk may resent Vogel harshness, but when old stories of orc raids are told, the lesson is simple: better a cruel lord with soldiers than a soft lord with ashes.

**Heartland saying:**  
“Pay the tax, bless the wall, and pray the horns stay far.”

**Frontier take:**  
Orcs are the black edge of the map. If they come, you fight. If they flee, you chase carefully, because they leave death behind them.

Frontier folk do not talk about “understanding” orcs. They talk about sentries, dogs, firebreaks, hidden grain, spare wells, and whether the children can reach the root cellar before the riders crest the hill.

**Frontier saying:**  
“An orc running is still burning something.”

**Common polite version:**  
“Orcs are not folk we treat with.”

**What they mean:**  
“Bar the door, ring the bell, and bring every bow.”

---

### Clergy / Holy Orders

**Common private take:**  
Priests are needed. Paladins are admired. Both can become frightening when they stop smiling.

Common folk rely on clergy for healing, birth, death, marriage, oaths, truth, festivals, charity, and protection from things no spear can settle.

Pelorites are usually beloved.
Heironeans are respected.
Pholtans are respected and dreaded.

**Heartland take:**  
The Church is part of order. A priest can be kinder than a lord, but also harder to argue with.

**Heartland saying:**  
“A reeve takes your coin. A priest weighs your soul. Mind which debt you neglect.”

**Frontier take:**  
A good priest rides out. A bad priest preaches from behind walls.

**Frontier saying:**  
“Blessings are fine. Bring bandages.”

**Common polite version:**  
“The holy folk keep us right.”

**What they mean:**  
“And gods help us when they decide someone else is wrong.”

---

### Wizards and Scholars

**Common private take:**  
Wizards are useful, expensive, and probably responsible for whatever that smell is.

Most commoners do not understand magic well. They respect healing, wards, weather-work, useful charms, and protective magic. They fear curses, illusions, mind tricks, fire, and anyone who smiles while explaining that the explosion was “expected.”

Scholars are less feared, unless they are tax scholars, legal scholars, or genealogists. Those are basically plague rats with ink.

**Heartland take:**  
Wizards belong in towers, courts, temples, or under noble supervision.

**Heartland saying:**  
“A wizard without a patron is a storm without a roof.”

**Frontier take:**  
If the wizard can help, welcome. If the wizard starts experimenting near the well, run him off.

**Frontier saying:**  
“Magic’s like fire. Good in the hearth. Bad in the hayloft.”

**Common polite version:**  
“Learned folk know many strange things.”

**What they mean:**  
“Please do not demonstrate them indoors.”

## Common Folk About Their Own Rotten Elements

Common folk know they have their own villains.

They know about poachers, wife-beaters, oath-breakers, grain cheats, smugglers, informants, cowardly watchmen, false pilgrims, hedge-witches gone bad, corrupt millers, violent sons, cruel mothers, and neighbors who smile too much during hard times.

They do not romanticize poverty as virtue. Poor folk can be bastards too. They just usually do not have heraldry.

**Common public version:**  
“Every village has trouble.”

**Common private version:**  
“Jory beats his mule, cheats his sister, and would sell his mother’s roof-beam if the price was right.”

**Heartland response:**  
Report him to the reeve, the priest, or the lord’s man — unless he is protected.

**Frontier response:**  
Warn him once, shame him twice, then let his cousins handle it behind the barn.

## The Common Folk Politeness Weapon

Common folk do not usually speak like court. Their politeness is practical, guarded, and full of weather.

### Translation Table

**“He’s from good people.”**  
His family is better than he is.

**“She’s had a hard year.”**  
She is behaving badly, but we know why.

**“He means no harm.”**  
He causes harm accidentally and often.

**“He’s not from around here.”**  
Watch him.

**“They keep to themselves.”**  
We do not know what they are doing and do not like it.

**“That’s lord’s business.”**  
Do not ask where soldiers can hear.

**“That’s priest’s business.”**  
Something is morally ugly or magically strange.

**“That’s old trouble.”**  
People died before you were born. Drop it.

**“Road’s been quiet.”**  
Something changed and everyone noticed.

**“Winter will tell.”**  
We will see who was lying when supplies run thin.

**“Can’t eat promises.”**  
Your plan is useless unless food follows.

**“He works hard.”**  
He is acceptable despite other flaws.

**“She pays fair.”**  
She is welcome.

**“I wouldn’t go that way.”**  
You may die, and I am done warning you.

## Sample Common Folk Lines About Everyone

> “Nobles keep the peace. Mostly from problems nobles started.”

> “A good lord is worth taxes. A bad lord is just a bandit with witnesses.”

> “Elves are fine folk, I’m told. They do talk like every sentence has a grandmother.”

> “Wood elves know the forest. Best not argue trees with them.”

> “Wild elves? If you see one, he wanted you to. Mind that.”

> “Dwarves build dear and build right. Shame they know both.”

> “Halflings know food, gossip, and doors. That covers most of life.”

> “Gnomes are clever. That’s praise and warning both.”

> “Half-orc? Depends. Is he working, drinking, guarding, or looking for someone?”

> “Orcs are why we pay for walls.”

> “An orc running is still trouble. Smoke follows them like a dog.”

> “Priests are good to have nearby. Paladins too, unless they came looking for someone.”

> “A wizard can save your harvest or curse your cow. Best be polite either way.”

## The Core Common Horror

Common folk are quietly horrified because everyone above them, outside them, or stranger than them can turn their lives upside down.

Nobles feud.  
Priests judge.  
Wizards experiment.  
Armies march.  
Elves remember.  
Dwarves bargain.  
Halflings listen.  
Gnomes meddle.  
Halfbloods draw attention.  
Orcs burn.  
Taxmen count.  
Winter comes anyway.

Heartland folk believe safety comes from order, even harsh order.

Frontier folk believe safety comes from neighbors, arms, fences, and lords who show up when called.

The common folk worldview is basically:

> “Fine words are wind. Good roads, full barns, fair weights, quiet nights, and someone answering the bell — that’s civilization.”
