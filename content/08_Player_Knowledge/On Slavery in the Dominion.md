# On Slavery in the Dominion

> [!abstract]
> A short account by **Petar Velyan**, lecturer in comparative law at the Royal College. Velyan was born to a common household in the Dominion, worked as a junior copyist in a provincial grain office, and left the country as a young man. He was never enslaved and has repeatedly objected to introductions implying otherwise.

## By Petar Velyan

I must begin with a limit.

I was never a slave.

I have worn a wooden tally around my neck while moving state grain. I have been searched at a city gate, questioned by a commissar, struck by an officer, and threatened with labor judgment over a debt that was not mine. None of these things made me a slave. Proximity to a chain does not confer the authority of having worn it.

I write only of what a common free man could see.

In the Dominion, that was quite enough.

Slavery is not hidden at the edge of respectable life. It carries laundry through noble courtyards, unloads barges, digs roads, tends furnaces, works estates, serves temples, and marches in front of soldiers. A citizen may pass slaves every day while claiming never to have visited a market.

This claim is much like saying one has never seen rain because one avoided looking upward.

Foreigners often imagine a single slave law and a single class of slave. The Dominion prefers categories.

There are captives taken in war, children born into household ownership, debtors transferred by judgment, criminals sentenced to labor, political offenders whose families become liable with them, temple dependents, penal workers, and the Chainbound driven into military service. The names matter greatly to clerks and masters. They matter less to the person who cannot leave.

That variety serves a purpose. A man who condemns the sale of captives may still approve penal labor. A priest uncomfortable with hereditary slavery may accept temple correction. A merchant who would never buy a child may acquire an entire indebted household through lawful judgment. Each can declare himself innocent because his chain bears a different entry in the ledger.

The Dominion’s defenders will say that masters are regulated.

This is true.

They will say that slaves may submit an **Iron Petition** against unlawful treatment.

This is also true.

A petitioner may be taken into **Protective Seizure** while an accusation is examined. A master who destroys valuable state property without permission, conceals taxable births, falsifies labor classifications, withholds a slave required for military service, or kills someone protected by another office may indeed be punished.

Occasionally a cruel master is ruined.

Occasionally a slave is transferred to gentler hands.

Very occasionally, freedom is granted.

These events are displayed as proof that the system contains justice. They prove only that the Dominion dislikes unauthorized cruelty, wasted property, and masters who forget that all private authority remains beneath the state.

An Iron Petition asks whether the owner broke the rules of ownership. It does not ordinarily ask whether ownership is itself the wrong.

This distinction is the foundation upon which the whole respectable defense rests.

As a copyist, I once prepared extracts from a seizure roll concerning a mill owner who had beaten two bonded workers after they became too injured to complete their quotas. He was fined, stripped of his contract, and denounced from the temple steps.

The workers were not freed.

They were transferred to the provincial road office because the judgment found the miller unfit to retain them.

My superior called this mercy.

I copied the word as instructed.

The greatest power of Dominion slavery is not the whip, though there are many whips. It is the knowledge among free commoners that slavery is not a distant station occupied only by conquered strangers.

It can be entered beside one’s name.

A failed debt, a false account, the disgrace of a patron, an accusation of disloyalty, a relative’s crime, or a priest’s finding of incorrigible disorder may narrow a life very quickly. Not every debtor becomes a slave. Not every condemned household is seized. The uncertainty is useful. A commoner who knows precisely where the boundary stands may approach it. A commoner who knows the boundary can be moved becomes careful everywhere.

This is why free laborers sometimes defend the institution. They are frightened of being mistaken for those below them. A dockman may spit upon a chain gang not because he is secure, but because he wishes the watching clerk to record the difference between them.

It is also why some slaves appear better fed than free families. A skilled smith, household tutor, arena fighter, or trained body servant may represent substantial value. Masters point to such people and ask whether hunger in freedom is truly preferable to shelter in bondage.

Only a person certain of his own freedom could mistake the question for wisdom.

The slave’s food may be generous. The slave’s bed may be warm. The slave remains subject to sale, transfer, punishment, breeding, separation, conscription, and the ambitions of someone whose kindness is protected by force.

I knew masters who were personally gentle. I knew slaves who mourned when such masters died.

Neither fact alters the law.

The gentlest master still possesses the key.

The Iron Synod teaches that hierarchy is sacred, that strength proves fitness to command, and that obedience preserves the whole. The civil ministries turn this doctrine into classifications. The noble houses make it hereditary. The army consumes the bodies that remain. Each institution may blame another for excess, but none can remove its hand without admitting how much of the Dominion rests upon bound labor.

I left through a western customs post with a scholar’s letter, two shirts, and permission to study abroad. I did not return when ordered.

I was fortunate enough to emigrate by becoming a criminal only after I had crossed the border.

Those who escape without letters are called thieves of state property, deserters, oathbreakers, fugitives, or stolen dependents. The Dominion has many precise names for a person running toward freedom.

I know only one.

**Free.**

— **Petar Velyan**  
Lecturer in Comparative Law
