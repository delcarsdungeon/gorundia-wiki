# Baron Stanislav Vorokov

> “Vorokov rides like the gods gave him one spine and his horse another. Sensible men find that inspiring. Smarter men find cover.”
> — Sorek Redmarch

## Overview

**Baron Stanislav Vorokov** is the lord of [[Barony Vorochev]], a Royonad holding sworn through [[County Mallov]] to [[House Orenic]]. He is one of Duke Vladek Orenic’s most visible cavalry nobles and the living face of the **Vorokov Hussars**, Royonad’s prestige fast-response cavalry.

Stanislav is not merely a noble who commands horsemen. His barony, household, reputation, and military value are all fused around mounted war. Vorochev breeds the horses. Vorokov funds the officers. The hussars carry the family’s prestige. Stanislav wears the scars.

He has joined [[Sir Beringer Amadis]] with a body of his hussars, making his presence a major sign of Orenic favor. This is not a token contribution. House Orenic has sent one of its sharpest mobile forces and the baron most personally tied to its reputation.

That kind of gift has weight.

## Appearance

![[Baron Stanislav Vorokov.png|340]]

Stanislav is a striking, frightening man even before he speaks.

He wears his long white-blond hair loose over his shoulders, with several tight warrior-braids worked through it in the Royonad cavalry fashion. The style is too practical to be courtly and too deliberate to be casual, which is very much the point. His mustache is long and narrow, weighted with small metal beads that click faintly when he turns his head.

His eyes are pale blue-gray, sharp and cold, with the fixed stare of a man who has watched horses, men, and weather all betray the careless.

His face is marked by lance scars. Not old heroic scars softened by time, but hard red seams and pale healed cuts across the brow, cheek, and jaw. The hussars say he earned them in high-speed lance duels, the sort of noble cavalry ritual that proves courage, balance, and a worrying indifference to facial symmetry.

He favors excellent scale or lamellar cavalry armor, fitted close for movement in the saddle, usually worn beneath a deep red cloak lined with fur. At his breast or cloak clasp he often wears a roundel version of the House Vorokov arms: the white warhorse of Vorochev beneath the golden lance.

He looks less like a court baron and more like the man court barons send when the map has caught fire.

## Personality

Stanislav is brave, proud, disciplined, and openly contemptuous of hesitation once a decision is made. He is not stupidly reckless, which makes him more dangerous. Stupidly reckless men are easy to predict. Stanislav understands scouting, withdrawal, pursuit discipline, feigned retreat, and timing.

He simply believes courage is a tool that should be sharpened often.

He is formal with rank, warm with proven soldiers, and brutally dismissive toward anyone who treats cavalry as ornamental. He has little patience for parade knights who think mounted warfare begins and ends with a single glorious charge. He likes charges. He likes them very much. But he prefers charges that happen after the enemy is tired, blind, stretched, thirsty, and already regretting every prior decision.

Around his own men, he laughs easily, but the humor has teeth. Among the Vorokov Hussars, mockery is affection, and praise is usually disguised as insult to prevent fatal swelling of the skull.

## Voice

Stanislav speaks in clipped, direct Common with Royonad phrasing and occasional Suel turns of speech. He is less volcanic than [[Duke Vladek Orenic]], but the cultural resemblance is obvious: direct, proud, and allergic to polished cowardice.

Common phrases:

- “Speed is armor, if you know how to wear it.”
- “A horse that cannot turn is only a cart with delusions.”
- “We do not chase qazaq. We make them explain themselves.”
- “If you want a clean face, join the clerks.”
- “A charge is not bravery. A charge at the correct moment is command.”
- “My men are expensive because dead fools are cheap.”
- “When the pennon drops, we are one hand. Until then, gods help the tavern.”

## Family

### Baroness Mila Vorokov

**Baroness Mila Vorokov** is Stanislav’s wife and the lady of Vorochev Hall. She is not merely a household ornament or a passive noble spouse. In a barony built around elite cavalry, remount contracts, officer families, and military supply, Mila’s authority matters.

She manages much of the barony’s domestic and logistical stability while Stanislav is in the field. Her responsibilities include household accounts, remount obligations, veteran pensions, marriages among officer families, and the steady political correspondence required to keep Vorochev useful rather than merely famous.

Where Stanislav embodies the charge, Mila embodies the system that lets the charge happen.

She is likely less theatrical than her husband, but no less formidable. Servants, grooms, armorers, and young officers know that disappointing the baron may get them shouted at. Disappointing the baroness may get them corrected so thoroughly their grandchildren inherit the lesson.

### Ser Lev Vorokov

**Ser Lev Vorokov** is Stanislav’s heir.

Lev has inherited the expectations of a cavalry house where childhood riding injuries are treated less as tragedy and more as progress reports. He is being raised to command, ride, shoot, judge horses, read terrain, and understand that a noble officer’s courage is meaningless if it gets good men killed for vanity.

That does not mean he has learned the lesson yet.

Lev is talented, proud, and watched closely by the hussars. To them, he is both future lord and unfinished weapon. If he enters play, he should carry the pressure of being the heir to a house whose traditions are glorious, lethal, and not especially interested in letting young men grow slowly.

## Heraldry

**Arms of House Vorokov:**  
*Gules, a horse courant argent barded for war; on a chief argent, a lance Or fesswise.*

![[baron vorokov.png|340]]

In plain speech: a red shield bearing a white warhorse in motion, armored for cavalry service, beneath a white chief charged with a gold lance.

The arms are old Royonad cavalry heraldry: simple, martial, and about as subtle as a hoof to the teeth. The white horse represents the Vorokov remount lines and the family’s reputation for speed, endurance, and disciplined violence. The gold lance above it marks noble cavalry service, officer honor, and the house tradition of lance trials.

The red field is usually read as courage and blood-price, though Vorokov men tend to say this with suspicious cheer, which makes everyone nearby slightly less comfortable.

## Barony Vorochev

[[Barony Vorochev]] is a small but important Royonad barony under [[County Mallov]]. It is open, hard-ridden country: rolling grass, wind-cut ridges, fortified villages, horse pastures, road shrines, and military tracks worn deep by generations of patrols.

Vorochev is not rich in the soft, courtly sense. It is rich in horses, riders, saddles, scouts, remount contracts, and military usefulness. That makes it valuable in Royonad, where distance and speed decide whether a border alarm becomes a skirmish, a raid, or a massacre.

The barony is famous for horses descended from mixed Dominion and Gorundian stock. They are not as massive as Vogel chargers, nor as elegant as Blackbriar destriers, but they are fast, agile, durable, and mean enough to survive Royonad training.

Vorochev Hall is less a manor than a cavalry fortress with a noble residence attached under protest. Stables, exercise yards, messenger gates, farrier sheds, and tack rooms are everywhere. Even the chapel yard is laid out so mounted couriers can pass through without dismounting.

The locals consider this normal, because people can normalize anything if it happens near them long enough.

### Economic Base

Vorochev’s wealth comes from:

- horse breeding
- cavalry remount contracts
- leatherwork and saddlecraft
- bowmaking and composite bow maintenance
- border tolls and escort fees
- military stipends from House Orenic

Its peasants and townsfolk are accustomed to soldiers moving fast and often. Many families have at least one member tied to the hussars as groom, bowyer, courier, remount handler, scout, farrier, or veteran pensioner.

### Culture

Vorochev culture prizes horsemanship, nerve, and memory. The Dominion is not an abstraction here. It is the old master, the old enemy, and the old wound.

Families keep stories of who fled, who fought, who bent the knee, who betrayed, and who rode through the night to warn the next village.

Children learn to ride early. Noble children learn earlier. A Vorokov child who falls from a pony is comforted, checked for broken bones, and put back in the saddle before anyone has time to develop poetry about it.

## The Vorokov Hussars

The **Vorokov Hussars** are Royonad’s prestige cavalry formation and among [[House Orenic]]’s most reliable fast-response troops. They are tied directly to Vorochev’s identity, economy, and honor.

They are one of those units every army produces when talent, money, death rates, and masculine stupidity are poured into the same barrel and left to ferment.

When they are **on duty**, they are terrifyingly disciplined. They ride in formation with precise spacing, keep silence on approach, obey hand signals instantly, and can shift from scouting screen to missile harassment to shock charge with almost no visible confusion. Their officers are expected to read terrain at speed, judge horseflesh by sound, and know when a pursuit has turned into bait.

When they are **off duty**, they are among the wildest men in the army.

They sing too loudly, drink too hard, gamble recklessly, race horses where horses absolutely should not be raced, and hold insult contests that somehow have rules older than some baronies. They are famous for smashing taverns, then paying the owner more than the damage was worth, as if destruction is merely another form of patronage.

This contradiction is not accidental.

The hussars are trained to live with pressure. On campaign, they are often the first to see the enemy, the first to be cut off, the first sent into uncertain ground, and the last ones back if the army needs eyes behind it. Their casualties are high, their horses irreplaceable, and their reputation heavy enough to crush the merely sensible.

So when the bit is out of their mouths, they run.

Stanislav permits more disorder from them than most commanders would tolerate, because he understands the bargain. A hussar may sing, boast, drink, flirt, gamble, and make a public idiot of himself.

He may not miss watch.

He may not mistreat his horse.

He may not abuse civilians.

He may not steal.

He may not draw steel in camp without cause.

He may not disgrace the pennon.

The first category gets laughter.

The second gets punishment.

The distinction is explained exactly once.

### Reputation

Among common soldiers, the hussars are admired, resented, imitated, and cursed in roughly equal measure. Infantrymen call them peacocks until the flank collapses, at which point everyone suddenly becomes very fond of peacocks.

Among young nobles, they have dangerous glamour. The hussar life promises speed, scars, songs, beautiful horses, red cloaks, elite company, and an early grave wrapped in applause. This is, naturally, irresistible to a certain kind of young lordling, because apparently humans see mortality and think it needs better tailoring.

The hussars know this and encourage it just enough to recruit the brave while humiliating the merely vain.

A man can buy red cloth.

He cannot buy a seat at full gallop.

### Badge of the Vorokov Hussars

The hussars use a simplified badge drawn from the family arms: **a gold lance over a white horse’s head on red**.

The full arms belong to House Vorokov. The badge belongs to the regiment. This distinction matters to heralds, officers, and other people desperately trying to make war more paperwork-shaped.

### Camp Customs

**The Quiet Tack Rule:** Before any mission, there is no singing, no jokes, no dice, no unnecessary speech. Tack is checked in silence. A horse making more noise than its rider is considered a bad omen.

**The Red Cup:** After a successful return from dangerous scouting or pursuit, the surviving riders drink from a shared red-lacquered cup. The first drink is poured out for those who did not return.

**The Empty Saddle Toast:** No hussar sits in a dead man’s place at table until his name has been spoken aloud. The toast is brief. The drinking afterward is not.

**The Spur Debt:** A hussar who is saved by another rider owes that rider a spur-token. These tokens are traded, worn, gambled, stolen back, and sometimes sent home to widows.

**The Three Forbidden Boasts:** A hussar does not boast he cannot be unhorsed, cannot be outrun, or cannot be killed. These are considered invitations to the gods, and the gods are known cheats.

**Pay Before Breaking:** When entering a friendly town, senior hussars sometimes pay innkeepers and taverners in advance. This is called “courtesy.” Everyone else calls it “evidence.”

### Discipline

Hussar discipline is sharp because it has to be. Loose cavalry dies. Loose elite cavalry dies expensively.

Their wildness off duty is tolerated only because their obedience under signal is absolute. When the pennon drops, the laughter stops. When the horn sounds, dice remain where they fall. When the commander gives the hand sign, a hundred bright, arrogant, half-drunk cavalrymen can become a single moving blade.

This is the part outsiders struggle to understand.

The hussars are not undisciplined.

They are disciplined enough to survive being wild.

## Doctrine

The hussars fight in layers.

First they see.

Then they sting.

Then they turn.

Then they sting again.

Then, when the enemy has opened a gap, lost formation, or committed too far, they charge.

Their officers train constantly in controlled aggression. The ideal Vorokov officer is not fearless. Fearless men die quickly and make horses nervous. The ideal is a man who feels fear, measures distance anyway, and can still put a lance through a target while traveling at an unreasonable speed.

### Common Tactics

**The Glass Spur:** A sudden acceleration from apparent withdrawal into a flank charge.

**The Wolf Circle:** Mounted archery harassment around slower infantry or supply trains.

**The Broken Rein:** Feigned command confusion meant to bait enemy cavalry into pursuit.

**The Red Pennon:** A signal that the hussars are shifting from harassment to lance charge.

**The Empty Saddle Drill:** Every hussar trains to recover, lead, or remount a riderless horse under pressure. The unit treats horses as battlefield assets, not decorations with legs.

**The Silent Mile:** The final approach before a surprise attack or scouting contact. No speech, no metal rattle, no unnecessary movement.

**The Laughing Return:** A deliberately noisy ride back into camp after a successful raid or screen action. It reassures allies, unnerves rivals, and advertises survival with all the subtlety of a thrown chair.

## Lance Duels

Among Vorokov hussar officers, formal lance-dueling is a tradition of prestige, lunacy, and social sorting. The duel is fought mounted, at speed, with blunted but still dangerous lances. Armor is worn. Death is discouraged but not unknown.

The duel tests:

- seat
- nerve
- timing
- lance control
- pain tolerance
- ability to stay mounted while regretting your ancestry

Stanislav’s facial scars come from these duels. He is not embarrassed by them. Quite the opposite. Among his men, a clean-faced officer over thirty is viewed with suspicion unless he has an excellent explanation, such as “archer,” “priest,” or “recently transferred from somewhere less interesting.”

## Relationship with House Orenic

Stanislav is loyal to [[Duke Vladek Orenic]], but not servile. He respects Vladek’s courage, directness, and hatred of Dominion interference. He also respects [[Duchess Irina Orenic]]’s political judgment, and privately regards her as the reason Royonad has not turned every insult into a cavalry raid.

His barony’s prestige is tied directly to ducal trust. When House Orenic needs a fast answer, Vorochev rides.

Sending Stanislav and the hussars to support [[Sir Beringer Amadis]] is a major sign of favor. It tells other nobles that Beringer has not merely acquired spare horsemen from a distant ally. He has been given a piece of Royonad’s pride.

Nobles can count troops.

They can also count symbols.

## Relationship with Beringer

Stanislav does not know Beringer intimately at first, but he respects what he sees: burden, command pressure, and the kind of restrained violence that suggests a man trying very hard not to become a disaster.

He will test Beringer professionally.

Not with insults. Not with open defiance.

With questions.

How far ahead does the screen ride?  
Who has authority to break pursuit?  
What happens if qazaq bait the left?  
Who decides when refugees slow the column?  
Is the Lord Marshal willing to spend elite horse to save common infantry?

The answers will matter.

If Beringer answers well, Stanislav becomes one of those very useful allies who still complains constantly, because loyalty and peace of mind are apparently separate departments.

## Relationship with Sorek Redmarch

[[Sorek Redmarch]] respects Stanislav and finds the hussars ridiculous in the way only a veteran infantry-minded knight can find elite cavalry ridiculous: with irritation, admiration, and a private wish he had a few more of them.

Stanislav thinks Sorek is exactly the sort of old soldier who has earned the right to be rude.

Their conversations are mostly dry insults with useful information hidden inside.

> “Your men ride beautifully.”
> — Sorek Redmarch  
>
> “Your tone suggests an insult is limping behind that compliment.”
> — Baron Stanislav Vorokov  
>
> “No limp. Infantry has to walk.”
> — Sorek Redmarch

## Relationship with Baron Jareth DeSalle

Stanislav regards [[Baron Jareth DeSalle]] with more respect than he would offer an untested inheritance lord.

Jareth is a proven knight. That matters. In Stanislav’s world, a title inherited from a dead father opens doors, but a knighthood earned under hard conditions proves the man had to stand somewhere dangerous and not disgrace himself. Stanislav respects that distinction immediately.

But Jareth is also an unproven baron.

That matters too.

To Stanislav, battlefield courage and baronial command are cousins, not twins. A knight may win honor by riding well, striking hard, and holding his place. A baron must keep men fed, villages loyal, roads watched, widows paid, officers restrained, and pride from turning into policy. This is uglier work, and less likely to produce songs, which is how one knows civilization is involved.

Stanislav will not patronize Jareth, but he will test him.

Not as a boy.

As a new lord.

He will watch how Jareth handles pressure, supply disputes, refugee movement, wounded men, exhausted horses, and competing noble tempers inside Beringer’s host. If Jareth listens, adapts, and makes hard calls without hiding behind either grief or rank, Stanislav’s respect will deepen quickly.

If Jareth leans too heavily on being “Baron DeSalle” without proving he understands the burden, Stanislav will become very polite.

Which, from Stanislav, is worse than being insulted.

## Relationship with Baron Kelric Vorthal

Baron Kelric Vorthal is disciplined infantry and shieldwall loyalty. Stanislav is elite cavalry speed and strike doctrine. They are natural complements and natural irritants.

Stanislav respects Vorthal’s infantry because they hold. Vorthal respects the hussars because they arrive. Neither fully understands why the other insists on doing war the slow or insane way.

This is healthy. Armies need friction. Otherwise officers might start agreeing, and then where would civilization be?

## Current Role with the Lord Marshal

Stanislav rides in Beringer’s host alongside other baronial powers, including [[Baron Jareth DeSalle]] and Baron Kelric Vorthal. Each represents a different kind of noble military value.

Jareth DeSalle is the proven knight newly burdened with rule.

Kelric Vorthal is the steadier baronial soldier, disciplined and tied to Sorek’s orbit.

Stanislav Vorokov is the cavalry aristocrat: fast, prestigious, expensive, and impossible to ignore.

Together, they give Beringer more than manpower. They give him visible noble confidence from multiple houses, each with different reasons to watch him succeed.

Stanislav’s detachment gives Beringer several immediate advantages:

- a reliable cavalry screen
- fast messengers who can fight their way through trouble
- pursuit capacity against raiders
- a prestige signal to other nobles
- a flexible reserve for sudden flank action
- a politically meaningful Orenic observer inside the host

His presence also creates pressure. If Beringer wastes the hussars, Royonad notices. If Beringer uses them brilliantly, Royonad also notices.

Either way, the alliance deepens.

## Hooks

### The Scar Challenge

A younger hussar officer challenges another noble’s officer to a lance duel after an insult. Stanislav can stop it, but may choose not to unless Beringer gives a compelling reason. This lets Beringer either enforce discipline or understand Royonad honor on its own terms.

### The Qazaq Chase

Enemy raiders appear just beyond ordinary pursuit range. Stanislav believes his hussars can catch them if released immediately. The risk is that it may be bait. The reward is prisoners, intelligence, and a huge morale victory.

### The Remount Problem

The hussars’ horses are elite and cannot be replaced by ordinary nags. A supply or remount issue forces Beringer to choose between keeping them fresh for battle or using them hard to save civilians.

### Irina’s Quiet Instruction

Duchess Irina may have given Stanislav a private instruction: observe Beringer and determine whether he is merely brave, or truly capable of command.

### Lev Vorokov Arrives

Ser Lev arrives with reinforcements or dispatches. He is talented, hungry to prove himself, and exactly the kind of young cavalry noble who could either become magnificent or die while trying to make an entrance.

### The Tavern Paid in Advance

The hussars arrive in a town and their quartermaster pays the innkeeper before anyone orders a drink. Locals realize this is not generosity. It is weather warning.

### The Silent Tack

A nervous allied officer sees the hussars go completely silent before a mission and finally understands that the drunken lunatics from the night before were only the off-duty skin of something much sharper.

## GM Notes

Stanislav should not be comic relief, though his unit’s customs are absolutely ridiculous in the way real military elites often become ridiculous when left alone with tradition, money, danger, and alcohol.

Play him as competent first.

The scars are not decoration. They tell the table what his world rewards: nerve, speed, pain tolerance, and public proof of courage.

The hussars should feel paradoxical: riotous, glamorous, loud, and half-mad in camp; silent, exact, obedient, and lethal once the signal is given. Their wildness should make other soldiers uneasy until the first real action, when it becomes obvious that the madness has rails.

Stanislav is a useful mirror for Beringer. Both men understand duty and elite martial expectation, but Stanislav comes from a culture where risk is worn openly and almost celebrated. Beringer carries guilt and restraint. Stanislav carries scars and velocity.

That contrast should spark well.

## Quick Use at Table

**First impression:** scarred white-haired cavalry baron with immaculate riding posture and amused predator eyes.  
**Mood:** charming when calm, lethal when moving.  
**Wants:** prove Royonad’s trust was well placed, preserve his hussars, see whether Beringer deserves Orenic favor.  
**Fear:** wasting irreplaceable men in someone else’s bad plan.  
**Best scene:** quietly asking Beringer hard tactical questions while his hussars laugh nearby about a duel that nearly removed someone’s nose.  
**Worst mistake:** treating him like decorative cavalry.  
**Fast quote:** “Give me ground, distance, and one honest signal. I will bring you back either prisoners or an explanation.”
