# Radovan Belik

**In-Game Date:** 10th of Coldeven, 1231 OR

> “If Belik tells you to check your girth, check your girth. If Belik tells you to pray, check your girth first.”
> — common saying among the Vorokov Hussars

> “Belik called me an undisciplined young bastard the first night I met him. He was right, naturally, which made it worse. Then the old bastard drank me under the table, carried me home over one shoulder, and sang a regimental song so filthy the horses looked offended. I learned three things that night: never match cups with a hussar, never mistake polish for softness, and never, ever assume Radovan Belik is too old to ruin your evening.”
> — Sorek Redmarch
## Overview

**Radovan Belik**, better known as **Old Red-Spur**, is the senior practical authority of the [[Vorokov Hussars]]. In western terms, he functions as the regiment’s sergeant major. In Royonad terms, that description is accurate in the same way calling a siege engine “a large stick” is accurate.

Belik is a **druznikh by battle honor**, not genealogy. His place was earned in the saddle, not inherited from a family chapel full of expensive dead men. Among the Orenics, that matters. Battle honors carry more weight than bloodline, and Old Red-Spur has enough honors hanging from him to make court genealogists nervous.

He is lean, white-haired, scarred, starting to bend with age, and still absolutely not a man anyone fucks with.

Not young officers.

Not noble sons.

Not foreign volunteers.

Not even most captains unless they have thought very carefully about the last words they want associated with their names.

Formally, Radovan Belik serves beneath [[Stanislav Vorokov]]. Practically, the working hierarchy of the regiment is often summarized as:

1. The Gods
2. The Baron
3. Old Red-Spur
4. Everyone else

There are arguments about whether Old Red-Spur belongs above or below the Baron in practical matters.

These arguments are held quietly.

Far away.

By men who enjoy remaining structurally intact.

## Appearance

![[Radovan Belik.png|340]]

Radovan Belik is old in the way ruined fortresses are old: weathered, leaning slightly, scarred by history, and still clearly capable of killing the first idiot who mistakes age for weakness.

He is lean rather than broad, with ropey strength, high cheekbones, and a neck like old tack leather. Time has begun to bend him forward at the shoulders, but the bend only makes him look more predatory, as if he is forever leaning into a report he already knows is disappointing.

His hair is white and usually drawn back with severe practicality. Several short braids are worked into it, each bound with campaign cords, mourning wraps, horsehair, and small metal beads earned through service. His mustache is long, heavy, and weighted in the hussar style, though with less vanity than the younger men. His jewelry is not decorative. It is a record.

His face is cut by old scars: lance graze, saber line, hoof split, arrow crease. None are hidden. His pale eyes are stern, cold, and horribly alert.

Belik wears richly marked hussar armor: scale and plate, red cords, old campaign knots, medal chains, spur honors, and the worn distinctions of a man decorated too often by people who quietly hoped he might retire. His kit is polished, but never pretty. Even his ornamentation looks like it has corrected someone’s posture.

When giving reports, he stands at attention with his helmet under one arm. Not because he is relaxed.

Because everyone should be able to see his face when he ruins their morning.

## Rank: Druznikh-Starshy

**Druznikh-Starshy** means something close to **Senior Battle-Companion** or **Elder Druznikh**. It is both rank and honor.

A **druznikh** is roughly equivalent to a knight in Gorundian usage, but the Orenics care less about genealogy than battle honor. A druznikh must have proven himself in war. Noble blood may help a man get noticed. It does not make him worthy.

A **druznikh-starshy** is a senior honored rider whose authority comes from repeated proof, not parchment. He may not outrank noble officers in the formal chain, but a wise officer understands that formal chains are only useful until a veteran knows where to yank.

Belik is the current druznikh-starshy of the Vorokov Hussars.

He is responsible for making sure the regiment remains what it claims to be.

That is worse than command.

Command wins battles.

Belik preserves the thing that can win them.

## Old Red-Spur

The name **Old Red-Spur** comes from Belik’s most famous visible honor: a battered red-enamel spur token worn near his left hip. The original spur was awarded after he returned to the line wounded, unhorsed, and bleeding badly enough that another man would have had the decency to fall down.

He did not fall down.

He stole an enemy horse, rallied three scattered hussars, recovered two wounded men, and returned with a broken pennon strapped to his saddle.

When told afterward that he was lucky to be alive, he reportedly answered:

> “The horse was lucky. I knew where we were going.”

Since then, red spur-tokens have become a recognized mark among the hussars, but Belik’s is the old one. The ugly one. The one nobody touches.

## Social Origin

Belik was not born to one of the grand cavalry houses. He came from the practical underside of Vorochev’s military world: horse lines, tack sheds, border roads, remount work, and the kind of families that knew every noble’s horse by temperament before they knew the noble’s name.

Different stories place his birth in different places:

- a remount village under Vorochev protection
- a petty-freeholder family near a border road
- the son of a widowed farrier and a dead scout
- a groom taken into hussar service after saving a horse line during a raid

Belik does not clarify.

When asked directly, he says:

> “I was born close enough to horses to learn sense, and far enough from nobles to survive childhood.”

This is generally treated as answer enough.

## Early Service

Belik entered the hussar world below the dignity of men who cared about dignity.

He started in the horse lines: mucking stalls, carrying tack, learning feed ratios, listening to veterans, and being shouted at by men who had not yet realized he was listening better than their sons.

He became a remount handler, then courier, then auxiliary scout. He was not admitted because of lineage, patronage, or courtly promise. He was admitted because he kept doing useful things under conditions that should have discouraged him.

His first true notice came during a border pursuit when a patrol was baited into broken ground. A young noble panicked, a senior rider went down, and the horses began to bunch.

Belik cut through the confusion, turned the spare mounts, and opened a path before the enemy could close the trap. He was still not a hussar then.

Afterward, several officers agreed that ignoring him had become inconvenient.

He was made a Young Spur.

This displeased some noble families.

Belik survived their displeasure, which became a theme.

## Earning His Spurs

Belik earned his spurs the hard way: without enough friends, without enough bloodline, and with everyone watching for the mistake that would prove he had never belonged.

He did not give them one.

He proved himself through horse care first, then riding, then silence under pressure. He was never the flashiest Young Spur. He was rarely the loudest. He did not sing well, which remains one of his few documented failures.

But he could ride.

He could read terrain.

He could keep frightened horses moving.

He could hear bad tack from twenty paces.

He could tell when a noble officer was about to choose glory over survival.

That last skill proved invaluable and politically irritating.

Belik’s spurs were awarded after a pursuit action in which he refused a reckless chase, held a reserve file back, and then used those riders to recover the men who had ignored the signal and been ambushed.

One of the rescued men was noble-born.

Two of the men who had followed Belik were not.

The regiment noticed.

## Earning His Wings

Belik earned his wings during the action later remembered as **Zalvod Ford**, though hussars often just call it **the Ford** in the grim way soldiers shorten the names of places that still hurt.

A Vorokov detachment was caught between raiders and bad water during a withdrawal. The enemy expected panic. What they got was Belik holding the ford with a broken lance, three riders, and a string of half-blown remounts while the rest of the patrol crossed.

He was wounded twice.

His horse died under him.

He crossed last, on foot, carrying another man’s saddle and leading a wounded mare by the reins because abandoning tack and horseflesh was apparently more offensive to him than bleeding.

For that action he received:

- the Black Cord of Zalvod Ford
- the White Rein
- full wings
- eventual nomination as druznikh

When a noble complained that a common-born rider receiving druznikh honor was “irregular,” the then-Baron reportedly asked whether the noble wished to repeat Belik’s ride and make the paperwork easier.

The complaint vanished.

Civilization occasionally works when sarcasm has cavalry support.

## Battle Honors

Belik wears his honors in hussar fashion: visible, specific, and interpretable by those who know the code.

### Red Spur

Awarded for returning to battle after being unhorsed and wounded.

Belik’s Red Spur is the old honor from the action that gave him his name.

### White Rein

Awarded for saving a mount, remount line, or superior officer’s horse under fire.

Belik earned his at Zalvod Ford.

### Black Cord of Zalvod Ford

A mourning honor worn for a disastrous action where many riders were lost. Belik wears it for his dead lance-partners and the horse that carried him into the ford.

### Gold Lance Knot

Awarded for breaking enemy cavalry in close action.

Belik’s knot is faded, rewrapped, and older than several current officers.

### Iron Beads

Worn for surviving failed pursuit, encirclement, or enemy bait.

Belik wears several. He dislikes discussing most of them.

### Empty Saddle Keeper

Not merely an honor, but an office.

Belik keeps the regiment’s roll of dead hussars, dead horses of distinction, and men whose names must be spoken before their place is reused at table.

He remembers names better than he remembers compliments.

This is not a flaw.

## Duties

Belik’s duties are both formal and terrifyingly informal.

He oversees:

- recruit trials
- Young Spur discipline
- daily drill
- silent signal discipline
- lance practice
- saber correction
- mounted archery standards
- watch rotation
- tack inspection
- remount condition
- camp conduct
- internal punishments
- veteran honors
- mourning customs
- the Empty Saddle Roll
- the correction of arrogant officers

He also performs the one duty nobody writes down because everyone is happier pretending it does not exist:

He tells the Baron when the regiment is lying to itself.

## Authority

Belik does not command the regiment in the noble sense. He does not lead grand charges by right of office. He does not make baronial policy. He does not speak for House Vorokov.

That is the theory.

In practice, his authority is immense.

A hussar may appeal an officer’s punishment.

He does not appeal Belik’s disappointment.

A rotmistrz may argue tactics with the Baron.

He does not argue tack with Belik.

A noble father may complain that his son is being treated harshly.

Belik will explain that the horse is being treated fairly, and the son is being offered the same opportunity.

The father usually regrets writing.

## Relationship with Stanislav Vorokov

Belik serves [[Stanislav Vorokov]] loyally, but not softly.

Their relationship is one of mutual necessity. Stanislav commands the hussars, embodies the barony, and carries the noble face of the regiment. Belik maintains the machine beneath the red cloak.

Stanislav trusts Belik because Belik does not flatter him. Belik trusts Stanislav because Stanislav listens before making glorious mistakes, which is more than can be said for many men born to command horses and other people’s sons.

They disagree often.

They do not perform those disagreements for the regiment unless the lesson requires it.

Stanislav knows that if Belik says a horse line is unfit, it is unfit. If Belik says a Young Spur is not ready, he is not ready. If Belik says an officer is brave but dangerous, the officer is already standing over a hole and admiring the view.

Belik, for his part, knows Stanislav is not merely a noble peacock. The Baron rides hard, reads war well, and has earned enough scars that Belik does not need to pretend respect.

This may be the closest Belik comes to affection.

## Relationship with Mila Vorokov

Belik respects [[Mila Vorokov]] without theater.

This is significant.

Mila manages the household, remount obligations, veteran pensions, officer family politics, and the web of supply that lets the regiment exist. Belik understands better than most that cavalry glory is built on ledgers, feed, farriers, and widows being paid before resentment curdles into politics.

He reports to her when the matter concerns:

- pension failures
- remount family disputes
- wounded veterans
- dead men’s households
- misconduct involving local families
- marriages that may disrupt officer politics
- supply dishonesty
- horse theft
- young nobles creating messes larger than their rank

Their conversations are famously efficient.

Neither wastes words.

Servants claim the two of them once resolved a three-family inheritance dispute, a remount debt, and an officer’s secret engagement in less than twenty minutes.

This is considered one of the great administrative cavalry actions of the decade, though no bard has yet had the courage to sing about it.

## Relationship with Lev Vorokov

Belik treats [[Lev Vorokov]] with the careful cruelty reserved for heirs who might someday matter.

He does not bully Lev.

That would be too easy.

He tests him.

Constantly.

Lev’s seat, tack, timing, silence, temper, and assumptions are corrected with the same pitiless fairness Belik gives any Young Spur. This is both honor and warning. Belik will not let the heir become decorative while better men die around him.

Lev may someday command the hussars.

Belik intends for him to understand what that means before men start obeying him for the wrong reasons.

When Lev does well, Belik says almost nothing.

When Lev does badly, Belik becomes educational.

The hussars watch these exchanges closely. They are not merely about training. They are about succession, worthiness, and whether the next Vorokov understands that the regiment is not inherited like a silver cup.

## Relationship with the Hussars

To the hussars, Belik is terror, law, memory, and reluctant pride.

Young Spurs fear him.

Full hussars pretend not to fear him.

Veterans know better and fear him more intelligently.

He is the man who notices everything:

- a frayed girth
- an unclean bit
- a false boast
- a hidden limp
- a rider favoring one shoulder
- a horse refusing pressure
- dice loaded badly
- grief being worn as recklessness
- cowardice disguised as caution
- courage disguised as stupidity

He punishes sharply, but not randomly. This is why the men accept him. Belik is hard because the work is hard. He is cruel only when someone tries to make danger someone else’s problem.

The hussars call him **that old bastard** with real affection.

Not where he can hear.

Usually.

## Relationship with Commoners and Foreign Riders

Belik is often harder on noble recruits than common-born candidates, but not because he is sentimental.

He knows noble recruits arrive with advantages. Horses, tutors, armor, letters, family names, and the assumption that the world will at least pause before disappointing them.

Commoners arrive with fewer illusions or have them beaten out faster.

Belik respects ability wherever it appears, but he does not confuse hunger with readiness. A commoner must still ride, obey, fight, care for the horse, and survive the judgment of men who do not like being outdone by someone with no crest.

Belik does not protect common-born riders from hardship.

He protects the standard from dishonor.

If a commoner earns wings, Belik will enforce that fact with all the warmth of a closing portcullis.

The same is true of foreigners. If a rider from outside Orenic territory earns wings cleanly, Belik treats him as a hussar.

The accent may be mocked.

The man is not.

## Relationship with Sorek Redmarch

Belik and [[Sorek Redmarch]] understand each other too well to waste time on warmth.

Sorek sees in Belik the same old soldier’s disease he recognizes in himself: a refusal to die before the work is properly handed over. Belik sees in Sorek a dangerous infantry-minded professional who knows enough about cavalry to ask useful questions and enough about command not to dress stupidity as morale.

They would not call each other friends.

That would require both men to become briefly worse.

But each knows the other is real.

If Sorek asks for a straight report, Belik gives one.

If Belik says a situation will cost horses, Sorek listens.

If either man insults the other, everyone nearby should assume useful information is being exchanged under camouflage.

## Relationship with Beringer Amadis

Belik’s view of [[Beringer]] is cautious and measuring.

Beringer is not his lord. Beringer is not Orenic. Beringer did not grow up under the red pennons, and Belik has no interest in being impressed by grief, destiny, or court whispers about succession.

But Beringer carries command pressure without making it everyone else’s problem. That matters.

Belik watches how Beringer treats horses, scouts, messengers, exhausted men, and bad news. He watches whether Beringer listens to men who know their work. He watches whether Beringer spends elite troops for glory or necessity.

If Beringer proves worthy of the hussars, Belik will never say so plainly.

He will simply begin giving him shorter reports.

That is praise.

Apparently.

## Mannerisms

- stands too straight for a man his age
- leans forward slightly when displeased
- checks tack while speaking to people
- uses silence as a weapon
- names horses before riders
- rarely raises his voice
- never repeats an order unless the second time is punishment
- taps his red spur before delivering especially bad news
- looks at young nobles as if estimating repair costs
- removes his helmet for formal reports so the recipient must look him in the face

## Voice

Belik speaks plainly, with dry brutality and very little wasted motion. His humor exists, but it is old, sharp, and usually discovered in the wound after the fact.

He does not rant.

He reports.

Somehow this is worse.

Common lines:

- “Your horse knows you are lying.”
- “Again. Worse this time, but louder. Very noble.”
- “If courage were enough, cemeteries would be empty.”
- “The Baron gave you a command. I am giving you a chance to survive obeying it.”
- “Do not apologize to me. Apologize to the horse.”
- “You may keep the mustache when you have earned the face.”
- “That was not a charge. That was a funeral rehearsing.”
- “You are not dead, so the lesson continues.”
- “The pennon does not care who your father was.”
- “Good. Now do it tired.”

## The Empty Saddle Roll

The **Empty Saddle Roll** is one of Belik’s sacred duties.

It records the hussars who died in service, the horses of distinction lost under them, the actions where they fell, and the debts still owed to widows, children, comrades, and memory.

No dead hussar’s place at table is reused until his name has been spoken.

No horse of distinction is sold into common work if it can be pensioned, bred, or buried honorably.

Belik enforces these customs with grim precision.

A man may forget a debt.

The roll does not.

## The Red-Spur Punishments

Belik’s punishments are famous because they are rarely theatrical. He does not waste time humiliating men when exhaustion will teach more thoroughly.

Common punishments include:

- silent tack checks before dawn
- remount yard duty after night drinking
- carrying saddle gear on foot for miles
- cleaning every bit in the squadron
- riding without stirrups until posture improves
- feeding and watering every horse before eating
- memorizing the Empty Saddle Roll entry of a man whose lesson applies
- standing watch beside a horse one has wronged
- being forbidden to wear a decorative honor until conduct catches up with vanity

The worst punishment is not shouting.

The worst punishment is Belik saying:

> “Not yet.”

That means a man who thought he was ready has been found wanting.

The young ones hate it.

The veterans remember it.

## Secrets and Rumors

### He Refused Officer Rank

There are repeated rumors that Belik was offered formal officer elevation and refused it.

The common version says he answered:

> “If I become an officer, who will teach them not to die like officers?”

This may be apocryphal.

It also sounds exactly like him, which is how apocrypha survives.

### He Can Name Every Dead Horse

The hussars claim Belik can name every horse of distinction lost during his service, including color, temper, rider, and death.

This is probably exaggerated.

Probably.

### He Once Corrected a Baron

The young hussars whisper that Belik once corrected a previous Baron of Vorochev in front of witnesses.

Older hussars do not whisper.

They were there.

### He Keeps Letters

Belik keeps letters from widows, mothers, sons, and daughters of dead hussars. He does not discuss them. He does not display them. He does not throw them away.

### He Is Older Than He Says

No one knows his exact age. The official rolls are inconsistent, possibly because border records burned, possibly because Belik refuses to let clerks become overconfident.

## Current Role

As of the 10th of Coldeven, 1231 OR, Belik rides with the Vorokov Hussars attached to [[Beringer]] and the Lord Marshal’s host.

His job is not merely to keep the hussars disciplined.

His job is to make sure the regiment remains itself while operating inside a larger host full of foreign officers, noble tempers, refugee pressures, desperate decisions, and commanders who may not yet understand what the red pennons cost.

He will watch Beringer.

He will watch Stanislav.

He will watch the Young Spurs.

He will watch the horses first.

Naturally.
