

# Sir Edmar Rusk

> “Law is not mercy. Law is not vengeance. Law is the knife we keep sharp so fools stop reaching for axes.”  
> — Sir Edmar Rusk

## Overview

**Sir Edmar Rusk** is the **High Provost of the Marshal’s Court**, the officer responsible for enforcing military law under the authority of the [[Beringer|Lord Marshal of Gorundia]]. He investigates misconduct among officers, knights, soldiers, sworn men, garrisons, and wartime commands.

He is not popular.

This is generally taken as proof that he is functioning correctly.

Rusk is a [[Duchy Gravendar|Gravendar]] man by birth, an old veteran of the **Iron Levy**, and a product of the hard military culture that House Vogel long claimed as its own. He is not, however, a Vogel loyalist. That distinction matters to him more than most people realize.

He believes discipline is civilization’s last clean tool. He has seen what happens when command becomes appetite, when uniforms become excuses, and when men decide that “following orders” is a spell of absolution.

It is not.

Not in his court.

## Appearance

Rusk is a spare, iron-gray man in his late fifties or early sixties, still straight-backed despite old campaign injuries. He is not imposing by size, but he has the unpleasant stillness of a man who has watched armed men lie and waited until they stopped.

His hair is cropped short. His beard, if worn, is trimmed close. His clothes are severe, clean, and practical: dark wool, Marshalate badge, court-chain when required, gloves when handling evidence.

He wears a sword, but not theatrically.

His most dangerous weapon is usually a sealed folio.

## Demeanor

Rusk is dry, exacting, and nearly impossible to rattle.

He does not shout. He does not bluster. He does not threaten unless the threat has already been entered into procedure.

He speaks in clean categories:

- prisoner    
- witness    
- suspect    
- officer    
- sworn man    
- criminal    
- noble prisoner    
- protected person    
- evidence    

He has little patience for men who blur those categories for convenience.

He is not cruel, but he is merciless toward evasion. He will give a frightened soldier water, a wounded prisoner time to stop bleeding, and a noble every courtesy his rank requires. Then he will ask the question again.

## Faith

Rusk is privately a lay worshiper of **Stern Alia**, revering discipline, duty, lawful judgment, and the stern obligations of command.

He is not clergy.  
He is not a paladin.  
He does not preach.

His faith appears in habit rather than display:

- a small iron token kept inside his writing case    
- a brief pause before passing sentence recommendations    
- distaste for theatrical mercy and theatrical cruelty alike    
- a belief that law must bind the powerful first, or it becomes theater for the poor    

Some assume he is Pholtine because of his love of clean procedure, exact language, and evidentiary order. He does not correct them unless it matters.

He respects Pholtus.

He worships Stern Alia.

That difference is not sentimental. It is structural.

Pholtus says truth must be illuminated.

Stern Alia says duty must be borne.

Rusk has lived too long among soldiers to think light alone is enough.

## The Iron Levy

The **Iron Levy** was a Grevandar emergency levy tradition raised from mining towns, forge districts, toll-road settlements, and hard border communities. It was not a knightly formation and not a noble household guard.

It was heavy common infantry: spear, shield, bill, hammer, crossbow, and wagon-chain.

The Iron Levy was called when Grevandar needed bodies that would hold.

Its men were not glamorous. They were blacksmiths, miners, roadmen, charcoal haulers, town guards, and older veterans dragged back into harness because they knew how not to run.

Under Vogel discipline, the Iron Levy became brutally reliable. Too reliable, sometimes. It taught men to obey, endure, and distrust any command that sounded too pretty.

Rusk served in the Iron Levy as a young man, first as a common levy officer, then as a field adjutant during internal suppression actions and frontier scares. He learned three things there:

1. Common soldiers are often braver than their betters deserve.    
2. Officers lie upward and downward with equal ease.    
3. Bad orders become atrocities when no one writes them down.    

That last lesson made him what he is.

## Knighthood — The Red Chain Muster

Rusk earned his knighthood during the **Red Chain Muster**, a Grevandar levy crisis that began as corruption and nearly became massacre.

During a frontier scare, the Iron Levy was raised from mining towns, road settlements, forge districts, and debtor communities. The muster commander and quartermaster falsified rolls, stole pay, and quietly substituted chained debtors and convict laborers for trained levy men while still claiming full military credit.

When the levy discovered the fraud, the camp broke into violence.

Officers called it mutiny.  
The men called it theft.  
Both were correct enough to make the law inconvenient.

Rusk, then a levy adjutant attached to provost duty, secured the pay rolls, sealed the muster chest, and prevented the immediate execution of several men accused without witness. When the disorder spread toward the Chainbridge crossing, Rusk gathered twenty-six loyal levy men and held the bridge until Crown riders arrived.

He did not merely suppress the rising. He preserved the evidence needed to prove why it had happened.

The later court found several levy men guilty of violence and desertion, but also convicted the commander, quartermaster, and two recruiting officers for fraud, unlawful confinement, and abuse of Crown levy authority.

Rusk was knighted for conduct under arms and for preserving lawful judgment during military collapse.

Formal citation:

> “For holding the Chainbridge, preserving the muster rolls, preventing unlawful execution, and delivering guilty men of both high and low station to judgment.”

Among soldiers, the story is shorter:

> “Rusk got knighted because everyone else wanted blood and he wanted names.”

Rusk dislikes both versions.

## Arms

**Blazon:** Sable, a chain fesswise argent between three closed eyes argent; on a chief gules, a bridge of iron proper.

Sir Edmar Rusk’s arms are spare and severe, granted after the Red Chain Muster. The silver chain represents lawful custody and restraint before judgment, while the three closed eyes signify refusal to be led by spectacle, rumor, or rank. The iron bridge on red recalls the Chainbridge, where Rusk held the crossing and preserved the muster rolls until Crown riders arrived.

The arms are often disliked by young knights, who find them grim.

Rusk considers this a favorable review.

**Motto:** Names Before Blood
## Service History

Rusk’s early career began in Grevandar’s military apparatus. He was competent, exact, and difficult to intimidate. He showed little interest in glory and considerable interest in whether a unit’s muster rolls matched the men actually standing in the mud.

He rose through court-martial work because no one could easily bribe him, flatter him, or frighten him. Worse, from the perspective of most officers, he remembered details.

He became known as the sort of man sent after:

- missing pay chests    
- deserter rings    
- false musters    
- unlawful floggings    
- suspicious deaths in garrisons    
- commanders who confused discipline with personal temper    
- noble sons who thought rank was a weatherproof cloak    

When the Marshalate required a High Provost, Rusk was an obvious and regrettable choice.

Obvious because he was qualified.

Regrettable because he would do the job.

## As High Provost

As **High Provost of the Marshal’s Court**, Rusk enforces military law within the Lord Marshal’s jurisdiction.

His duties include:

- convening field courts    
- gathering testimony    
- sorting prisoners by legal status    
- ordering confinement    
- preserving evidence    
- reviewing officer conduct    
- recommending punishment    
- distinguishing lawful resistance from rebellion    
- distinguishing obedience from criminal complicity    
- making everyone in the room wish law was less specific    

He works closely with the [[Keeper of Oaths]] when parole, sworn submission, knightly vows, or formal battlefield promises are involved.

He works less happily with chaplains, not because he disrespects them, but because confession, absolution, testimony, and evidence become a legal swamp if no one marks the banks.

## Personality

Rusk believes discipline is not obedience alone.

To him, discipline means knowing where obedience ends.

He has no patience for the phrase “just following orders” when used by men who profited, improvised cruelty, burned records, or enjoyed the work.

He does, however, distinguish carefully between:

- soldiers who stood under command    
- frightened levies    
- common men trapped by oath    
- officers who failed    
- officers who ordered crimes    
- men who wore uniforms while acting as criminals    

That distinction makes him more just than many softer men.

It also makes him more frightening.

## Relationship to Beringer

Rusk serves [[Beringer]] because the Crown has named Beringer Lord Marshal, and because Beringer appears to understand that command without law becomes hunger.

He does not flatter Beringer.

He does not soften reports for him.

He will tell Beringer when mercy is legally sound, when severity is required, and when a political execution is trying to sneak into the room wearing judicial clothes.

Rusk privately approves of Beringer’s instinct to separate honorable soldiers from criminals, but he watches him carefully.

Rusk has seen good men discover power and call it responsibility.

Sometimes they are right.

Sometimes they are only early.

## Relationship to Sorek Redmarch

Rusk respects [[Sorek Redmarch]] as a soldier who understands consequences.

They are not friends in any warm sense. They are two old campaign men who can stand in the same room, smell bullshit at the same time, and let the other speak first.

Sorek trusts Rusk’s categories.

Rusk trusts Sorek’s field instincts.

Neither of them trusts a clean report from a dirty fort.

## Relationship to Dame Katherine Kendal

Rusk respects [[Katherine Kendal]] more than he says.

Her conduct in formal witnessed combat, her insistence on fact over rumor, and her eye for the political danger of mishandled evidence all register with him.

He may privately consider her young, dangerous, and still learning where rage ends and justice begins.

That is not condemnation.

It is assessment.

After Blackbar, if Kat kills a principal criminal in fury, Rusk’s reaction will not be moral outrage first. It will be procedural horror.

Then, perhaps, private understanding.

Then, still, procedure.

Because civilization is mostly people doing the right thing in the wrong order and then needing paperwork.

## Relationship to Rykeena

Rusk grudgingly respects [[Rykeena]].

He does not trust her easily. He sees ambition, appetite, and social hunger. He also sees discipline when she chooses to use it, and a capacity to understand power without pretending it is clean.

His note from the Bannerhall era reads simply:

> Supports discipline, grudgingly respects Rykeena.

This does not mean he likes her.

It means he believes she may become useful if she learns to put a leash on the parts of herself that want applause before result.

That is almost praise, from him.

## Possible Flip

Earlier notes suggest Rusk “might flip later.”

If he flips, it should not be because he is secretly corrupt or suddenly sentimental.

Rusk flips only if he concludes that the lawful chain of command has become unlawful in substance.

Possible triggers:

- the Crown attempts to bury clear evidence    
- Beringer is ordered to commit or excuse atrocity    
- the Marshalate becomes a political weapon against lawful subjects    
- prisoners are executed for convenience rather than judgment    
- the king’s justice becomes indistinguishable from Vogel practice    
- someone tries to use Rusk’s court as a stamp for a decision already made    

Rusk will endure harsh law.

He will not sign false law.

That is the line.

## At Blackbar

Rusk is the correct officer to take over legal sorting after Blackbar falls.

He classifies the captured as:

- honorable prisoners    
- surrendered soldiers    
- detained witnesses    
- protected rescued persons    
- suspects    
- criminal prisoners    
- officers under inquiry    
- clerks and key-holders    
- principals    

His core assessment:

> “Blackbar is evidence.”

Not merely a location where crimes occurred. The layout, ledgers, holding pens, toll records, Pig Lantern accounts, patrol routes, and false debt language together form a machine.

Rusk’s likely recommendation:

- Alric’s road detachment: parole under House DeSalle surety after names are checked    
- ordinary garrison: hold by unit, question, release or reassign only after witness comparison    
- Black Spur patrolmen: criminal custody    
- clerks and toll officers: court custody pending document review    
- Pig Lantern staff: separated by function    
- rescued persons: protected, not examined, until healers and appointed women permit it    
- Mother Salla Greaves: principal suspect    
- Brennart Soot: isolated in irons, no contact with other prisoners    

## Speech Style

Rusk speaks plainly, dryly, and in legal categories.

He does not waste metaphors, though when he uses one, it tends to land like a dropped anvil.

Sample lines:

> “A uniform does not launder a crime.”

> “He is embarrassed. Not yet guilty.”

> “There is a difference between smelling rot and owning the knife.”

> “Patrolmen sin with hands. Clerks sin with continuance.”

> “If anyone steals it, hang them before they explain.”

> “Mercy is not the absence of judgment. It is judgment refusing to become appetite.”

> “No man will be charged because he wore the wrong colors. No man will be spared because he wore them under orders.”

> “The law does not become cleaner because everyone is shouting.”

## Game Use

Use Rusk when the party needs:

- legal clarity    
- prisoner sorting    
- hard truth without melodrama    
- evidence handling    
- military law    
- field courts    
- someone who can stand up to Beringer respectfully    
- someone who can tell a furious knight “no” and survive the room    
- someone who knows how systems become crimes    

He is not there to steal scenes.

He is there to make scenes sharper.

When Rusk enters, the question changes from:

> “Who deserves punishment?”

to:

> “What can be proven, under whose authority, and at what cost if we get it wrong?”

That is his function.

Deeply annoying.

Absolutely necessary.
