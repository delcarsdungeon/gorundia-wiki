## Captain Brennic Ashhook

_Militia-Captain of Ashmire Kilns_

> “My lord, we’ve got sixty-three men who can hold a spear without stabbing their own boots, twelve who’ve seen real fighting, and three who think courage means shouting before dying. I’ve put those three in back.”
> — Captain Brennic Ashhook

Captain Brennic Ashhook is the militia-captain of [[Ashmire Kilns]], a soot-stained settlement in Desalle territory known for its clay pits, brickworks, lime smoke, hard labor, and harder men.

He is a former kiln-yard foreman and retired soldier, broad-shouldered, practical, and missing most of his left arm. What remains of the limb is usually pinned or bound neatly beneath his coat, though he refuses to make theater of it. His voice sounds like gravel being shoveled into a coffin, and his patience has the exact temperature of cooling brick.

Brennic is not noble.

He is not courtly.

He is not impressed by noble problems.

This makes him immediately useful.

---

## Appearance and Bearing

Brennic is a thick-built man in late middle age, with smoke-roughened skin, heavy brows, close-cropped hair, and the permanent squint of someone who has spent half his life around kiln glare.

His clothes are clean but plain: dark wool, patched leather, a serviceable belt, old soldier’s boots, and a militia captain’s badge that looks better maintained than ornamental. Ash and clay seem to follow him even indoors.

He carries himself like a man who learned discipline the hard way and command the harder way. He does not bark unless barking is needed. He does not flatter. He does not waste words.

Men listen when he speaks because he sounds like he has already survived the consequences.

---

## Background

Before becoming militia-captain, Brennic served as a soldier, then returned to Ashmire Kilns and worked as a kiln-yard foreman. The two trades taught him much the same lessons: keep men in line, respect heat, never trust a cracked wall, and never let fools stand where pressure is about to release.

He lost most of his left arm years ago. Stories differ on how. Some say it was taken in battle. Others say a kiln collapse crushed it beyond saving. Brennic does not encourage either version, which naturally means both stories have grown more dramatic with each retelling.

After his soldiering days, he became the man local workers went to when disputes needed settling before knives came out. From there, militia command was almost inevitable. In a place like Ashmire, the distinction between “foreman,” “sergeant,” and “the man who stops the idiots from burning the district down” is mostly clerical.

---

## Role in Ashmire Kilns

Brennic commands the local militia with practical competence and very little romance.

He knows exactly how many men he has, which ones can march, which ones can shoot, which ones will panic, which ones will lie about panicking, and which ones need to be kept away from lantern oil.

His militia is not elite, but it is not useless. Ashmire men are used to heat, weight, smoke, and exhaustion. They can dig, haul, brace, build barricades, hold alleys, and fight dirty when cornered.

Brennic does not pretend they are knights.

He also does not permit anyone else to pretend they are cowards.

---

## Meeting the Lord Marshal

When the Lord Marshal rode through Desalle territory, Brennic presented himself politely and without hesitation. He offered his men for service, not with bluster, but with the sober understanding that the barony had reached the point where decent men either stood up or spent the rest of their lives explaining why they had not.

He also tried, carefully, to warn the Lord Marshal about [[South Desalle]].

He did not accuse wildly. He did not rant. He did what practical men do when speaking upward to power: he chose his words like stones laid across bad ground.

South Desalle, he suggested, was not what it appeared to be.

Too clean.

Too rehearsed.

Too ready for visitors.

Too full of people who knew what to say and too empty of people willing to say what they knew.

Brennic had no ledger, confession, or formal proof. What he had was the instinct of a foreman, a soldier, and a local man who knew the smell of fresh paint over rot.

That was enough for him to risk speaking.

---

## Meeting Edwyn Durathen

Brennic asked the Lord Marshal for one favor: to speak with [[Edwyn Durathen]].

The Lord Marshal did not hesitate. He sent for Edwyn.

Edwyn, for his part, was slightly nervous. That alone says something, because men who have killed barons in formal judgment are not usually made nervous by kiln captains unless something honest is about to happen. Horrifying stuff, honesty. Gets under the armor.

When Edwyn arrived, Brennic stepped forward and offered his hand.

> “You did a great thing for this barony.”

Edwyn was embarrassed and confused by the praise.

> “I did what honor demands.”

Brennic held his hand a moment longer and answered plainly:

> “That may be, but I thank you all the same.”

The exchange was brief, but it mattered.

To Edwyn, the death of Baron Remy Desalle may have been a matter of honor, law, and necessity.

To Brennic and men like him, it was something simpler.

Someone powerful had finally answered for what had been done.

That distinction matters. Nobles often die for principles. Commoners remember what those principles cost them.

## Character

Brennic is polite, fearless, and direct. He understands rank and observes it, but he does not confuse rank with worth. He has worked for nobles, fought under nobles, buried men because of nobles, and watched nobles explain disasters as though vocabulary could resurrect the dead.

He is not a radical.

He is not a rebel.

He is a loyal local captain who believes authority has duties. When those duties are abandoned, his respect does not survive on ceremony alone.

He will obey a lawful command from the Lord Marshal.

He will not waste men for vanity.

He will not send boys to die so a lord can pretend the village is grateful.

He will stand in a doorway with one arm and sixty-three half-trained men if that is what the barony requires.

---

## Personality Traits

- Practical before brave, which makes his bravery more useful.
- Polite to rank, blunt to nonsense.
- Counts men before speeches.
- Notices staged prosperity and rehearsed answers.
- Deeply suspicious of clean boots in dirty places.
- Treats soldiers, laborers, and villagers as people with names, not “local resources.”
- Does not romanticize war, poverty, or noble honor.
- Respects Edwyn Durathen, though not in a fawning way.
- Trusts the Lord Marshal more after being heard without delay.

---

## Views on South Desalle

Brennic considers [[South Desalle]] a lie with cottages.

He does not know every mechanism behind it, but he knows what a genuine village looks like. South Desalle does not breathe right.

The people are too careful.

The paths are too clean.

The wrong houses are repaired.

The wrong mouths speak.

The grain stores look managed for inspection rather than use.

The mayor’s answers arrive too quickly.

To Brennic, the whole place feels like a kiln wall whitewashed over a crack: impressive until the heat comes on.

---

## Use in Play

Brennic works best as a grounded local witness. He is not there to dump exposition like a broken cart of plot bricks. He is there to tell the party what a place feels like to someone who actually lives under its consequences.

He can provide:

- local militia support
- warnings about staged villages and false prosperity
- names of reliable locals
- quiet testimony against Desalle corruption
- practical village-level intelligence
- a common soldier’s view of noble justice
- a moral counterweight to courtly abstractions

He should not be treated as helpless. He is physically maimed, not diminished. He knows how to command, how to read men, and how to recognize danger before it becomes official.

## Relationship with Edwyn Durathen

Brennic respects [[Edwyn Durathen]] because Edwyn killed Baron Remy Desalle and, in doing so, broke a thing many locals had come to believe would never break.

That does not mean Brennic thinks Edwyn is perfect. It means Edwyn did something that mattered.

For Edwyn, the act may have been a grim matter of honor.

For Brennic, it was the first visible proof that the barony had not been abandoned entirely to painted lies and polite cruelty.

This gives Brennic’s gratitude weight. He is not cheering bloodshed. He is thanking a man for making consequence possible.

---

## Relationship with the Lord Marshal

Brennic’s respect for the Lord Marshal sharpened when his request to meet Edwyn was granted without delay.

That mattered.

Many lords would have brushed him aside, stalled, demanded written petition, or treated the request as an impertinence from a soot-stained cripple with a militia badge.

The Lord Marshal simply sent for Edwyn.

To Brennic, that says more than ceremony.

He may not understand every move the Crown is making, but he recognizes a commander who listens when a local man risks speaking plainly.

That is enough to earn cooperation.

Not blind loyalty.

Cooperation.

The difference is one Brennic cares about.

---

## Reputation

In Ashmire Kilns, Brennic is trusted because he is known.

He has settled wage fights, stopped drunken knife-work, dragged men out of kiln smoke, buried workers, drilled boys who thought spears made them soldiers, and told grieving mothers the truth when softer men hid behind phrases.

The workers call him hard.

The militia calls him fair.

The mayoral types call him difficult.

That last one is probably the strongest endorsement.

---

## Summary

Captain Brennic Ashhook is the kind of local commander who becomes important in a crisis because he knows the ground, the people, and the lies.

He is no courtier, no schemer, and no grand strategist.

He is a one-armed former soldier from a kiln village who looked at South Desalle, smelled fresh paint over rot, and had the nerve to tell the Lord Marshal something was wrong.

In a barony built on staged loyalty and managed appearances, that makes him dangerous.
