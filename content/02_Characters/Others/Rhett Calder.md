## Rhett Calder

**Race:** Human  
**Profession:** Bard (Satirist, Performer, Narrative Operator)  
**Reputation:** Bold, sharp-tongued, survives things he probably shouldn’t  

---

## Description

Rhett Calder is a broad-chested man in his early 30s with a stage presence that hits before his voice does—and that’s saying something.

- thick shoulders, built more like a caravan guard than a court minstrel  
- dark hair, usually tied back, a little too loose to be fashionable  
- trimmed beard, kept just shy of respectable  
- eyes that are always working the room, even when he’s “relaxed”  

His voice is his weapon:

- deep, resonant, carries without effort  
- can fill a hall without raising volume  
- can drop to a near-confessional murmur that pulls people in  

He dresses well, but not like a peacock—more like a man who expects to move quickly if needed.

When he stands still, he looks composed.

When he performs, he looks **dangerous**.

---

## Overview

Rhett Calder is an itinerant bard of the western circuits known for satirical ballads that walk the line between admiration and insult.

He built his reputation on songs that sound like praise until the listener realizes—half a verse too late—that they’ve just been cut.

He is not reckless.

He is **fast**.

And when one of his songs—*The Marshal’s Table*—crossed from clever into lethal, he moved faster than the fallout.

---

## Background

Rhett was born along a western trade circuit, the son of:

- a caravan guard who died before Rhett was ten  
- a tavern singer who taught him two things:
  - how to hold a room  
  - how to leave one before it turns  

He learned early that:

> A bad crowd doesn’t heckle.  
> They remember your face.

He apprenticed under a middling bard—skilled, obscure, and very good at staying alive—where Rhett learned:

- satire that doesn’t quite cross the line  
- mimicry of noble speech and posture  
- how far you can push before someone reaches for steel  

Rhett pushed further than his teacher ever did.

And got away with it.

Until now.

---

## The Incident

Rhett authored and performed *The Marshal’s Table*, a satirical ballad about power, proximity, and perception around [[Beringer]].

His intent—if you believe him—was to highlight:

- how people gather around power  
- how rumor reshapes reputation  
- how perception becomes truth  

What people heard was simpler:

- the Marshal is indulgent  
- Dana is compromised  
- the court is exactly as corrupt as they suspected  

The song spread fast.

Too fast.

And then the political winds shifted:

- [[Dana Sylvanranth]] takes Blackvale  
- [[Beringer]] consolidates authority  
- [[Fayge Danon]] is in the room  

Rhett reads the room.

And realizes:

> This isn’t applause anymore.  
> This is a rope.

---

## The Move

Rhett does not hide.

He requests audience.

Public enough to be seen.  
Private enough to survive.

He:

- burns the last written copy in his possession  
- admits authorship and profit  
- refuses to hide behind bardic privilege  
- offers his services  

Then he does the thing that saves him:

He doesn’t retract the song.

He replaces it.

> **“The Marshal’s Burden”**

Same structure.  
Different meaning.

---

## Narrative Method

Rhett doesn’t fight stories head-on.

He **outcompetes them**.

His approach:

- mirror the original so people recognize it  
- shift the meaning without denying the words  
- let the audience “correct” themselves  
- seed the new version until it feels like the truth  

He understands something most don’t:

> People don’t like being told they were wrong.  
> They love deciding it themselves.

---

## Personality

Controlled. Observant. Never idle.

Rhett is always tracking:

- who’s watching  
- who’s reacting  
- who’s about to become a problem  

He jokes, but rarely wastes words.

He apologizes, but never without purpose.

He is not humble.

He is **practical**.
