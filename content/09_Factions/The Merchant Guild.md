# Grand Unified Mercantile, Logistical, Contractual, Arbitration, and Standardization Consortium of Accountable Trade Interests

> [!quote] Sorek Redmarch
> “Long-lived merchant houses are like old wells. They look respectable from the road, they’ve got stonework and family names carved into the side, and if you lean over too far you find out something’s been dead down there for years. The rich call it tradition. I call it smell.”

![[Merchants Guild.png]]

The **Grand Unified Mercantile, Logistical, Contractual, Arbitration, and Standardization Consortium of Accountable Trade Interests** is the kingdom’s largest organized mercantile body, though almost no one uses its full name unless they are reading from a charter, threatening litigation, or trying to win a bet while drunk.

Most people call it **the Merchant Guild**, **the Guild**, or **the Consortium**.

The full acronym, **G.U.M.L.C.A.S.C.A.T.I.**, is technically correct and morally punishable.

In theory, the Consortium exists to regularize trade, enforce fair weights, arbitrate disputes, maintain letters of credit, secure caravan routes, and prevent commerce from collapsing into armed robbery with receipts.

In practice, it is a vast, old, wealthy, self-protective institution built out of ledgers, favors, bonded warehouses, polite threats, and the ancient mercantile belief that anything can be made respectable if witnessed by three clerks and sealed in red wax.

It is useful.

It is powerful.

It is necessary.

It is also exactly the sort of organization that insists it knows nothing about the bodies under the counting house, while somehow charging storage fees on the shovels.

## Public Function

The Consortium provides structure to trade across much of Gorundia and beyond. Its recognized branches handle arbitration, credit, freight, insurance, weights, measures, toll agreements, caravan contracts, warehouse bonds, and trade disputes between merchants, noble houses, temples, and civic authorities.

A guild seal on a contract means something.

A guild letter of credit can open doors across half the kingdom.

A guild blacklist can ruin a small trader before breakfast.

The Consortium’s public services include:

- Trade arbitration
- Debt registration
- Caravan bonding
- Warehouse certification
- Merchant credentials
- Letters of credit
- Standard weights and measures
- Contract enforcement
- Freight and portage agreements
- Toll and road-use documentation
- Insurance against loss, theft, fire, banditry, war, divine displeasure, and other common expenses of civilization

At its best, the Consortium keeps trade moving and prevents every toll bridge, mule train, grain house, and river dock from becoming its own private kingdom.

At its worst, it becomes a cartel with a clerk’s smile.

Most days, it is both.

## Common Reputation

The Consortium is viewed differently depending on who is being asked and whether they currently owe it money.

Merchants call it necessary.

Nobles call it useful when obedient.

Temples call it tolerable when watched.

Peasants call it another way rich men turn hunger into columns.

Adventurers call it the place where a payment becomes a fee, the fee becomes a surcharge, the surcharge becomes an administrative hold, and everyone acts surprised when someone kicks over a desk.

In Bard’s Gate, the Consortium’s reputation is far worse.

There, its name is tied to cult infiltration, burned debt records, half-ogre “attendants,” murdered enforcers, ruined branches, and the unforgettable image of a high-ranking guild representative hanging outside a palisade in a canvas bag.

The locals have long memories.

The guild has longer ledgers.

Neither has helped.

## Structure

The Consortium is divided into regional offices, affiliated trade houses, specialist committees, bonded partners, and recognized outside contractors.

Typical offices include:

### Factor-General

The senior regional officer of a branch. A Factor-General oversees policy, major contracts, relationships with local rulers, and the general task of pretending all problems are temporary irregularities.

### Ledger-Master

Keeper of debts, bonds, letters of credit, sealed obligations, inheritance claims, mortgage instruments, and the sort of paperwork that lets a man rob your grandchildren without ever raising his voice.

### Master of Weights

Responsible for scales, measures, ingots, grain standards, cloth lengths, cargo rates, and arguments over whether a merchant’s thumb was resting on the balance by accident.

### Contract Arbiter

An officer charged with resolving commercial disputes between guild members or recognized outside parties.

Often very calm.

Rarely reassuring.

### Bonded Warehouse Keeper

Maintains guild-protected warehouses and sealed storage. A valuable post, because goods in a bonded warehouse are often worth more than the men guarding them.

### Caravan Assessor

Evaluates freight risk, escort requirements, road conditions, expected losses, insurance rates, and whether a given route is “unsafe” or merely “profitable for bandits.”

### Guild Advocate

A legal specialist in everything but official temple or crown title.

The kind of person who can say “with respect” and make it sound like a knife entering slowly.

### Reputation Clerk

Maintains blacklists, trust marks, warning notices, informal risk lists, and private advisories about debtors, cheats, dangerous nobles, unstable knights, predatory lenders, and adventuring parties who should not be cornered unless the building is already empty.

## Bard’s Gate Branch

The Bard’s Gate branch was once a major frontier office of the Consortium.

Its location made it valuable. Bard’s Gate sat far enough from the capital to avoid constant scrutiny, close enough to trade routes to matter, and dirty enough that money could enter without needing to explain where it had been.

The branch handled:

- Frontier loans
- Caravan contracts
- Noble credit
- Warehouse bonds
- Long-distance letters of credit
- Underworld laundering
- Speculative land purchases
- Private debt registration
- Trade insurance
- Quiet political favors
- The usual civic rot with better handwriting

It also became deeply corrupt.

That corruption was not merely financial. It was spiritual, criminal, and institutional. Elements of the branch became compromised by an [[Urdlen]] cult, which used guild habits of secrecy, debt, leverage, silence, and buried obligation as perfect cover.

By the time the rot was exposed, the Bard’s Gate branch had become something worse than a corrupt office.

It was a counting house built over a burrow.

## Urdlen Corruption

The Urdlen cult did not need to invent the guild’s sins.

It merely fed them.

The branch already depended on private ledgers, sealed agreements, discreet payments, leverage over debtors, and the careful management of secrets. Urdlen’s servants turned those habits toward darker ends.

Known or suspected signs of the corruption included:

- False warehouse receipts
- Missing debts
- Altered guild seals
- Active accounts for dead men
- Protection payments
- Smuggling contracts
- Noble debt concealment
- Cult-favored loans
- Vanishing witnesses
- Irregular payments to “security contractors”
- Merchant houses shielded from ordinary scrutiny
- Records that did not match goods, money, or corpses

The cult appears to have used the guild’s structure as both shield and weapon. A name in the wrong ledger could become pressure. A debt could become obedience. A shipment could carry more than grain, cloth, or iron.

The branch’s destruction was violent and humiliating.

The official language calls it “the Bard’s Gate Commercial Collapse.”

No one local calls it that.

Locals call it the time the rat-god filth finally got scraped out from under the floorboards.

## Theron Sylvanranth and the Burning of the Crown Debt Records

During the chaos after the Bard’s Gate branch was exposed and broken, [[Theron Sylvanranth]] acted.

A large body of guild-held royal debt records, crown-backed bonds, and related financial instruments was burned.

This was not an accident.

Theron saw an opportunity and took it.

The ruined branch had been compromised. Its ledgers were suspect. Its officers were dead, missing, corrupt, intimidated, or very suddenly devoted to rural living. The Crown’s debt records, in guild hands, represented leverage over the kingdom at precisely the moment the guild’s own legitimacy had rotted out beneath it.

So Theron burned them.

The results were immediate and ugly:

- Some debts became unenforceable.
- Some creditors lost lawful proof of claim.
- Some noble houses quietly benefited.
- Some honest merchants were financially harmed.
- Some criminals lost leverage.
- Some royal obligations became politically impossible to discuss.
- Some guild officers learned that paper burns faster than institutional dignity.

The Consortium never forgave him.

It also never found a clean way to condemn him.

To condemn Theron’s act fully, the guild must admit exactly what those records were, why they mattered, and how a cult-compromised branch came to possess so much leverage over the Crown.

This has left the Consortium in the miserable position of being both victim and suspect.

It hates this.

Naturally, everyone else finds it educational.

## The Chainers

The **Chainers** are a slaver guild that operates openly in the [[Dominion]] and covertly inside Gorundia.

In the Dominion, they are an accepted institution: brokers of captives, bonded laborers, engineered servants, “disciplinary stock,” and other categories of personhood that civilized men invent when they want slavery to look like administration.

In Gorundia, their presence is illegal, semi-hidden, deniable, and persistent.

They operate through:

- Middle-men
- False labor brokers
- Caravan factors
- Private guards
- Debtor-transfer schemes
- Servant-leasing contracts
- Smuggling houses
- Corrupt warehouse offices
- Offshore or cross-border entities
- Men with clean gloves and no names on anything important

The Consortium does not officially deal with the Chainers.

This is one of those sentences whose shape matters more than its truth.

Most Consortium officers have never knowingly signed a contract with a slaver guild. Most do not know the names of the men behind the leasing papers. Most do not care to know. Most were not paid to ask, and in mercantile circles that is often mistaken for innocence.

A common guild defense is:

“We leased labor through recognized intermediaries.”

Which means:

“We paid the man who paid the man who paid the man who owned the cage.”

## The Half-Ogre Secret

Among powerful merchants in Bard’s Gate, half-ogre attendants became a near-constant presence.

They stood behind important factors during negotiations. They carried locked chests. They guarded warehouses. They watched doorways. They flanked counting tables. They accompanied caravans and private delegations.

They were enormous.

They were calm.

They were vacant.

That vacancy was the point.

Unlike ordinary hired muscle, these half-ogres did not swagger, boast, drink, leer, threaten, or bargain. They stood still. They watched without seeming to watch. They waited without impatience. They obeyed without visible thought.

To most observers, they appeared slow, dull, or broken.

To those who knew better, they were weapons.

At least some of these half-ogres were bred, cloned, conditioned, or otherwise produced through methods connected to [[Kursk]] and Dominion experimentation.

The exact process remains uncertain. It may have involved alchemy, flesh-craft, forced breeding, arcane cloning, Dominion biological methods, or some combination of all four, because apparently one atrocity was not efficient enough.

The Chainers served as middle-men in the supply.

The Consortium’s official position is that it did not know the truth of their origin.

In many cases, this may even be true.

The more damning truth is that most involved parties did not want to know.

## Official Position on Half-Ogre Attendants

The Consortium recognizes that some affiliated merchants, private trade houses, and regional factors have historically employed or leased half-ogre attendants through outside labor brokers.

The Consortium denies:

- Maintaining breeding facilities
- Ordering cloning procedures
- Sanctioning Dominion-derived experimentation
- Owning half-ogre persons
- Authorizing the use of Chainer slave stock
- Knowing that any leased attendants were produced through unlawful methods
- Participating in magical, alchemical, or biological conditioning

This denial is carefully worded.

It does not deny that merchants used them.

It does not deny that guild-associated men knew where to lease them.

It does not deny that Chainer middle-men were involved.

It does not deny that payments flowed.

It does not deny that no one asked the right questions.

A clean conscience is easier to maintain when all the paperwork uses the word “labor.”

## How the Half-Ogres Were Used

The half-ogre attendants served several purposes at once.

They were guards, porters, symbols, threats, and insurance policies.

A merchant with a half-ogre behind him did not need to say he could have a man beaten. The room already understood.

They were especially useful because they seemed calm. An ordinary thug changes the temperature of a meeting. He brags, fidgets, glares, spits, scratches, or tries to impress someone.

The half-ogres did not.

They stood there like furniture that could tear your arms off.

This made them fashionable among certain Bard’s Gate merchants. A successful factor with a half-ogre attendant looked serious. Important. Protected. Connected.

A very successful factor had one who did not blink.

The problem, now increasingly whispered, is that these attendants may not have been free in any meaningful sense.

Some were likely victims.

Some were weapons.

Some were both.

That distinction is where the law begins to sweat.

## Triggers and Weaponization

The most disturbing rumors concern the way some half-ogres could be activated.

Certain handlers allegedly used command phrases, gestures, whistles, hand signs, or conditioned cues to shift the attendants from passive obedience into sudden violence.

Witnesses describe the change as immediate.

One moment, the half-ogre is vacant and still.

The next, he is breaking bones.

This made them ideal in guild and underworld settings. They could stand through hours of dull negotiation without incident, then become lethal when directed.

The existence of such trigger conditioning has not been formally proven in open court.

This is largely because the people who could prove it tend to be dead, missing, protected, terrified, guilty, or very interested in the phrase “I was only a purchasing agent.”

## The Fort Stormarrow Incident

The Consortium’s relationship with [[Baroness Braga Kharne]] was permanently damaged by the incident involving **Tibnock Tinkerspan**, a major gnomish merchant tied to the eastern guild networks.

Tibnock was at Fort Stormarrow with an entourage, discussing contracts, trade routes, and supply arrangements with Baron Kendel. He was important enough that his death could have triggered guild retaliation and commercial pressure against a wide region, perhaps even the Crown.

Then Tibnock made the sort of mistake that proves intelligence and wisdom are different ledgers.

He insulted [[Baroness Braga Kharne]].

He did not recognize her. She was not armored, not formally dressed, and not announced with noble ceremony. To him, she appeared to be a common woman interrupting important business.

He reportedly told her not to interrupt her betters again and threatened to have his manservant spank her, throw her over the wall, or treat her as the soldiers might.

This was not merely rude.

It was suicidal in a waistcoat.

Braga attacked him immediately.

No proclamation.

No challenge.

No duel.

Just her hands and his regret arriving at roughly the same speed.

Tibnock attempted to defend himself, reportedly swinging a bag at her. Whether it contained coin, tools, ledgers, or the last scraps of his judgment remains unknown.

Then his half-ogre attendant intervened.

Braga killed him.

The details vary by witness. Some say dagger. Some say chair leg. Some say butter knife. Some say all three, which is unlikely but emotionally persuasive.

What matters is that a half-ogre, one of the guild’s calm, vacant, leased giants, tried to pull Braga off Tibnock.

And Braga put him down.

Afterward, Tibnock was beaten, bloodied, and barely conscious.

Braga had him stuffed into a canvas bag and hung outside the palisade as punishment.

Not hanged by the neck.

Hung in a bag.

A distinction with legal importance, moral ambiguity, and enormous comic value.

## Kathreine Kendel’s Warning

Baron Kendel understood immediately that the situation could become disastrous. Tibnock was too important to die casually and too offensive to deserve easy mercy.

Kathreine Kendel was sent to warn [[Beringer]] and [[Orin Kharne]].

Her account made clear the essential danger: if Tibnock died from injury or exposure, the eastern guilds might retaliate. Trade pressure, credit restrictions, shipping complications, caravan slowdowns, and political complaints could all follow.

The problem was not whether Tibnock had earned the beating.

By most accounts, he had.

The problem was that dead merchants have friends, contracts, and heirs with lawyers.

Kathreine’s warning helped turn a private act of Kharne discipline into a political crisis.

It also spread the story, because no one in Gorundia can resist repeating a tale where a pompous gnome insults the wrong woman and ends the day hanging from a wall like laundry with internal bleeding.

## The Guild’s Internal Version

Inside guild circles, the story mutated almost immediately.

The clean version says that Master Tinkerspan was involved in an unfortunate misunderstanding with a noblewoman of martial temperament.

The less clean version says he mouthed off to Lady Braga, got beaten nearly flat, watched his half-ogre killed, and was hung outside the fort in a sack.

The tavern version says Braga killed the half-ogre with a chair leg and a butter knife, snapped him like a breadstick, and had to be pulled off Tibnock by several grown men while the gnome’s beard turned white from fear.

The guild version depends heavily on who is speaking, who is listening, and whether Tibnock is in the room.

Among junior guildsmen, clerks, and caravan agents, the rumors became elaborate:

- Tibnock now refuses to negotiate with anyone taller than a dwarf.
- Tibnock’s beard went white overnight.
- Tibnock eats only soup.
- Tibnock cannot hear the word “bag” without sweating.
- Tibnock gave away concessions because his brains were scrambled.
- Tibnock soiled himself during negotiations.
- Tibnock now checks every fort wall for hooks before entering.
- Tibnock keeps a clause in all contracts forbidding “sack-based restraint.”
- Tibnock claimed the half-ogre was merely “overenthusiastic staff.”
- Tibnock tried to describe Braga as “unreasonably vigorous.”

Most of these are probably false.

Probably.

## The King’s Deal

The strangest result of the incident is that it did not become the commercial disaster Baron Kendel feared.

While Tibnock was recovering, the King cut a deal.

Against expectation, the agreement worked out to the guild’s benefit. The Consortium gained improved access, adjusted obligations, favorable movement terms, or other concessions depending on whose summary is believed.

The exact terms remain buried under formal language, royal discretion, and guild self-congratulation.

What matters is that the guild did not lose.

It may even have gained.

This has created a second layer of rumor. Many guild members believe Tibnock, shaken by the experience, conceded too much out of fear. Others argue that the Crown, seeking stability, offered favorable terms to avoid a broader trade conflict. Some think both are true.

The public story is absurdly neat:

Tibnock insulted Braga.

Braga beat him senseless.

The King made a deal.

The guild profited.

This has left many Consortium officers unsure whether to classify the incident as a catastrophe, a humiliation, or a successful negotiation with unusual bruising.

## The Kharne View

House Kharne has little sympathy for Tibnock.

The common Kharne interpretation is simple:

He insulted Braga.

He threatened her with sexual humiliation.

He relied on rank, wealth, and a half-ogre enforcer.

His enforcer stepped in.

The enforcer died.

Tibnock lived.

Therefore, mercy was shown.

This is not a legal argument so much as a stone wall with words painted on it.

Braga’s defenders also point out that the half-ogre’s presence was not innocent. If the creature was one of the leased, conditioned, Chainer-supplied half-ogres, then Tibnock did not merely bring a bodyguard.

He brought a weapon and expected no one to object.

That argument has teeth.

So did Braga, apparently.

## Political Consequences

The Fort Stormarrow incident changed how the Consortium behaves around the party and its allies.

The guild learned several lessons:

- Baroness Braga Kharne cannot be bullied.
- Half-ogre attendants are not a guarantee of safety.
- Insulting unrecognized noblewomen is expensive.
- The Crown may turn disaster into leverage.
- The party’s circle converts commercial disputes into historical events.
- No one should bring Chainer-sourced muscle near people inclined to ask ethical questions with weapons drawn.

The Consortium now treats Braga, Orin, Beringer, and their immediate allies as a special class of risk.

Not because they are impossible to negotiate with.

Because they are possible to negotiate with, but only if approached like unexploded siege powder.

## Current Consortium Protocol Regarding Half-Ogre Attendants

After Fort Stormarrow, careful guild officers began revising their habits.

Known or suspected guidance includes:

- Do not bring half-ogre attendants into meetings with Baroness Braga Kharne.
- Do not bring half-ogre attendants into meetings with Orin Kharne.
- Do not bring half-ogre attendants into meetings with Beringer.
- Do not bring half-ogre attendants into meetings with temple investigators.
- Do not refer to half-ogres as stock.
- Do not refer to half-ogres as leased muscle in writing.
- Do not mention the Chainers.
- Do not admit knowledge of Dominion sourcing.
- Do not ask too many questions about old leasing arrangements.
- Do not allow unknown parties to speak trigger phrases near them.
- Do not assume vacant means harmless.
- Do not assume obedience means consent.
- Do not assume Braga will leave enough of the next one for questioning.

The smartest merchants have begun replacing half-ogre attendants with ordinary guards.

The proudest continue the practice.

The richest pretend theirs are different.

That sentence has buried more scandals than plague.

## The Party’s Reputation

The party scares the hell out of the Consortium.

The guild is accustomed to danger. Bandits, debtors, nobles, smugglers, corrupt clerks, toll lords, rival houses, and occasional monster attacks are ordinary business risks.

The party is different.

From the guild’s perspective, these people do not resolve problems.

They detonate them.

They enter a room, ask questions, survive assassins, expose cults, topple nobles, break infernal arrangements, uncover bloodlines, burn leverage, and leave behind a new political order that requires every surviving clerk to revise the filing system.

The Consortium has seen enough to understand the pattern.

A normal lord threatens tariffs.

A normal knight threatens duels.

A normal priest threatens judgment.

The party threatens context.

That is much worse.

## Exceptional Hazard Classification

Some private guild risk lists now mark the party and close associates not as ordinary persons, creditors, clients, or hostile actors, but as:

**Exceptional Hazard: Political, Martial, Theological, Commercial, and Accounting**

This classification is unofficial.

It is also accurate.

Recommended handling includes:

- Be polite.
- Be truthful where possible.
- Be very careful where not possible.
- Do not threaten them.
- Do not mention disputed debt without perfect documents.
- Do not rely on technicalities.
- Do not conceal cult ties.
- Do not bring Chainer assets.
- Do not bring half-ogre attendants.
- Do not use the phrase “private commercial matter.”
- Do not say “you have no authority here.”
- Do not assume the wall behind you will remain structurally relevant.

## Internal Factions

The post-Bard’s Gate Consortium is divided.

### The Clean Ledgers

Reformers who believe the Bard’s Gate disaster proved the guild must become cleaner, stricter, and more transparent. They support audits, spiritual screening, duplicate records, and a sharp break from Chainer middle-men.

They are irritating.

They are also right more often than anyone wants to admit.

### The Old Hands

Pragmatists who believe corruption is not eliminated, only managed. They argue that trade requires discretion, flexibility, and arrangements not fit for public sermon.

These are the men who can explain why a bribe is only a bribe when poorly documented.

### The Wounded Creditors

Merchants and financiers harmed by Theron’s burning of the Crown debt records.

Some were innocent.

Some were parasites.

All are angry.

### The Quiet Complicit

Those who did not breed, clone, condition, or enslave anyone personally, but knew where the half-ogres came from in the broadest possible sense and decided “broadest possible sense” was enough distance for a clean night’s sleep.

This faction is large.

It is also the reason the scandal has legs.

### The Fearful Majority

Most members simply want trade to continue without cults, bags, burned ledgers, temple inquisitions, Chainer exposure, or Baroness Kharne asking where the next half-ogre came from.

Their cowardice may be the guild’s healthiest instinct.

## Adventure Use

The Consortium is most useful when played as compromised, frightened, necessary, and dangerous rather than simply villainous.

Possible uses include:

- A reformist guild officer asks for help investigating Chainer-linked leasing contracts.
- A half-ogre attendant begins remembering his conditioning.
- A Chainer middle-man is found murdered before he can testify.
- A surviving Urdlen-tainted ledger contains names tied to half-ogre purchases.
- A merchant insists he leased “labor” lawfully and knew nothing else.
- A noble house used Chainer-supplied half-ogres as household guards.
- A guild faction tries to blame all half-ogre activity on the ruined Bard’s Gate branch.
- Tibnock’s favorable deal with the King becomes a point of internal resentment.
- The party discovers that some half-ogres have dormant command triggers.
- Braga learns where one of the leasing agents lives.
- A guild clerk quietly kept duplicate records because blackmail is the one language institutions never forget.
- The Chainers attempt to silence anyone connecting them to Kursk-derived material.

## Roleplaying the Consortium

Consortium officers should not be played as fools.

They are cautious, educated, wealthy, and institutionally trained to survive scandal by narrowing definitions until sin becomes a clerical ambiguity.

They should be:

- Polite
- Controlled
- Precise
- Defensive
- Nervous around the party
- Careful with names
- Skilled at omission
- Extremely interested in paperwork
- Reluctant to say “slave”
- Reluctant to say “clone”
- Reluctant to say “Dominion”
- Reluctant to be alone with Braga

They should not openly threaten powerful local figures unless desperate or catastrophically misinformed.

They should not admit knowledge they can plausibly deny.

They should absolutely try to define guilt as something that happens only when recorded in the correct column.

Translation: several people deserved what happened, but please stop asking which ones.

> “The destruction of the Crown debt records remains a matter of grave institutional concern.”

Translation: Theron Sylvanranth kicked us in the soul and set the receipt on fire.

> “The attendant’s legal status remains under review.”

Translation: please do not ask whether the giant was a person or inventory.

> “We contracted through recognized labor intermediaries.”

Translation: we paid men whose names were clean enough to write down.

> “We have no confirmed knowledge of Chainer involvement.”

Translation: confirmed is carrying a lot of weight and would like to sit down.

> “No recognized guild authority sanctioned cloning, breeding, or unlawful conditioning.”

Translation: every word in this sentence has been fitted for armor.

> “Master Tinkerspan’s injuries were regrettable.”

Translation: he said something so stupid even the lawyers stared at the floor.

> “The Baroness’s chosen method of restraint was irregular.”

Translation: there was a bag. We all know about the bag. Please stop saying bag.

Translation: Braga killed one, and now apparently this is everyone’s problem.

> “The royal agreement was ultimately favorable to all parties.”

Translation: somehow the man in the sack got us better terms, and nobody knows what to do with that.

## Current Status

The Consortium remains powerful.

It still controls money, contracts, caravans, credit, bonded warehouses, letters of passage, trade seals, and enough quiet influence to make a smaller man vanish beneath lawful inconvenience.

But in Bard’s Gate and the surrounding region, its swagger is wounded.

The Urdlen corruption ruined trust.

Theron’s fire destroyed leverage.

The Chainer connection threatens legitimacy.

The half-ogre secret may become a moral and legal disaster.

Tibnock’s humiliation made the guild a joke in every tavern with a working door.

And the party scares its officers badly enough that even the arrogant ones are learning the value of humility.

The Consortium is not broken.

That would be too easy.

It is bruised, embarrassed, guilty, useful, rich, cautious, and still very much alive.

Which makes it dangerous.

Old money does not become harmless because someone found blood on the ledger.

It hires a better clerk.
