# Vignette — Breakfast at the Blood and Sand

Morning at the Blood and Sand was quieter than night, but only in the way a forge is quieter than a battlefield.

The front room had been swept, mostly. Chairs were stacked, mostly. The floor had been scrubbed in the places where it was worth lying to yourself about cleanliness. A few lamps still burned low. Somewhere under the smell of old beer, ash, bread, and damp sawdust, breakfast was happening.

The crew sat around one of the long kitchen tables because Fenby insisted people ate better if they were trapped where he could see them.

Fenby Goodbarrel had made eggs with peppered onions, thick slices of fried bread, a pot of beans, sausages cut mean and crisp, and honeyed bread he kept pretending was not specifically for Druuna.

Druuna sat near him because Fenby had put her there with one hand and a glare, like a priestess being installed against her will.

Tinnet sat two places down, perched on the bench with her bowl close and both elbows tucked in. Everyone called her Tiny, because the Dregs had a talent for naming people after either a joke, a wound, or the last thing anyone should say to their face.

She ate fast, but not greedily.

Fast like someone still did not entirely trust breakfast to remain available.

Grenda noticed and nudged another slice of fried bread toward her.

Tiny hesitated.

Grenda gave her a look.

Tiny took it.

That was how arguments worked in the Blood and Sand. The winner was whoever could stare longer without making the moment soft.

“I am eating,” Druuna said.

Fenby did not look up from cutting sausages.

“You’re considering eating. That’s not the same trade.”

“I have food in my mouth.”

“Barely. A mouse would accuse you of caution.”

Druuna chewed more deliberately.

Fenby grunted, satisfied, and slid another piece of honeyed bread toward her.

“I don’t need another.”

“You need several things. I am solving the one in front of me.”

Across the table, Toots hummed into her mug.

Not loudly.

Not theatrically.

Just a little tune under her breath, soft and aimless and irritatingly pleased with itself.

Grenda noticed first, because Grenda noticed people even when she failed to notice onions turning into charcoal.

She leaned back with Brak settled against her hip and narrowed her eyes.

“That’s a good-mood hum.”

Toots stopped immediately.

“No, it isn’t.”

Caddoc Murn, who was eating like a man still suspicious that breakfast might be taken away by officers, grunted.

“That was absolutely a good-mood hum.”

Nesta Wray tore a heel of bread in half.

“That was a woman thinking about something and hoping nobody asked.”

Harl Coney lifted his cup.

“Then we should all respect her privacy.”

Everyone looked at him.

He shrugged.

“What? I can grow.”

“No,” said Fenby.

Scrub sat at the far end of the table, broad shoulders tucked in, hands wrapped around a chipped cup. He was half-orc, grown, quiet, and too still for anyone to mistake him for simple. He wore his plain work shirt like a disguise that had given up asking to be believed.

Everyone knew Scrub was more than he said.

Nobody knew exactly how much more.

Nobody asked.

The Blood and Sand had rules about secrets. You watched whether a person hurt yours. If they didn’t, you let them keep theirs. Civilization had been built on shakier foundations and worse tavern soup.

Scrub’s eyes flicked from Toots to Fenby to Tiny to Slab, missing nothing.

Slab sat near the door on a stool built to endure either him or a minor siege. He had a bowl in one hand and Brak’s wooden rattle in the other, because Brak had dropped it and Slab had picked it up without comment. Brak, being a baby, had accepted this tribute like a small wet monarch.

Slab looked at Toots.

“When is Sir Osric coming back with more stories?”

Toots stared at him.

The table went still in that delighted, predatory way breakfast tables do when blood is in the water.

“What?” Slab asked.

Toots’ ears darkened.

Caddoc leaned back.

“Sir Osric.”

Nesta nodded solemnly.

“Stories.”

Harl clicked his tongue.

“More stories. Important distinction.”

Tiny looked at Toots.

“Is he nice?”

The question was small, honest, and therefore immediately worse than teasing.

Toots opened her mouth.

Closed it.

“He’s a knight.”

Tiny considered that.

“That isn’t an answer.”

Grenda smiled into her cup.

“She has you there.”

Toots pointed at Tiny.

“You’re learning too much.”

Tiny looked down at her bowl, but she smiled.

“I work in a kitchen.”

“Kitchen don’t teach that.”

Fenby snorted.

“Mine does.”

“I will throw this mug through someone’s teeth,” Toots said.

Harl covered his mouth with one hand.

“My growth has ended again.”

Gravel, seated at the end of the table with one ledger open beside his plate and Brak’s spare cloth over his shoulder, looked up from his figures. He had been doing tavern sums, pit tallies, pay marks, and whatever else came to him because the Blood and Sand was apparently a tavern, fighting pit, halfblood hall, street office, and proof that sleep was for wealthier people.

He looked at Brak.

Then he looked at Grenda.

“Brak needs changing.”

Grenda frowned.

“No, he doesn’t.”

Gravel blinked at her.

Grenda shifted the baby, checked his face, then his little fists.

“He’s fine.”

“He is not fine. He is weaponized.”

“How do you know?”

Gravel stared.

“How do I know?”

“Yes.”

“I can smell it.” He pointed vaguely upward. “Hell, they can smell it uptown.”

Nesta coughed into her cup.

Caddoc shut his eyes.

Harl whispered, “The nobility has fallen.”

Grenda looked at Brak, then at Gravel, still not embarrassed. Not defensive. Just confused, as if Gravel had claimed to hear the color blue.

“He doesn’t smell like anything.”

Fenby stopped cutting sausage.

Only for a heartbeat.

But Scrub saw it.

So did Nesta.

So did Gravel, because Gravel had survived long enough to notice when a halfling cook went still near knives.

Grenda stood, hitching Brak against her hip.

“I’ll change him if you’re going to be dramatic.”

“I am not dramatic,” Gravel said.

“You once threatened a man with a chair leg because he kept touching Sella after she told him no.”

Gravel’s expression went flat.

“That wasn’t dramatic. That was policy.”

No one argued.

No one with working survival instincts argued with Gravel about Sella.

Grenda kissed Brak’s head and started toward the back stairs.

As she passed Toots, she said, “And you are humming.”

“I am not.”

“You were. Your face is trying to walk around without permission from the rest of you.”

“I will hum at your funeral.”

Grenda smiled without turning.

“Make it cheerful. I dislike waste.”

She disappeared down the hall toward their room, Brak babbling at her shoulder.

The second she was gone, Fenby straightened.

Not quickly.

Not loudly.

Worse.

Carefully.

“She didn’t smell the bread burning.”

The table turned toward him.

Fenby stared at nothing, one eye narrowed, hand still on the knife.

“She didn’t smell it. Last week. I thought she was ignoring it because she was arguing with Harl about onions.”

“I was right about the onions,” Harl said.

“You were not invited into the autopsy.”

Harl shut up.

Fenby’s mouth worked silently.

Then:

“She didn’t smell the difference between cinnamon, cumin, and smoked paprika.”

Caddoc’s face changed.

Nesta slowly lowered her bread.

Toots glanced toward the back hall.

Tiny looked from one adult to another, suddenly very still.

Druuna noticed that and shifted slightly, the way she did when some street-child fear stirred too close to the surface.

Fenby pointed at Gravel.

“Does Grenda have a sense of smell?”

Gravel looked offended by the question.

“Hey. I think she smells good.”

Fenby closed his eye.

“No, you fucking idiot. Can she smell?”

Gravel opened his mouth.

Closed it.

Opened it again.

“Sometimes.”

Fenby stared.

Gravel spread one hand.

“Everyone gets sweaty.”

Caddoc made a small choking sound.

Nesta covered her face.

Toots looked down at the table with the desperate focus of someone trying not to laugh at a bad time and failing spiritually.

Fenby ran both hands over his face.

At the far end, Tiny whispered, “Is that why she says the smoke is lying?”

Fenby stopped.

“What?”

Tiny’s shoulders came up a little, like she had not meant to speak.

Druuna’s hand moved under the table and gently touched the bench beside her, not quite touching Tiny. Permission without trapping her.

Tiny swallowed.

“When the bread gets too dark. She says the smoke is lying. Because the bread looks fine until it doesn’t.”

Fenby’s face did something complicated.

Caddoc looked away.

Nesta said nothing.

Gravel looked toward the hall where Grenda had gone, and for a second the ledger, the room, the whole rough machinery of the Blood and Sand left his face.

Then Harl, because some men are born carrying a shovel and looking for emotional graves to dig, muttered, “Maybe if little Saint Sunbeam here spent less time preaching charity into the soup, someone would have noticed.”

The table froze.

Druuna blinked.

It was not the worst thing anyone had ever said to her.

That was the problem.

She had a face for insults. A trained face. The soft, steady, merciful little mask she put on when people threw ugliness and expected her to either bleed or bless them for it.

Toots set her cup down.

Caddoc stopped eating.

Gravel’s head turned slowly.

Tiny went pale in the particular way half-orcs went pale, where the green-grey undertone seemed to ash over.

Scrub’s eyes moved to Harl.

Slab looked at Harl too.

Slab did not move.

That somehow made it worse.

Harl looked up and realized, much too late, that he had mistaken breakfast for safety.

Druuna opened her mouth.

Fenby beat her to it.

He pointed the sausage knife at Harl.

“You.”

Harl held up both hands.

“I didn’t mean—”

“No. You did. That’s why your mouth made the shape.”

Druuna said gently, “It is all right.”

Fenby did not look at her.

“It is not.”

She tried again.

“I am not harmed.”

“Not the point.”

Tiny stared at her bowl.

Fenby saw that too.

So did Scrub.

So did Toots.

There were pressure points in the Blood and Sand. Children. Food. Hands where they should not be. Men sniffing around young girls. Mocking someone too kind to hit back.

The crew did not always agree on theology.

They agreed on pressure points.

Harl shrank a little.

Fenby’s voice sharpened.

“You like eating breakfast here?”

“Yes.”

“You like having two hands?”

Harl nodded once.

“Then let me educate the first and preserve the second. Druuna sits here because I told her to sit here. She eats because Grenda and I make her eat. If she talks mercy over the soup, that’s because some of you need mercy and soup before your souls collapse from neglect.”

Druuna went faintly pink.

Fenby was not done.

“And if you call her Saint Sunbeam again in that voice, I will make you peel onions until your ancestors cry.”

Harl swallowed.

“That’s fair.”

“It was not a negotiation.”

Tiny looked at Fenby.

Druuna, because she was Druuna and therefore impossible, said softly, “Thank you.”

Fenby turned on her at once.

“Eat.”

“I am.”

“You stopped.”

“I was being defended.”

“You can be defended while chewing.”

Druuna took another bite.

Fenby nodded sharply.

“Good.”

The tension began to loosen.

Not vanish.

Loosen.

Then the back door opened.

Quietly.

Not like a guest.

Like a man who knew which hinge complained and how to avoid it.

Longjaw stepped in from the alley with his hood low and one shoulder damp from mist. His wrapped arm hung close to his side. The street-beggar shape was still on him: rounded back, slack mouth, harmless weight in the knees.

Then he shut the door behind him, straightened, and became himself.

Not fully.

Longjaw never came fully into a room anymore. Some part of him stayed outside with the gutters, the watchers, and whatever news he had not yet decided to spend.

Tiny saw him first.

“Longjaw!”

She was up before anyone could pretend she was not glad.

She crossed the kitchen fast, then stopped just short of throwing herself into him because she had learned there were rules about sore arms, pride, and who got touched in front of whom.

Longjaw solved it by bending slightly and bumping his forehead to hers.

“Tiny.”

Her whole face lit.

“Did you eat?”

Longjaw looked past her toward the table.

Fenby’s eye narrowed.

Longjaw sighed.

“No.”

Fenby pointed to the bench.

“Sit before I become religious.”

Druuna blinked.

“I think that is my area.”

“Not today.”

Gravel lifted his chin in greeting.

“Longjaw.”

“Boss.”

Toots gave him a two-finger wave.

“Still ugly?”

Longjaw touched his long jaw with his good hand.

“Improving daily. Your eyes are weak.”

“Your mirror is kind.”

Slab nodded.

“Back door.”

Longjaw nodded back.

“Back door.”

That seemed to satisfy Slab completely.

Nesta slid him a bowl without asking. Caddoc moved bread closer. Harl, still recovering from nearly being turned into a moral lesson with onion fumes, passed the sausages with unusual humility.

Longjaw looked at the table.

Then at Fenby’s knife.

Then at Druuna eating too deliberately.

Then at Tiny, who was still standing too close to him.

Then at Toots’ flushed ears.

Then at Gravel holding a ledger open upside down because he had stopped reading when the room turned.

Longjaw paused.

“Right,” he said. “What did I miss?”

Scrub answered before anyone else could.

“Grenda can’t smell. Toots is humming because of the knight. Harl insulted Druuna and survived by mercy and labor scarcity. Fenby is inventing kitchen policy. Brak committed an act of war. Gravel is still behind the facts.”

The room went silent.

Longjaw looked at Scrub.

Scrub looked back.

A beat passed.

Then Longjaw gave the smallest nod.

A clean little exchange.

Recognition.

Not of truth exactly.

Of method.

Longjaw sat down.

“Good breakfast, then.”

Toots wheezed.

Nesta laughed into her bread.

Caddoc slapped the table once.

Fenby glared at Scrub.

“You talk that well all the time?”

Scrub drank from his cup.

“No.”

Longjaw picked up his spoon.

“Smart.”

Tiny sat beside him, practically vibrating.

“Grenda can’t smell anything.”

Longjaw glanced toward the hall.

“Ah.”

Tiny leaned in.

“She didn’t know.”

Longjaw’s face shifted.

There it was again, that sharpness under the beggar act. The street had taught him to notice wounds by the shape people built around them.

“No,” he said. “Likely not.”

Fenby pointed at him.

“Do not become thoughtful before eating. It encourages poetry.”

Longjaw obediently took a spoonful.

Then another.

Druuna watched him with open relief.

“Your arm?”

Longjaw flexed his wrapped fingers once.

“Itches like a curse and aches when rain thinks about existing.”

“That is not unusual,” Druuna said.

“Good. I was afraid the gods had gotten personal.”

“They often do.”

Longjaw looked at her.

“Hm.”

Tiny leaned against the bench beside him, not quite touching, but close enough to show everyone exactly whose arrival mattered most to her.

Gravel noticed.

His expression hardened just slightly, not at Longjaw, but at the shape of the world that had made a girl that relieved to see the man who got her clear of Morna Coalbraid’s reach.

Longjaw saw Gravel notice.

Gravel saw Longjaw see him.

Neither man said anything.

Some debts did not improve when spoken aloud.

Fenby, however, believed silence was best used sparingly.

“We are discussing Grenda’s nose.”

Longjaw looked at him.

“That sounds dangerous.”

“It is governance.”

“Worse.”

Fenby jabbed his spoon toward the hearth.

“She doesn’t smell smoke, rot, sour milk, burnt bread, or Brak.”

Longjaw considered this.

“Brak I understand.”

Gravel said, “What does that mean?”

Longjaw kept eating.

“Means he respects her.”

Slab nodded.

“Gift.”

Gravel looked between them.

“No.”

Toots lost another piece of composure and had to cover it with coughing.

Druuna tried to hide her smile behind honeyed bread.

Harl, attempting to rejoin civilization, said, “We’re making rules. More bells.”

Longjaw looked at Tiny.

Tiny nodded importantly.

“More bells. So nobody fusses.”

“That so?”

Tiny turned to Fenby.

“Right?”

Fenby looked at her.

Then at Longjaw.

Then back at Tiny.

“Right.”

Tiny sat a little straighter.

That was the thing about the Blood and Sand. Half the rules were violent. Half were practical. Some, when nobody was looking too directly at them, were mercy in a dirty apron.

From the hallway came Grenda’s voice.

“Who’s crying?”

Every head turned.

Grenda came back in with Brak changed and freshly wrapped, looking between them all with immediate suspicion.

The room performed innocence badly.

Tragically.

Like amateurs.

Caddoc picked up his fork.

Nesta buttered bread that was already buttered.

Harl became fascinated by a stain on the table.

Toots started humming again by accident, then stopped, which helped no one.

Tiny leaned closer to Longjaw and whispered, “Watch.”

Longjaw whispered back, “I am.”

Scrub watched the whole disaster with the faintest suggestion of amusement buried somewhere behind his eyes.

Grenda stopped.

Then she saw Longjaw.

Her suspicion softened at once.

“Longjaw.”

“Grenda.”

“You eat yet?”

He looked down at the bowl in front of him.

“I have been captured.”

“Good.”

She handed Brak to Gravel, then looked at Fenby.

“What?”

Fenby pointed at the bench.

“Sit.”

Grenda’s eyes narrowed.

“That was a command voice.”

“It was a breakfast voice.”

“That’s worse.”

“Sit anyway.”

Grenda sat slowly.

Fenby stood across from her, arms folded over his round belly, eye rag crooked, expression severe enough to curdle milk from across the room.

Then he glanced at Druuna.

Druuna lifted her eyebrows.

Plainly. Kindly.

Fenby scowled at her like she had personally invented moral accountability.

Then he looked back at Grenda.

“Can you smell anything?”

Grenda blinked.

“What?”

“Anything,” Fenby said. “Smoke. Sour milk. Rotten meat. Bread burning. Brak declaring war on the room.”

Grenda stared at him.

Then at Gravel.

Gravel held Brak a little higher, as if presenting evidence.

“I told you,” he said. “They could smell him uptown.”

Grenda looked back at Fenby.

“I can smell some things.”

“What things?”

She paused.

“Strong things.”

“Name one.”

Grenda thought about it.

The room waited.

Outside, a cart rattled past. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe clicked. Brak made a small satisfied noise, like a man proud of his work.

Grenda’s brow furrowed.

“Smoke, maybe.”

Fenby did not move.

Grenda’s eyes narrowed.

“If I see it.”

Fenby nodded once.

“There it is.”

Her expression shifted. Not embarrassed. Not wounded.

Annoyed.

At the facts.

At the room.

At possibly herself, though only because the world had been smug and unclear.

“So what?”

That was Grenda.

Clean as a thrown stone.

So what?

Fenby’s face changed again, and this time even he could not hide all of it.

“So nothing about you is wrong.”

Grenda went very still.

Fenby continued before the room could get sentimental and ruin his morning.

“You’re missing a bell. That’s all. Other folk hear it ringing and you don’t. So we hang more bells.”

Grenda looked at him for a long moment.

Then at Toots.

Then Gravel.

Then Scrub.

Then Longjaw.

Longjaw gave one small nod.

Scrub did the same.

Different men. Same message.

Not pity.

Confirmation.

Grenda looked back at Fenby.

“What kind of bells?”

Fenby lifted one finger.

“You don’t work the hearth alone.”

Her mouth opened.

He pointed harder.

“Not because you’re helpless. Because smoke is sneaky and kitchens are flammable. Second, someone checks bread when you’re on other work. Third, spices get better marks. Fourth, if Gravel says the baby has turned into a public hazard, believe him.”

Gravel nodded solemnly.

“Public hazard.”

Grenda looked at Brak.

Brak smiled at her.

Traitor.

Tiny raised her hand.

Everyone looked at her.

She lowered it halfway.

“Can I watch the bread?”

Fenby stared at her.

Tiny’s chin lifted in immediate defense.

“I already watch it.”

Grenda looked at her.

“You do?”

Tiny shrugged one shoulder.

“When you say the smoke is lying.”

The room softened and tried not to show it, because the Blood and Sand was full of cowards about tenderness.

Grenda’s face changed.

Just a little.

“Oh.”

Tiny looked down quickly.

Longjaw nudged her bowl closer to her.

She ate.

Fenby cleared his throat.

“You may watch the bread.”

Tiny looked up.

“But you tell me if it darkens. You don’t rescue it alone, you don’t move the big pans alone, and you don’t decide you’re grown because someone let you observe carbohydrates.”

Tiny nodded solemnly.

“Yes, Uncle Fenby.”

Fenby went still.

Everyone else became very interested in surviving the next breath.

Fenby looked offended.

Then pleased.

Then offended that he was pleased.

“Don’t weaponize manners at me.”

Tiny smiled into her bowl.

Harl said, “Fifth, no cloves in soup unless Fenby signs a writ.”

Fenby pointed without looking.

“You are not helping.”

Grenda sat back.

Her jaw worked.

Then she said, “I thought everyone was being dramatic about spoiled milk.”

Nesta winced.

“Sometimes we were.”

Caddoc shrugged.

“Sometimes the milk was declaring independence.”

Toots leaned an elbow on the table.

“You really can’t smell Brak?”

Grenda shook her head.

“No.”

Slab looked impressed.

“Gift.”

Longjaw nodded.

“Situational blessing.”

Gravel stared at both of them.

“It is not a gift.”

Slab shrugged.

“Sometimes.”

Longjaw lifted his spoon.

“Depends where you stand.”

That did it.

The laugh started with Toots, sharp and helpless. Nesta followed. Harl tried to hide his behind his cup and failed. Caddoc chuckled into his plate. Even Druuna smiled, warm and relieved.

Grenda looked around at them.

Then down at Brak.

Then at Gravel.

“You’ve been smelling all of that?”

“All of what?”

“All the baby things.”

Gravel’s face went grave.

“Yes.”

Grenda considered him with new respect.

“Living with me is harder than I thought.”

Gravel pointed at her.

“Thank you.”

Fenby muttered, “Finally, justice.”

Nesta looked into her cup.

Caddoc suddenly became very interested in his sausage.

Toots pressed her lips together so hard they nearly vanished.

Because everyone at that table knew.

Everyone except Gravel, obviously.

Grenda was it.

Not a girl. Not a convenience. Not a warm bed and shared rooms and a baby and the shape of a life that had somehow assembled itself around him while he was busy pretending he was still deciding things.

Gravel was trapped.

And worse, the poor bastard liked the trap.

He just hadn’t noticed the door had closed.

Scrub saw it. Of course Scrub saw it. Scrub saw everything and volunteered nothing, like a man who had made an entire religion out of minding his own business.

Longjaw saw it in less than a minute, which was frankly rude.

Fenby saw it and judged Gravel for being late to his own execution.

Slab saw it, but Slab thought most good things were simple and therefore did not require comment.

Toots saw it and, for once, did not tease.

Grenda probably saw more of it than she let on.

Gravel, meanwhile, sat there holding Brak like a man who believed the weather had simply changed indoors.

Grenda turned back to Fenby.

“And the cinnamon?”

Fenby’s expression darkened.

“We will speak of the cinnamon when my heart is stronger.”

“I thought it was cumin.”

“I know.”

“They look alike.”

“They do not.”

“They do to me.”

Fenby opened his mouth.

Closed it.

Druuna took a very deliberate bite of honeyed bread.

Fenby saw her and glared.

She smiled with her mouth full.

Grenda watched that exchange, then looked back at the table.

“So that’s it? More bells?”

Fenby nodded.

“More bells.”

“No fussing?”

“No fussing.”

Nesta coughed.

Fenby shot her a look.

“No unapproved fussing.”

Grenda seemed to accept that as the closest truth available.

She reached for a sausage.

Fenby slapped her hand with the back of his knife.

“Not that one. That’s Druuna’s.”

Druuna looked betrayed.

“I cannot eat all this.”

“You can attempt holiness through effort.”

Tiny quietly slid half of her own fried bread toward Longjaw.

Longjaw slid it back without looking.

Tiny scowled.

He murmured, “Eat.”

“I am.”

“More.”

She looked at him.

He looked at Fenby.

Fenby looked at Druuna.

Druuna looked at Tiny.

Tiny picked up the bread and ate with the bitter dignity of the overruled.

Toots started humming again.

This time, nobody teased her.

For almost three breaths.

Then Slab said, “So. Osric?”

Toots threw a crust at his head.

Slab caught it, ate it, and looked pleased.

Longjaw looked from Slab to Toots.

“Knight?”

“No,” Toots said.

Everyone stared at her.

She scowled.

“I mean yes, he is a knight. No, you don’t get to make a thing.”

Longjaw nodded.

“Too late?”

Scrub said, “Too late.”

Longjaw accepted this with the grim wisdom of a man who had arrived mid-disaster and found the map anyway.

Fenby pointed toward the stove.

“Civilization survives another morning.”

Scrub lifted his cup.

“Barely.”

Longjaw lifted his bowl.

“Generous.”

Everyone looked at them.

They drank.

Nobody asked.

And breakfast went on.
