# The Stupid Right Things

The Admiral’s Vice looked best after closing.

The lamps had been turned low, leaving the brass fixtures to glow like banked embers over dark wood, polished rails, green felt, cut glass, and the kind of careful shadows that made rich people feel safer while they lied to each other.

The place belonged up here, in the fancy part of Bard’s Gate. It had clean windows, thick doors, expensive locks, and customers who could afford to lose money while pretending they had meant to.

downtown was different.

downtown was the Dregs.

downtown was the Blood and Sand, where sawdust served as floor covering, cleaning solution, and occasionally triage. The Blood and Sand did not glow after closing. It brooded. It sweated. It waited for someone to get stupid enough to reopen the doors.

Thalrik Vaultforge stood behind the main bar with the Vice’s night’s take open in front of him.

Coins in stacks. Notes weighted flat. Markers sorted by name, house, and how annoying they were likely to become if pressed.

He was working through the numbers with the expression of a man who loved profit, hated arithmetic, and suspected the gods had invented both to keep dwarves from getting too cheerful.

The staff door opened after two careful knocks and one tired pause.

Gravel came in looking like someone had dragged him uphill by the collar, made him apologize to the street, and then sent him into a nice room so the contrast could hurt.

He had a wrapped bundle of books tucked under one arm.

That was rarely a good sign.

“Sorry I missed the party, boss.”

Thalrik did not look up.

“There was no party.”

“There’s always a party in this place. Sometimes with better shoes.”

Thalrik made another mark in the ledger.

Gravel rubbed the back of his neck. “I did one of those stupid right things.”

That got Thalrik to glance up.

Gravel gave him a tired little smile.

“You know. Instead of the smart wrong thing.”

“That sounds expensive.”

“Usually is.”

Thalrik set down the pen.

Gravel came farther in, then stopped beside one of the brass railings. He looked too tired to stand, too stubborn to sit, and too used to bad floors to trust a good one.

“I gave Toots the night off.”

“I know.”

Gravel paused.

Thalrik turned one page of the ledger with a careful fingertip.

“She came here.”

“She did?”

“She did.”

Gravel looked around the Admiral’s Vice as if he expected to find her hiding behind velvet curtains, offended by upholstery.

“How’d that go?”

Thalrik considered the question.

There were several answers. Most were too informative. A few were too useful. One or two were nearly tender, and therefore clearly unfit for speech in front of witnesses.

“She had a good time.”

Gravel waited.

Thalrik returned to the ledger.

“With the place?”

“With someone.”

The temperature around Gravel changed.

Not much. Just enough.

“Someone.”

“A man.”

Gravel’s hand tightened on the brass rail.

Thalrik noticed. Naturally. Thalrik noticed coin scratches on old copper and lies told by men with clean gloves. Gravel getting protective was not exactly subtle statecraft.

“She laughed,” Thalrik said.

Gravel’s jaw shifted.

“That all?”

“No.”

“Boss.”

Thalrik looked up.

“Careful.”

Gravel held his eyes for a moment, then looked away first. Not ashamed. Not backing down either. Just remembering where he was, who he was talking to, and that tired loyalty was still loyalty even when it had dirt on its boots.

“She’s crew,” Gravel said.

“I know.”

“She don’t always know when she’s being led somewhere she shouldn’t go.”

“She was not.”

“You know that?”

“Dagda was watching.”

That landed better than reassurance would have. Reassurance was cheap. Dagda was not.

Gravel breathed out through his nose.

“Dagda liked him?”

“Dagda says he behaved properly.”

Gravel grunted.

“That ain’t the same.”

“No.”

“But it’s not nothing.”

“It is not.”

Thalrik let the words sit a moment before adding, “She says he was a gentleman.”

Gravel gave him a flat look.

“Dagda said that?”

“She was very firm.”

“She’s a dwarf. I bet she’s really firm.”

Thalrik stared at him.

Gravel shrugged one shoulder.

“What?”

“Try saying that to her.”

“I’m stupid, boss. I’m not suicidal.”

A corner of Thalrik’s mouth betrayed him.

Gravel saw it and wisely did not celebrate. Men had been killed for less. Probably by dwarves. Possibly by really firm ones.

“She was glowing,” Thalrik said.

“Dagda?”

“Yes.”

“That’s unsettling.”

“Extremely.”

Gravel rubbed at his eyes.

“Toots really laughed?”

“More than once.”

“With him?”

“With him.”

Gravel looked toward the dark windows, toward the city beyond, toward the long downtown run from polished Vice to the Blood and Sand.

“She barely laughs when things are funny.”

“I am aware.”

“She must’ve been nervous.”

“She was.”

“Proud?”

“Painfully.”

“Miffed at you?”

“Immediately.”

Gravel’s mouth twitched.

“There she is.”

Thalrik picked the pen back up.

“She came in ready to resent the chair, the room, the line, my help, and possibly the concept of clean glassware.”

“And then?”

“And then she had a good time.”

Gravel nodded slowly.

He did not ask the man’s name.

Thalrik did not offer it.

That was deliberate. Gravel could feel it, though not all the way through. Thalrik had put a curtain over the shape of the thing, and Gravel knew better than to yank curtains in a room full of expensive secrets.

Still, he filed it away.

Toots. The Vice. A man. Dagda watching. Laughter.

Not enough to know.

Enough to remember.

“Good,” Gravel said at last.

“Yes.”

“She needed that.”

“She did.”

The words hung there, simple and bare and therefore embarrassing to both men.

Civilization had produced steel, ledgers, gambling houses, sewers, perfume, and compound interest, but two working men admitting they cared about a woman on their crew still apparently required a funeral atmosphere.

Gravel cleared his throat first.

“Right. Since I’m already here doing responsible things like some kind of punished kid, I need to bring up another one.”

Thalrik looked at the wrapped books under Gravel’s arm.

“How many right things did you do tonight?”

“Too many. I’m disgusted with myself.”

“Proceed.”

“It’s about Fenby.”

Thalrik opened his eyes.

“Who?”

“Fenby.”

Thalrik stared at him.

“The cook.”

Thalrik’s stare did not improve.

Gravel pointed vaguely downtown, as if the gesture could pass through polished walls, rich streets, respectable gates, and every bad decision between the Admiral’s Vice and the Dregs.

“At the Blood and Sand.”

“The Blood and Sand has Grenda.”

“The Blood and Sand had Grenda cooking.”

“That distinction sounds important.”

“It is if you’ve eaten there lately.”

Thalrik leaned back against the bar.

“Gravel.”

“Boss.”

“Who is Fenby?”

“The old fur-foot. One eye. Gray hair. Bad hip till he don’t want it to be. Moves quick when someone touches his knives.”

“Fur-foot.”

“Halfling, boss.”

“I know what you meant.”

“Then why’d you ask?”

“To give you the opportunity to hear yourself.”

Gravel considered that, then rejected it as a trap built by education.

“He’s Uncle Fenby.”

“I do not know Uncle Fenby.”

Gravel blinked.

Then he blinked again, slower.

“Right.”

“Do not say right like that.”

“I just mean…”

“Gravel.”

“He’s been cooking there almost two months.”

Thalrik said nothing.

Gravel shifted the wrapped books from under his arm and set them on the bar.

The Admiral’s Vice, being both expensive and well-built, declined to creak dramatically. Respectable buildings had no sense of theater.

Thalrik looked at the books.

Then at Gravel.

“You brought the Blood and Sand books.”

“I did.”

“After closing.”

“I did.”

“To discuss a man I do not know.”

“That’s the part I’m enjoying least, yes.”

Thalrik unwrapped the bundle.

The Blood and Sand accounts did not look like the Admiral’s Vice accounts. They were uglier. More irregular. Less perfumed with polish and more stained with reality. One page had a thumbprint that might have been gravy or blood, and Bard’s Gate remained too sophisticated a city to ask which.

Still, they were books.

Books could talk.

Thalrik turned the first one toward the lamp.

“How long did you say?”

“Almost two months.”

“At the Blood and Sand.”

“Yes.”

“As cook.”

“Yes.”

“And I do not know him.”

“You’ve said that part.”

“I enjoy returning to the central horror.”

Gravel rubbed the back of his neck.

“I thought you did.”

Thalrik looked up.

“You thought I knew him.”

“I thought maybe you sent him.”

“To the Blood and Sand.”

“Maybe.”

“Without telling you.”

“You do things without telling people.”

“That is not the same as doing things without telling you.”

Gravel gave him a look.

Thalrik held it for half a breath, then returned to the books.

“Fine. It is sometimes the same.”

“Thought so.”

“Do not get comfortable.”

Gravel leaned both hands on the bar, clearly already comfortable in the way exhausted men were comfortable near judgment.

“Look here.”

He turned one of the books around and tapped a line with his finger.

“This is before.”

Thalrik looked.

Food purchases. Spoilage. Wastage. Breakage. Ale movement. Small beer. Cheap wine. Soap. Salt. Firewood. Onions.

The Blood and Sand lived in those lines. Poorly, but it lived.

Gravel flipped forward.

“This is after.”

Thalrik’s expression changed.

Not much. A fraction of a fraction. But Gravel knew enough to see it.

Waste was down.

Beer movement was up.

Ale was up too.

Cheap wine was down, which was interesting. People were buying better drink when the food stopped punishing them.

Bread costs had risen.

Meat losses had dropped.

Kitchen breakage was down.

Soap purchases were up.

Thalrik frowned.

“That’s obscene.”

“Which part?”

“We made money on food.”

Gravel nodded.

“A tidy bit.”

“At the Blood and Sand.”

“I know. I nearly went to temple.”

Thalrik flipped another page.

“Drink sales are up.”

“That’s what I’m saying. People stay longer when eating ain’t a dare.”

“And Grenda?”

“Grenda is a mile away from the cooking, which is where she should have been standing all along. She still helps. Chopping, hauling, washing, watching the little ones. Fenby don’t treat her cruel. Just doesn’t let her season anything without supervision.”

“That may be the first act of mercy performed in that building.”

“Even I eat there now.”

Thalrik looked up, suppressing a smile.

“You?”

“Me.”

“Voluntarily?”

“I know.”

“That changes the valuation.”

“Should.”

Thalrik turned another page, then reached for one of his own smaller ledgers from beneath the bar. The Admiral’s Vice ledger remained open beside it, neat and civilized. This second one was thinner, private, and considerably less honest-looking.

He began cross-referencing.

Payroll.

Kitchen expenses.

House retainers.

Odd payments.

Names.

Nicknames.

Cash draws.

Gravel watched him work in silence.

That was one of the things about Thalrik. The boss could talk smoke around a room until grown men thanked him for being robbed, but when he got his teeth into numbers, the smoke vanished. There was just the blade.

Thalrik turned one page.

Then another.

Then one more.

“No Fenby.”

Gravel grimaced.

“Right.”

“No Fendby.”

“I’ve heard it both ways.”

“No Uncle Fenby.”

“Wasn’t expecting that one in the books.”

“No old one-eyed halfling kitchen tyrant listed under petty cash.”

“Now you’re just being difficult.”

“I am being thorough.”

“That too.”

Thalrik closed the private ledger with one finger marking his place.

“Who hired him?”

Gravel opened his mouth.

Nothing came out.

Thalrik waited.

Gravel looked at the bar. Then at the ceiling. Then at a perfectly innocent gaming table.

“He just walked in.”

“Walked in.”

“And started.”

“Started cooking.”

“Declared he was the new cook first.”

Thalrik stared.

Gravel winced.

“I was busy.”

“For two months?”

“In my defense, the food got better immediately.”

“That is not a defense. That is a confession with gravy.”

Gravel rubbed his face.

“The place was chaos. We’d had two knife fights in three nights, Grenda burned the barley, Toots was doing four jobs, and this old fur-foot comes in through the back like he owns the hearth. Sets down a bundle. Looks at the pot. Says, ‘No.’ Just that. One word. Like judgment day, but shorter.”

Thalrik said nothing.

“Then he took the spoon out of Grenda’s hand.”

“Brave.”

“Suicidal, I thought.”

“And Grenda allowed this?”

“She tried to argue. He called her ‘girl’ and told her if she wanted to kill customers she should use a cleaner knife.”

Thalrik’s brows rose.

“And she didn’t flatten him?”

“She looked confused. Then he fixed the stew.”

“Fixed.”

“I don’t know cooking words, boss. He did something with onions and fat and spite, and suddenly the room smelled like people had reasons to live.”

Thalrik looked back down at the numbers.

“And nobody asked where he came from.”

“I asked about pay.”

“At least there is that.”

“He said not to worry about it.”

Thalrik stopped.

Gravel watched his face and immediately regretted saying it that way.

“Not to worry about it,” Thalrik repeated.

“That’s what he said.”

“In those words.”

“Near enough.”

“Gravel.”

“He said, ‘Pay? Don’t worry about pay.’ Then he laughed like I’d said something funny.”

“Was it funny?”

“No.”

“Then he thought something else was funny.”

“That’s what I’m saying.”

Thalrik leaned very slowly back against the bar.

Both men were quiet for a moment.

The Admiral’s Vice, polished and respectable and full of expensive little sins, seemed suddenly less comforting than usual.

Gravel scratched at his jaw.

“I figured maybe he meant you were paying him.”

“I was not.”

“I know that now.”

“You thought I had hired a cook for the Blood and Sand and failed to mention it to the man managing the Blood and Sand.”

“When you say it like that, it sounds bad.”

“It is bad.”

“It also sounded like something you might do.”

Thalrik looked at him.

Gravel lifted one hand.

“Not as an insult.”

“Try again.”

“As saying you got a habit of not telling folk things till they step in ’em.”

“That is not better.”

“It’s truer.”

Thalrik held his stare a moment longer, then let it go because the worst part was that Gravel was not entirely wrong. Few things damaged authority like an protege accidentally brushing against truth.

“So,” Thalrik said, “an unknown halfling walked into my tavern, took over my kitchen, improved my books, fed my staff, refused pay, and found the subject amusing.”

“The Blood and Sand kitchen,” Gravel said.

Thalrik’s eyes narrowed, then he smiled.

“The failing kitchen.”

“Not failing now.”

“Because of an unknown man.”

“Unknown halfling.”

“As if that helps.”

“It does a little.”

“It does not.”

Gravel shifted his weight.

“He’s not asking for anything.”

“That makes it worse.”

“It should make it better.”

“No. Men ask for things. Coin, drink, favors, a warm bed, somebody else’s boots. A man asks for nothing, he’s hiding the bill.”

Gravel shifted his weight.

“Maybe he’s just being decent.”

Thalrik looked at him.

Gravel grimaced.

“I know. Sounds stupid when I say it - he’s doing good work.”

“For free.”

“Maybe he’s bored.”

“Bored men play cards. Bored men drink. Bored men start fights they lose to feel young again. Bored men do not usually restructure a tavern kitchen and alter the profit curve.”

“Old ones might.”

“Old ones are worse. They have had more time to become complicated.”

Gravel made a face.

“I don’t think he’s bad trouble.”

“Every man is trouble. Free men are expensive trouble wearing a discount.”

“That’s a horrible sentence.”

“It is a useful sentence.”

Gravel looked down at his hands.

“He feeds the crew.”

Thalrik’s expression changed, just a little.

There it was.

Not the profit. Not the cleaner kitchen. Not the miracle of people voluntarily buying food at the Blood and Sand without first making peace with their gods.

The crew.

Gravel pushed on before he could make it sound polished and ruin it.

“He makes the girls eat before a rush. Makes the kitchen boys wash their hands twice. Caught one of the dock idiots trying to crowd Tiny by the back door and told him the next thing he touched would be soup with his fingers in it.”

“That work?”

“Dock idiot left with all his fingers and a new respect for soup.”

“Effective.”

“He makes Toots sit.”

Thalrik went still.

Gravel saw it, but kept his voice level.

“Not long. Ten minutes here and there. Bowl in front of her. No fuss. Just says if she falls over in his kitchen, he’ll step on her by mistake and then they’ll both be embarrassed.”

Thalrik looked away.

“She eats?”

“She does.”

Gravel hesitated, then added, “Does it with Druuna too.”

Thalrik’s eyes came back to him.

“Druuna.”

“Aye. Only different.”

“How different?”

“With Toots he barks. With Druuna he pretends he’s barking.”

Gravel scratched at his jaw, looking uncomfortable with the softness of it.

“Girl comes in all bones and tusks, looking like a stiff wind could carry her off if the gods weren’t weighing her down with purpose. He sets real food in front of her. Not scraps. Not thin soup. Real food. Bread with butter thick enough to leave teeth marks. Eggs if he’s got ’em. Meat cut small. Sweet things she’ll actually eat.”

Thalrik said nothing.

“Says it mean, mind you. Tells her no prophet ever did good work rattling like a bag of spoons. Calls her ‘little sermon’ when he thinks no one’s listening.”

“And she eats?”

“She does for him.”

That sat there a moment.

Gravel looked down at the books.

“He’s gruff with everyone. But with her…”

He shrugged, as if shrugging could murder sentiment before it grew witnesses.

“With her he’s sweet.”

The Vice went quiet again.

Thalrik looked down at the Blood and Sand numbers, then at his own ledger, then at the space where Fenby’s name was not.

“He feeds the crew,” Gravel said.

This time, neither of them pretended he only meant food.

The Vice was quiet for a few breaths.

Then Thalrik shut the Blood and Sand book with one hand.

“You came uphill after closing to tell me that a man I did not hire is feeding my staff, improving my business, enforcing kitchen discipline, and refusing wages.”

Gravel scratched his jaw.

“When you say it like that, it sounds strange.”

“It is strange.”

“It’s also right.”

“No. It is suspiciously useful.”

“That too.”

“And you want what?”

Gravel straightened a little.

Not much. Enough.

“I want him paid.”

“He refused pay.”

“He said not to worry about pay. That ain’t the same.”

“No. It is more annoying.”

“I want it set aside, then. Back pay. Proper rate. More than Grenda was worth near a stove, less than he’s saving us, because I’m not a fool and neither are you.”

Thalrik’s eyes sharpened.

Gravel noticed and swallowed whatever rough joke had been next in line. Even he knew when not to elbow a dwarf near money.

“He’s been good for them,” Gravel said instead. “The crew. Not just the house. The people.”

Thalrik watched him.

Gravel held still under it.

He respected Thalrik. That was the thing most people missed. Gravel would grumble, curse, use ugly little words for half the city, and spit sideways at most authority on principle, but Thalrik was different. Thalrik had built things that worked. Thalrik paid when payment was due. Thalrik noticed value even when it came in dirty boots.

And Gravel, for all his rough edges and dockside vocabulary, knew when something mattered.

“He walked in,” Gravel said, “and the place got better. I don’t know his story. I don’t know if he’s hiding from someone, laughing at us, paying off some debt nobody told me about, or just too bored to die properly. But he works. He feeds my people. So I’m asking you to do right by him before the joke turns out to be on us.”

Thalrik said nothing for long enough to make the lamps seem louder.

Then he set the pen down.

“You should have told me sooner.”

“I know.”

“You should have gotten his full name.”

“I know.”

“You should have asked who he was.”

“I know.”

“You should have investigated the man who found wages amusing.”

Gravel looked pained.

“I know.”

Thalrik leaned on the bar.

“But you came tonight.”

Gravel nodded once.

“Yes.”

“After giving Toots the night off.”

“Yes.”

“After making one stupid right decision.”

“Guess I was already rolling downhill.”

“Dangerous condition.”

“Trying to hit something soft.”

Thalrik opened the Blood and Sand book again and drew it beside his own private ledger.

“We set aside back pay from the first full week his purchases changed the books.”

Gravel blinked.

“That far back?”

“That is when he became measurable.”

“Gods. You stonebacks really do romance with ledgers.”

Thalrik looked up slowly.

Gravel froze.

Then, very carefully, he added, “Respectfully.”

“Try harder.”

“Boss.”

“That will do.”

Gravel let out the breath.

Thalrik wrote a note in the margin.

“We do not call it charity. We do not surprise him with a purse like he is a beggar.”

“No.”

“We offer terms.”

“Title?”

“Maybe.”

“What kind?”

“Kitchen Steward. Hearthmaster. Something useful. Not decorative.”

“He’ll hate decorative.”

“Most competent people do.”

Gravel nodded.

“And if he says he doesn’t work there?”

“Then we ask what would make him say otherwise.”

“And if he still won’t?”

“Then the money remains set aside until he either takes it, names someone who should, or gives me a reason not to trust him.”

Gravel’s shoulders eased.

That was as close to relief as he liked to show in clean rooms.

“Good.”

“Do not look comforted. It encourages poor boundaries.”

“Wouldn’t dream of it.”

“We also find out who he is.”

Gravel grimaced.

“He’s a halfling. They’re all someone’s uncle.”

“Then find the someone.”

“I can ask around.”

“No.”

Gravel frowned.

“No?”

“You can ask badly. Loudly. With words like fur-foot.”

“That’s just what they are.”

“That is exactly the defense that gets a man stabbed with a pie fork.”

Gravel considered this and, with visible reluctance, accepted the existence of pie forks as a credible threat.

Thalrik closed the private ledger.

“We ask a halfling.”

Gravel’s brow furrowed.

“Guaco?”

“If he knows, he will know too much.”

“That’s usually how Guaco knows things.”

“Yes. Infuriating habit.”

“You want him digging?”

“I want him curious.”

“That’s worse.”

“Usually more efficient.”

Gravel scratched at his jaw again.

“And what do I tell Fenby?”

“Nothing yet.”

“Nothing?”

“Nothing that sounds like investigation. Nothing that sounds like accusation. Nothing that sounds like pity.”

“So just normal?”

“Can you manage normal?”

“No.”

“I admire the honesty.”

Gravel gathered the Blood and Sand books, though Thalrik kept one thin copied sheet beside his own ledger.

“I’ll keep him cooking, then.”

“Do that.”

“And the back pay?”

“I will calculate it.”

“Proper?”

Thalrik looked offended.

“I am wounded.”

“You are rich.”

“I can be both.”

Gravel nodded toward the ledger.

“Thanks, boss.”

Thalrik picked up the pen.

“You are thanking me for agreeing to pay a man we apparently allowed to work unpaid for two months.”

“Still.”

“Get out before I become ethical enough to punish someone.”

Gravel turned toward the staff door.

At the threshold, he stopped.

“She really laughed?”

Thalrik did not look up.

“Yes.”

Gravel nodded once, mostly to himself.

“Good.”

Then he went out into the night.

The door shut behind him, leaving the Admiral’s Vice polished, quiet, and respectable.

Downtown, in the Dregs, the Blood and Sand slept badly around a kitchen that finally worked.

Somewhere in that kitchen, an old halfling with one eye and no listed wage had likely already put the knives in the correct drawer, covered the stock, cursed the bread, and decided nothing about anyone.

Not officially.

Which, in Bard’s Gate, was often how the serious things started.
