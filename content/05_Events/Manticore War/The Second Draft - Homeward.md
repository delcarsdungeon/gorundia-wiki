# The Second Draft — Homeward

The **Second Draft** had been underway long enough for the adrenaline to wear off.

That was generally when everyone discovered what hurt.

Armor had been loosened wherever dignity allowed and removed wherever dignity had finally surrendered. Weapons lay within reach rather than on belts. Somebody had found beer. Somebody else had found bread, cheese, and a pot of something that Lyosha had inspected before declaring it probably safe.

Nobody had bothered setting a table.

They had simply accumulated amidships.

Beringer sat against the rail with one knee raised, gauntlets beside him and the Lord Marshal's mantle folded into a tolerably clean heap. Orin had taken possession of most of a bench. Cwenheld was sitting on a crate while Lyosha examined the burn beneath one shoulder of her armor.

Again.

“I am fine.”

Lyosha did not look up.

“You were struck directly by a fireball.”

“It was not my first.”

“That is not medically reassuring.”

“It is historically accurate.”

Nearby, Radomir had acquired parchment, ink, and a reasonably flat ammunition chest.

He was writing.

Aelorin noticed.

“Report?”

“No.”

“Orders?”

“No.”

Aelorin watched the pen move for another moment.

“Rykeena?”

Radomir continued writing.

“Yes.”

Aelorin nodded.

“Good. I was worried you had developed administrative ambitions.”

“No.”

Mara sat nearer the stern, cloak around her shoulders, watching the land below stream past. Her hands were tucked beneath the cloak. Every so often one shoulder gave a small involuntary tremor she seemed determined not to notice.

Vianola Erinleaf had finally removed her helmet.

Her thick red hair, imprisoned beneath leather and padding for most of the raid, spilled down her back in a disorder which would have horrified several generations of university portraitists. Her staff rested across her knees.

Aelorin was checking arrows.

Not counting them.

Checking them.

Each shaft was rolled between his fingers, held against the fading light, inspected as though the arrow might have committed some subtle professional disgrace during the fighting.

Eventually he said:

“Professor.”

Erinleaf looked over.

“Yes, Master Archer?”

“I have a question.”

“That has traditionally been how conversations with you begin.”

Aelorin accepted the blow without visible injury.

## Why Did You Hold Back

“Why did you hold back?”

That drew Orin's attention.

Beringer's too.

Erinleaf regarded him over the top of her staff.

“I did not hold back.”

“You did.”

“I exercised judgment.”

“Professor, you spent most of Ravenfast invisible.”

“A useful condition in battle.”

“You made Baron Kharne wiser.”

Orin looked toward her, one eyebrow slowly going up.

Erinleaf inclined her head.

“No offense, Baron.”

“None taken.”

Aelorin continued.

“You sharpened the Lord Marshal's senses. You drank a Lifting and Carrying potion. You maneuvered. You observed. You selected targets very carefully.”

“And?”

“And I know what you can do.”

Erinleaf considered him for a moment.

Then held up one finger.

“Multiple reasons, Master Archer.”

Orin settled back.

Here it came.

“First, it would hardly do to arrive alongside the Venerated Shield of Heironeous, the Lord Marshal of Gorundia, and Baron Kharne, only to spend the engagement attempting to show them up.”

Beringer stared at her.

Cwenheld started laughing.

Orin pointed at Erinleaf.

“You see? That is respect.”

“No,” said Beringer. “That is an insult wearing academic robes.”

Erinleaf glanced down at her leather armor.

“Not presently.”

Aelorin smiled.

“And the second reason?”

“Ravenfast was indoors.”

The humor left her voice.

“Tight spaces. Doors. Choke points. Limited lines of sight. Our people crossing through enemy positions.”

She tapped one finger against the staff.

“Anything which controls the movement of the enemy also becomes an obstacle to our own team.”

Beringer nodded.

“Force walls.”

“Yes.”

“Domes.”

“Yes.”

“Area spells.”

“Especially area spells.”

Her eyes went briefly toward Mara.

“Fire.”

Mara looked back at her.

“Lightning.”

A faint smile.

“Yes. Lightning too.”

Erinleaf continued.

“Battlefield control is useful when you possess a battlefield. Ravenfast was a collection of rooms containing people trying to murder one another at conversational distance.”

Orin nodded sagely.

“Accurate.”

“The correct spell is not always the largest spell available.”

Aelorin turned another arrow between his fingers.

“That explains Ravenfast.”

“Yes.”

“It does not, however, explain the screaming from the drop pod.”

Erinleaf went still.

Not dramatically.

Just still.

Beringer looked up.

“What screaming?”

Aelorin's eyebrows rose.

“You never heard about that?”

“There was a great deal of screaming during the camp attack.”

“Fair.”

Beringer pointed at him.

“Which pod?”

“The eastern drop, near the wagon lines.”

Beringer's expression sharpened with recognition.

“I had the two nearer command.”

“Yes. Baron Kharne and Lady Cwenheld were elsewhere. I heard screaming and assumed our people had been caught.”

Erinleaf sighed.

Aelorin smiled pleasantly at her.

“So I went to investigate.”

Orin leaned forward.

“And?”

“I found Professor Erinleaf standing outside a Force Dome.”

Beringer looked at Erinleaf.

She looked serenely back.

“How many?”

“Seven,” Aelorin said.

“Six combat-effective,” Erinleaf corrected.

“One was unconscious.”

“Then he was not combat-effective.”

“He was still inside.”

“Seven occupants. Six combat-effective.”

Beringer rubbed his forehead.

“What were they screaming about?”

Aelorin looked delighted to have finally acquired an audience.

“That depended upon the man.”

Erinleaf's mouth tightened slightly.

“Master Archer.”

“One appeared convinced something was crawling beneath his armor.”

“There was not.”

“One was attempting to climb the dome.”

“He believed the ground was unsafe.”

“Two were trying to hide behind a third.”

“A predictable herd response.”

“One had thrown away his weapon and was begging something none of us could see to leave him alone.”

Erinleaf looked out past the rail, toward the darkening sky.

“That particular hallucination became stronger than I intended.”

Beringer slowly looked from Aelorin to Erinleaf.

“What the hell did you do?”

“Nothing permanent.”

Orin closed his eyes.

Cwenheld grinned.

Lyosha stopped examining her burn.

Aelorin said softly:

“There is a phrase which develops considerable character when spoken by a master enchanter.”

Erinleaf gave him a level stare.

“The distinction matters.”

“I believe you.”

“I induced fear, confusion, distorted sensory perception, and temporary mental incapacitation.”

“Temporary,” Aelorin repeated.

“Yes.”

“Very important.”

“Yes.”

Beringer looked at her staff.

“And then?”

Aelorin answered.

“Then I noticed she was exhausted.”

“I was fatigued.”

“You were leaning on the staff.”

“It is structurally adequate.”

“You were using it as a cane.”

“Only intermittently.”

Beringer frowned.

“You pushed too far.”

Erinleaf was quiet for a moment.

“Yes.”

The admission took some of the amusement out of the group.

She rested both hands upon the head of the staff.

“The dome had solved the immediate problem. They could not reach the camp.”

“But eventually it had to come down,” Beringer said.

“Yes.”

“And you were running out of power.”

“Yes.”

Aelorin nodded.

“That was when she asked me to remain.”

Erinleaf looked at him.

“I requested assistance.”

“You said, and I believe I remember this precisely, ‘Master Archer, when the field collapses these men are unlikely to behave rationally. I would appreciate your preventing them from dispersing throughout the camp.’”

“That is a request.”

“It is an execution order with excellent diction.”

Cwenheld laughed.

Erinleaf ignored her.

Beringer understood the problem immediately.

“They'd scatter.”

“Yes.”

The professor's voice became quieter.

“Terrified men do not necessarily run away from danger. They run away from whatever direction they believe danger occupies.”

She glanced toward the open air beyond the rail.

“There were wounded everywhere. Teamsters. Servants. Horses. Fires. Men trying to move casualties.”

Beringer nodded.

“Armed rats running in every direction.”

“A crude analogy.”

“Wrong?”

“No.”

Aelorin picked up his bow.

“So we waited.”

“How long?”

“Not long.”

Erinleaf said, “It felt longer.”

Aelorin glanced at her.

That seemed to be the part he remembered most clearly.

“The dome failed.”

He mimed drawing.

“One immediately went toward the wagons.”

His fingers released an imaginary string.

“He did not reach them.”

“Another charged me,” Erinleaf said.

Orin raised an eyebrow.

“After what you'd done to them?”

“Yes.”

“Brave.”

“Possibly.”

“Stupid.”

“Certainly.”

“What happened?”

Erinleaf considered.

“I persuaded him that attacking me was no longer a useful course of action.”

Aelorin looked at Beringer.

“He fell down.”

Erinleaf frowned.

“He experienced acute motor inhibition.”

“He fell down.”

“Both statements are accurate.”

Aelorin continued.

“One tried another direction. I took him. Two were killed by knights. One remained sufficiently confused that Professor Erinleaf was able to incapacitate him.”

“And the last?”

Aelorin smiled.

“He made the curious tactical decision to stand in the open and determine where I was.”

Beringer winced.

“Poor bastard.”

“Indeed.”

Aelorin picked up another arrow.

“He found me.”

Silence held for a moment.

Then Orin said:

“So that is what you do outdoors.”

Erinleaf looked at him.

“When circumstances justify it.”

Orin nodded.

“Good to know.”

Cwenheld pointed at Aelorin.

“You waited until now to tell anyone this?”

“I assumed it was known.”

Beringer looked offended.

“I dealt personally with two of the damned pods and nobody thought to mention that our university professor turned a third into a private hell?”

Erinleaf stiffened slightly.

“I would object to that characterization.”

Aelorin looked at her.

“Would you?”

A pause.

“I would object to the word _private._”

Orin laughed so hard the bench creaked.

Mara smiled despite herself.

Beringer shook his head.

“Unbelievable.”

Aelorin returned to his arrows.

## Ravenfast

“Speaking of things which apparently escaped appropriate appreciation...”

He looked at Orin.

“That opening at Ravenfast.”

Orin's expression brightened instantly.

“Finally.”

Beringer groaned.

“We are not encouraging him.”

“We absolutely are,” said Cwenheld.

Aelorin looked toward Orin.

“The Iron Covenant knight.”

“Yes.”

“You stabbed him through both eyes.”

“One motion.”

“Yes.”

Orin demonstrated with two fingers.

“He left an opening.”

“You nearly took the top of his head off.”

“Then it was a good opening.”

Mara spoke from the stern.

“It was disgusting.”

Orin looked at her.

“You sound impressed.”

“I can be both.”

“Good.”

Aelorin continued.

“And then the hatchet.”

Orin's expression soured.

“The hatchet was troublesome.”

“The throw was excellent.”

“The recovery was troublesome.”

Cwenheld laughed.

“He buried it almost to the haft in the mage.”

“I wanted it to stay.”

“It did.”

“Too well.”

Beringer looked toward Mara.

“And then the same mage put a fireball into Cwenheld.”

Mara's eyes hardened.

Cwenheld waved one hand.

“I survived.”

Lyosha immediately said:

“You were badly burned.”

“I survived badly burned.”

“That is not better.”

Mara looked at Cwenheld.

“He was watching you.”

Cwenheld nodded.

“He should not have been.”

Mara's voice was flat.

“So I shot him in the back.”

Aelorin added:

“With a lightning bolt which I suspect shortened his remaining theological questions considerably.”

Mara's mouth twitched.

“He was a legitimate target.”

“No criticism intended.”

Beringer nodded toward her.

“You hit him when it mattered.”

Mara looked down.

“He wasn't looking at me.”

“That,” said Aelorin, “is generally when arrows and lightning perform their finest work.”

Mara gave him a sideways glance.

“You approve?”

“Deeply.”

Cwenheld leaned back against the crate.

“The chain idiot was better.”

Aelorin sighed contentedly.

“Yes.”

Beringer looked at her.

“I still cannot believe that worked.”

“He hooked me.”

“Yes.”

“And tried to pull me toward him.”

“Yes.”

Cwenheld shrugged.

“So I pulled.”

Orin grinned.

She continued.

“I had the Invincibles blessings on me.”

Lyosha elaborated for her.

“Holy Might. Grace. Vigor.”

“Yes. Those.”

“Those are not interchangeable.”

“They all made me stronger.”

“That is not how theology works.”

“It is how chains work.”

Orin slapped one knee.

Cwenheld mimed wrapping the chain around herself.

“He pulled once.”

Her smile became unpleasant.

“I pulled harder.”

Aelorin nodded appreciatively.

“His expression was excellent.”

“He came to me very quickly.”

“You shield-bashed him.”

“Yes.”

“Then hit the manticore knight.”

“He was also there.”

“Then hit the chain knight again.”

“He remained there.”

Beringer rubbed his temples.

Lyosha muttered something prayerful.

Cwenheld looked at him.

“What?”

“You were alternating opponents.”

“Neither would leave.”

Aelorin lifted one finger.

“The chain knight eventually developed the correct instinct.”

Mara looked over.

“He disconnected his end.”

“Yes.”

“And ran.”

“He attempted to.”

Aelorin's voice became almost wistful.

“He had discarded his chain, turned his back, and entered a clear corridor.”

Orin shook his head.

“You make it sound romantic.”

“It was an immaculate line.”

“You shot him in the back.”

“Yes.”

“Good shot?”

Aelorin looked personally wounded.

“Baron.”

Orin held up both hands.

“Forgive me.”

"For questioning the shot?  Yes.  For hacking him to bits?  Perhaps."

Beringer looked toward Lyosha.

“And meanwhile you were keeping this collection of idiots alive.”

Lyosha shrugged.

“Mikhail did most of my fighting.”

Somewhere nearer the bow, his divine servitor was helping crewmen shift a damaged crate as though this were the most ordinary use imaginable for a messenger of divine power.

Lyosha continued.

“My task was simple. Preserve energy. Do not get dragged into melee unless necessary. Heal what must be healed. Leave bruises alone. Stop bleeding. Keep people moving.”

Cwenheld looked at him.

“You healed me.”

“You were on fire.”

“Briefly.”

“You keep saying that as though duration improves combustion.”

Beringer lifted his cup toward Lyosha.

“You did exactly what you were supposed to.”

Lyosha returned the gesture.

“Somebody had to.”

Then Beringer looked across the deck.

“Radomir.”

The pen continued moving.

“Mm?”

“You nearly died twice.”

“Yes.”

Aelorin waited.

Nothing else came.

Orin looked at Radomir.

“That's it?”

Radomir glanced up.

“I did not.”

“You did not what?”

“Die.”

Then he returned to the letter.

Aelorin leaned slightly toward him.

“Are you informing Rykeena that you nearly died twice?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

Radomir considered this an oddly stupid question.

“Could make worry.”

Cwenheld stared at him.

“So you're leaving it out?”

“Yes.”

Beringer shook his head.

“She'll find out.”

Radomir dipped the pen.

“Later.”

Aelorin peered toward the page.

“What are you telling her?”

Radomir covered the letter with one broad hand.

“No.”

“Something romantic?”

“No.”

“Poetry?”

Radomir looked genuinely offended.

“No.”

Orin leaned over.

“Tell her Beringer nearly cried.”

Beringer turned.

“I will throw you off this boat.”

Radomir calmly resumed writing.

“I write that Baron Kharne behaves normally.”

Orin laughed.

Even Mara did.

For a while that was enough.

The world moved beneath them.

The Second Draft creaked softly around its rigging.

Then Erinleaf looked toward Mara.

Not obviously.

Vianola Erinleaf had mastered the art of observing someone without making them feel examined centuries before Mara Moffet had been born.

Before Ravenfast she had sent Orin a message spell.

Mara was skilled.

Very skilled.

Well trained.

But there were signs in her reactions, in the places her attention went when she thought nobody was watching, that some earlier violence had lodged more deeply than she admitted.

There was no modern name for it.

Erinleaf did not need one.

Now Mara had fought extremely well.

And was sitting alone.

## No Word from Baron Moffet

Beringer finally said:

“Anything since Orin filled you in?”

The last of Mara’s smile disappeared.

“No.”

She looked down at her hands. They were trembling again, faintly now, enough that she curled them together beneath the edge of her cloak.

“Ravenster is in Moffet territory. Quinn is in contact with him. She’s...”

A pause.

“Functionally in charge.”

Beringer nodded.

“And your father?”

“I’ve tried to reach him.”

“How many times?”

Mara gave him a look.

“Enough.”

That answered the question.

She looked back toward the open sky.

“There are plenty of reasons I might not be able to reach him. Distance. Wards. Countermeasures. He could be underground. Moving. Somewhere with enough magical interference to foul the connection.”

The reasons came quickly.

Too smoothly.

“There are reasons.”

Nobody contradicted her.

Mara rubbed one thumb hard across the knuckles of the other hand.

“But no confirmed sighting. No message. Nothing.”

Her jaw tightened.

“That doesn’t mean anything.”

A moment.

Then, quieter:

“It doesn’t mean _one particular thing._”

Orin leaned forward, forearms on his knees.

“Your father commands men. Men leave tracks. Orders. Witnesses. Someone knows where he was last.”

“Eventually.”

“Yes.”

Mara looked at him.

“That isn't very comforting.”

“No.”

Orin held her gaze.

“But it is true.”

That almost got a smile.

Almost.

“I keep thinking the next attempt will work.”

Her voice had gone quieter.

“And then it doesn't.”

Just the thing underneath all those perfectly reasonable explanations.

Beringer said nothing.

There was nothing useful to say.

They had all spent too much time at war to offer her _he's probably fine_ like some cheap charm against reality.

Cwenheld reached for the beer.

She held it out.

Mara looked at it.

“My hands are shaking.”

“I can see that.”

Mara gave her an irritated look.

Cwenheld kept holding the cup out.

“Use both.”

That did get the smallest laugh.

Mara took it in both hands.

Cwenheld leaned back against the crate.

No reassurance.

No promise.

Just beer.

After another long stretch of silence, Aelorin said:

“For what it is worth, Lady Mara...”

She glanced at him.

“The mage at Ravenfast was dangerous.”

“Yes.”

“He had just put Lady Cwenheld on the receiving end of a fireball and was still trying to control the engagement.”

Aelorin inspected the fletching on another shaft.

“You removed him immediately.”

Mara said nothing.

“That was not merely a large lightning bolt.”

He looked at her now.

“That was good shooting.”

Mara gave him a faint smile.

“From you, I'll take that.”

“You should.”

Orin raised his cup.

“I also approve of lightning.”

Beringer looked at him.

“You approve of anything that produces a corpse.”

“Not true.”

A beat.

“Sometimes I approve of prisoners.”

Cwenheld laughed.

Beringer shook his head.

## The Brute Squad

“We did well.”

It was simple.

Perhaps because anything grander would have sounded ridiculous coming from him.

“We went into Ravenfast against the Iron Covenant. We hit them from both directions. We stayed together. Nobody froze. Nobody chased glory.”

He looked at Orin.

“Mostly.”

Orin objected.

“The eyes were tactically sound.”

“They were.”

“Then take that back.”

“No.”

Beringer looked around the group.

“Everybody did their damned job.”

Lyosha raised his cup.

“To doing your damned job.”

Cwenheld touched hers against it.

Aelorin followed.

“To doing it with proper form.”

Orin added his.

“To doing it harder than the other bastard.”

Mara finally leaned in and added hers.

“To hitting them when they're looking at someone else.”

Aelorin smiled.

“Now you're learning.”

Beringer looked toward Radomir.

Radomir was still writing.

“Radomir.”

He looked up.

Beringer raised his cup.

Radomir stared at it.

Then at the others.

He put down the pen.

Picked up his own cup.

“To not dying.”

Orin nodded.

“That's the spirit.”

They drank.

Aelorin sat back afterward, looking around the deck.

“You realize someone is going to give this group a name.”

Mara said quietly:

“They already have.”

Beringer frowned.

“What?”

She looked into her cup.

“The Brute Squad.”

Silence.

Orin grinned.

Cwenheld grinned.

Aelorin appeared to be seriously evaluating it.

Lyosha closed his eyes.

Beringer said:

“No.”

Mara's smile widened.

“Yes.”

“Absolutely not.”

Aelorin tilted his head.

“It has a certain directness.”

“We are not calling ourselves the Brute Squad.”

Orin lifted his cup again.

“To the Brute Squad.”

Cwenheld immediately joined him.

“To the Brute Squad.”

Lyosha sighed and raised his.

“I hate all of you.”

Aelorin lifted his.

“An excellent beginning for a unit motto.”

Beringer stared at them.

Then toward Erinleaf.

“You're supposed to be the sensible one.”

Erinleaf adjusted the fall of her cloak.

“I am a professor, Lord Marshal. My professional responsibilities do not include rescuing senior military officers from nicknames they have demonstrably earned.”

Mara laughed.

Not the little distracted breath she'd managed earlier.

Actually laughed.

Beringer looked around at the collection of battered lunatics surrounding him.

At Cwenheld, who had been on fire.

At Orin, who was still annoyed about retrieving his hatchet from a dead mage.

At Aelorin, who apparently regarded killing fleeing Covenant knights as an exercise in composition.

At Lyosha, who had spent the entire battle making sure nobody's heroics became funerals.

At Radomir, who had nearly died twice and was carefully making certain Rykeena would hear about neither occasion.

At Mara, finally laughing despite everything waiting for her back home.

And at Professor Vianola Erinleaf, who apparently considered trapping seven Iron Covenant soldiers inside an invisible cage and systematically terrifying them out of their minds to be a reasonable application of battlefield restraint.

Beringer drank.

“Fuck it.”

Orin brightened.

Beringer pointed at him.

“Don't.”

The Second Draft carried the Brute Squad home.
