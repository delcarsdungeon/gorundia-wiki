The rain had turned the yard at Blackbar to red-brown mud.

Men moved through it in silence.

Some carried wounded.  
Some carried the dead.  
Some carried nothing at all, because there are moments when hands forget what they are for.

Baron Jareth DeSalle stood near the whipping posts and looked toward South Desalle Village.

The false village.

The painted kindness.

The lie his father had permitted to stand.

Then he stepped forward.

Not to Beringer.  
Not to Lyosha.  
Not to Vianola.

To the yard.

To the men watching.  
To the women wrapped in blankets.  
To the prisoners.  
To the DeSalle retainers who could no longer pretend they did not understand what their house had served.

His voice carried cleanly through the rain.

> “Hear me.”

The yard stilled.

Jareth’s cloak hung heavy with water. Mud streaked one boot to the knee. His face was pale, but his voice did not break.

> “South DeSalle is opened.”

A murmur moved.

He cut through it.

> “Not as barracks. Not as storage. Not as another post with cleaner walls.”

He looked toward the strip.

> “As refuge.”

Silence.

> “Any woman taken from this place, any soul wrongfully held by Vogel chainers, cron-men, debt-takers, private jailers, or noble cowards hiding behind locked doors may be carried there under my protection.”

His hand closed once at his side.

> “By my purse. By my land. By my name.”

A DeSalle knight lowered his eyes.

Jareth saw it.

Good.

Let them feel it.

> “And because refuge alone is not enough—because a door opened today may be barred again tomorrow—I hereby endow and found an independent knightly order.”

That made even the soldiers look up.

Rain struck steel.  
Mud sucked at boots.  
Somewhere, someone wept quietly and did not stop.

Jareth raised his voice.

> “They will owe no household service to DeSalle.”

A beat.

> “Not mine. Not my father’s memory. Not any baron’s pride.”

He looked to Beringer now, briefly.

> “They will serve the Crown’s justice, the Light’s witness, and the prisoner whose name has been erased.”

Then back to the yard.

> “They will seek hidden cells. Open unlawful doors. Break false writs. Hunt chainers wherever they crawl. They will carry keys before swords, but by the gods, they will carry swords.”

The rain thickened.

No one moved.

> “Let them be known as the Free Order of the Open Chain.”

The words settled over the yard like a banner being raised.

> “Their charge is this: no chain without judgment. No prison beyond witness. No soul forgotten because the powerful found silence convenient.”

He turned toward the cages.

His voice dropped, but somehow carried farther.

> “My house helped make this place possible.”

No one contradicted him.

No one could.

> “Then my house will help make its answer.”

He drew his sword.

Not high.

Not grandly.

Point downward, into the mud.

> “I grant them land at South DeSalle. I grant them founding silver. I grant them right of hall, stable, chapel, infirmary, and court of witness.”

The blade sank into the wet earth.

> “And then I step back.”

That surprised them more than the sword.

Jareth’s jaw tightened.

> “Because this cannot become another lord’s private hand.”

He looked over the wounded, the rescuers, the soaked and silent men.

> “The war does not wait. I know that. I will not pull knights from the line for ceremony, nor demand men abandon the King’s need for my conscience.”

A cold breath.

> “But this war is already full of cages.”

His eyes hardened.

> “So let the first Chainwardens be those who can serve here and now. Scouts who find locked rooms. Priests who witness unlawful holding. Knights who ride to take chainers alive if possible—”

A pause.

> “—and otherwise.”

No one needed the rest.

Jareth pulled the sword free from the mud.

> “This begins today. In this yard. In this rain. Without banners. Without polished shields. Without the comfort of pretending we were ready.”

He looked once more toward South DeSalle.

> “The false village becomes a true refuge.”

Then to the cages.

> “The chain becomes evidence.”

Then to the men.

> “And the door opens.”

For a long moment, only rain answered.

Then one of the DeSalle men knelt.

Not to Jareth.

To the oath.

Another followed.

Then another.

Jareth did not smile.

He looked like a man watching judgment arrive and realizing, too late, that it had his face.

> “Stand,” he said quietly.

A beat.

> “There is work.”
