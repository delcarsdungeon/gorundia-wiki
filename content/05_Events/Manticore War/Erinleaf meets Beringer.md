## Professor Erinleaf Testing Beringer

Use this as her first real private conversation after she formally joins the Lord Marshal’s army.

---

Professor Erinleaf does not ask to speak with him alone.

She simply waits until the room has thinned enough that everyone left is either useful, trusted, or too tired to pretend they are not listening.

Her traveling cloak is still folded over one arm. Her aide has already vanished somewhere with the baggage, the papers, and probably someone’s dignity.

She studies Beringer for several seconds.

Not his armor.

Not his sword.

His face.

Then:

> “Lord Marshal.”

A pause.

> “Before I accept a place within your command structure, I require one clarification.”

She says it like she is asking about supply wagons.

> “Not legal. Not military. Personal.”

A beat.

> “Your brother had six wives.”

She lets that hang.

No drama.

No accusation she cannot prove.

Just the fact sitting there in its chair, tracking mud on the rug.

> “Six women drawn into the orbit of House Vogel. Six women destroyed by it.”

Her eyes remain steady.

> “You have broken how many hearts?”

A slight tilt of her head.

> “It is a difference of severity, certainly.”

Then the knife:

> “But is it a difference of kind?”

Silence.

She does not soften it.

> “You are not Wilhelm Vogel. I am aware of that. I am also aware that men do not become dangerous only when they begin killing.”

Another beat.

> “Sometimes they become dangerous when they discover that people will forgive them for surviving.”

That one should make the room uncomfortable. Good. Let it.

If Beringer snaps, she has learned something.

If he deflects, she has learned something.

If he answers honestly, she gets interested.

---

## Follow-Up Questions She Cannot Help Asking

These should feel like an oral exam from a woman who has ruined tenure committees for sport.

> “When you give an order that wounds someone you care for, do you remember the wound—or only the necessity?”

> “Do you prefer loyalty because it is freely given, or because it spares you the labor of being questioned?”

> “How many people near you believe they can tell you no?”

> “No, Lord Marshal. Not theoretically. Name three.”

That one is a bastard. Keep it.

> “When you are angry, do you become more honest—or merely more efficient?”

> “Your reputation is built on restraint under pressure. I am less interested in what you restrain than in what you believe restraint entitles you to.”

> “Do you fear becoming your brother?”

Pause.

> “Or do you fear discovering that the resemblance is useful?”

That’s the money line. Mean, fair, and absolutely her.

## If Beringer Pushes Back

If he says something like, “You compare me to a murderer?”

She answers:

> “No.”

A pause.

> “I compare you to a man raised by the same bloodline, shaped by the same expectations, and now handed lawful violence at national scale.”

Then, colder:

> “If that comparison offends you more than it informs you, we have found the first problem.”

Very Erinleaf. No shouting. No flinch. Just academic violence in a sensible robe.

## If Beringer Answers Well

Something like: “Yes. I fear it. Every day.”

She does **not** comfort him.

> “Good.”

A pause.

> “Fear is not virtue. But it is sometimes the last warning bell before certainty becomes rot.”

Then:

> “Keep people near you who are inconvenient.”

Her eyes flick briefly toward Norwin, if he is present.

> “You seem to have made a promising start.”

Tiny dry joke. Practically a hug, from her.

## If Norwin Is Present

> “Master Stoneyeye argues because he is untidy, curious, and constitutionally allergic to clean conclusions.”

A beat.

> “This makes him annoying.”

Then, with the smallest possible warmth:

> “It also makes him useful.”

Then back to Beringer:

> “Do not collect only useful obedience, Lord Marshal. Obedience is easy to organize. Judgment is not.”

She adjusts one glove.

> “I will serve in your army.”

A pause.

> “Not because I trust you.”

Then:

> “Because I suspect you may still be capable of distrusting yourself.”

And then she walks away, leaving everybody in the room emotionally pantsed.

## Erinleaf’s Answer

> “The point, Lord Marshal?”

Professor Erinleaf folds her hands.

Not nervously.

Never nervously.

Like a woman closing a book she has already memorized.

> “The point is that armies are not led by banners, laws, or declarations of virtue.”

A pause.

> “They are led by the tolerances of the man at the center.”

She lets that sit.

> “I am not asking whether you are good. Good men are often quite dangerous. They commit themselves beautifully to mistakes lesser men would abandon from embarrassment.”

A faint, surgical smile.

> “No. I am asking where you fail.”

Then the bridge analogy, but upgraded out of peasant metaphor territory:

> “When engineers inspect a bridge, they do not begin by praising the stonework. They ask where the load travels. Where the mortar has weakened. Which arch will crack first when the river rises.”

She looks directly at him.

> “You, Lord Marshal, are currently a bridge.”

Beat.

> “The Crown is crossing you. The army is crossing you. Half the kingdom’s wounded conscience is crossing you. Several extremely armed friends are crossing you while pretending they are not leaning on the railing.”

Dry, not cute.

> “So I am asking where the first crack forms.”

If Beringer says, “You think I’ll break?”

> “Of course.”

Immediate. No cruelty.

> “Everything breaks. The relevant question is whether it breaks predictably, repairably, and before anyone important is standing beneath it.”

That is the old-elf professor brain. She is not being dramatic. She is describing stress failure.

## If He Says the Wilhelm Comparison Was Offensive

> “It was intended to be.”

A pause.

> “Not because offense is useful. It usually is not. But because your reaction to it is.”

Then:

> “Wilhelm Vogel’s crimes were monstrous. That does not make every comparison to him monstrous.”

She raises a brow.

> “You share blood. History. Injury. Expectations. A talent for command. A capacity for severity. You have also inherited the political vacuum his death created.”

A beat.

> “To refuse the comparison entirely would be vanity.”

Then colder:

> “To embrace it would be pathology.”

And then, almost approvingly:

> “I am determining which error you prefer.”

## If Beringer Says “I’m Not My Brother”

She should answer:

> “No.”

Then nothing.

Let him breathe.

Then:

> “But ‘not Wilhelm’ is not a moral philosophy. It is merely a boundary marker.”

That’s a killer line.

> “A man can spend his whole life standing on the other side of a monster and still let the monster choose his shape.”

She gives him no escape hatch.

> “So I ask again: what are you when you are not reacting to him?”

## If He Says “Ask Directly, Then”

She does.

> “Very well.”

A pause.

> “When you hurt someone and the cause is just, do you feel grief…”

Beat.

> “…or relief?”

That one should hit like a dropped anvil in a library.

Then:

> “When someone forgives you, do you feel humbled by it?”

A little tilt of the head.

> “Or licensed?”

And:

> “When your friends excuse your temper because you are tired, burdened, and necessary…”

She watches him very closely.

> “Do you correct them?”

## Her Deeper Point

This is where she shows the “old and sharp” part.

> “I have taught enchantment for longer than your kingdom has remembered some of its wars clearly.”

Not bragging. Just fact.

> “Do you know the first lesson every competent enchanter learns?”

She does not wait long.

> “People rarely surrender themselves all at once.”

A pause.

> “They yield in small permissions.”

Now she starts counting, very softly.

> “A little anger, because the day was difficult.”

> “A little cruelty, because the target deserved worse.”

> “A little secrecy, because explanation would slow the necessary work.”

> “A little obedience, because questioning would burden the man already carrying so much.”

Her eyes narrow slightly.

> “That is how men become instruments.”

Then:

> “Not by waking one morning as tyrants.”

A beat.

> “By being useful yesterday, necessary today, and unquestioned tomorrow.”

That should make Beringer uncomfortable because it is not about Wilhelm anymore. It is about **the Lord Marshal’s army**.

## If Arlen Is Nearby

Arlen is trying not to exist.

Erinleaf absolutely notices.

> “Magus Corvis is afraid of me.”

Arlen dies.

> “That is unfortunate, but instructive.”

She turns slightly toward Beringer.

> “He does not fear that I will strike him. He fears that I will observe him accurately.”

A pause.

> “Many men confuse those sensations.”

Then:

> “Your staff will not always tell you when you frighten them. Some will mistake fear for loyalty. Some will call it respect because that sounds cleaner.”

She looks back to Beringer.

> “You must learn the difference without requiring them to bleed first.”

Arlen, somewhere behind a folio, silently files for death.

## Erinleaf’s Actual Opinion

She probably admired **Theron the institution**.

She distrusted **Theron the man**.

She pitied **Theron the father**, though she would rather swallow glass than say it sentimentally.

Her read:

> “Lord Sylvanranth understood people very well. Too well, perhaps. There is a point at which understanding becomes less like sympathy and more like ownership.”

And that maps hard to her enchantment ethics. Erinleaf teaches that influence is easy, consequence is not; compulsion removes choice rather than persuading. ## What She Thought of the Caervane Reckoning

This is where her opinion sharpens.

After Rowena’s assassination, Theron dismantled the entire conspiracy across the Gorundian–Dominion frontier, eliminating every identifiable person connected to the plot. Erinleaf would not dismiss that as blind vengeance. Quite the opposite. She would find it more disturbing because it **wasn’t** blind.

> “Theron did not rage. That would have been simpler. Rage burns hot and wastes fuel. He calculated. He traced. He corrected the world around the shape of his loss.”

That is a terrifying sentence, and she’d mean every word.

She would also know the Reckoning changed him: those who knew him before remembered warmth and restraint, while afterward he became colder, more calculating, and less tolerant of threats. Erinleaf would call that the dangerous hinge in his life.

That line is money. Use it.
