Cwenheld’s hand settles on the haft of her weapon.

“I have seen enough.”

Vianola Erinleaf turns from the prisoners.

“No,” she says.

The word is not loud.

It cuts anyway.

Cwenheld’s burned eyes shift toward her voice.

“Careful, professor.”

“I am being careful. That is rather the point.”

The nearest men stop breathing properly.

Vianola steps closer, robes dark at the hem from mud and blood, expression composed in the unbearable way of elves who have mistaken self-control for a personality.

“Some of these men should hang. Some should vanish into royal custody. Some should be questioned until their lies grow tired. Some may yet tell us who gave orders, who carried them, who enjoyed them, and who merely survived them.”

Cwenheld’s jaw tightens.

“You ask mercy for them?”

“No.”

Vianola’s answer is immediate.

“I ask accuracy.”

That makes Cwenheld pause.

Vianola lowers her voice.

“Theravain the Ashkeeper wrote that ash remembers the shape of the thing consumed.”

Silence.

Not confusion.

Recognition.

Cwenheld’s fingers tighten once on the haft.

Vianola continues, softer now.

“He did not say fire was wrong.”

A beat.

“He said fire used too soon leaves nothing to read.”

Cwenheld turns fully toward her.

“You knew him?”

“No.”

Vianola does not pretend otherwise.

“But I studied him. Which, among my people, is sometimes the nearest thing to affection we permit ourselves before becoming insufferable.”

A faint breath from someone nearby. Maybe not a laugh. Maybe survival instinct malfunctioning.

Vianola does not smile.

“You knew the man. I know the work. Between us, perhaps we can avoid betraying both.”

Cwenheld says nothing for a long moment.

Then:

“Sort them.”

Vianola inclines her head.

“Precisely.”

Cwenheld’s voice drops.

“And when they are sorted?”

Vianola looks back at the prisoners.

“Then, Dame Cwenheld… you may see enough.”
