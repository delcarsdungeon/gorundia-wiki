# Elunaeth Speaks to the Grugach

Orin’s force was away from the deep forest now.

That mattered.

The grugach did not come flowing down from high boughs or stepping out from ancient trunks like old tales given knives. There were no great trees here to hide them. No green vault overhead. No sacred dark.

So they came another way.

Twos and threes.

Out of brush. Out of ditch-shadow. Out of winter-bent hedgerow and broken scrub. From behind low stone walls. From places no grown man should have fit without making sound.

Ash-streaked faces. Bone charms. Thorn braids. Knives carried like fingers. Eyes too bright.

They did not form ranks. They gathered.

That was close enough.

Orin’s men gave them room.

Not respectful room.

Survival room.

Sir Morkken sat mounted near Orin’s banner with the bodyguard men-at-arms, one hand resting low near his sword. He did not interfere. He looked like a man watching wolves come close to a chapel door and deciding, grimly, that the priest had better know what she was doing.

Rhovanion came last.

Elunaeth walked on his arm.

The grugach changed.

Not dramatically. Not like court fools gasping over a saint’s relic. They were too proud for that, and too feral, and too well acquainted with blood.

But shoulders lowered.

Eyes dropped.

One old grugach with white scars across his throat touched two fingers to the mud, then to his lips.

A younger one, still wearing somebody’s teeth at his belt, stopped smiling.

Elunaeth looked at them as if she had known each of their mothers before they were born.

Then she smiled.

Small. Sad. Terrible.

“Oh,” she said softly. “There you are.”

No one answered.

The camp seemed to quiet around her. Men who had been pretending not to watch became very busy with straps, buckles, cookpots, and other lies.

Elunaeth let go of Rhovanion’s arm and stepped forward onto the damp ground.

“You have been very busy,” she said. “Little knives in the brush. Little ghosts with red hands. So proud. So hungry. So certain the trees clap when men scream.”

A few of the grugach shifted.

Rhovanion did not.

Elunaeth’s gaze moved over them.

“The trees do not clap.”

That landed harder than shouting would have.

One of the grugach flinched.

“The trees remember,” she said. “They remember who bled. They remember why. They remember whether the knife was needful, or merely pleased with itself.”

Silence.

One of the older grugach bowed his head.

Elunaeth turned slightly, looking back toward Orin’s banner, toward the lines of tired men, muddy horses, stacked shields, and wet firewood.

“The man you follow is not of your wood.”

A low murmur moved through them.

“No,” she said, answering it before it grew teeth. “He is not. He smells of iron, oath, horse, smoke, worry, and old grief. A dreadful bundle. Very human. Very loud inside.”

A few grugach blinked.

Elunaeth’s smile deepened.

“But he has not lied to the root.”

That stopped them cold.

“He has stood where men break. He has fed the hungry edge of duty with his own pride. He has carried fear without throwing it onto smaller backs. He has looked at power and chosen burden.”

She looked from face to face.

“You know what that is.”

No one moved.

“You call it different things in the old dark. Stone-heart. Thorn-father. The one who walks first when the trail hates feet.”

One of the grugach made a soft sound in his throat.

Elunaeth pointed gently toward Orin’s camp.

“He is not your king. Do not make that mistake. Kings are dangerous little cages. He is not your shaman. He does not know the names under the moss. He is not your blood.”

Then her voice thinned into something colder.

“But he is the man holding the line while your enemies sharpen hooks for children.”

The grugach went still.

There it was.

The real altar.

“So,” Elunaeth said, almost cheerfully, “perhaps do not bite his fingers while he is trying to hold the door shut.”

One of Orin’s men, listening from much too close, suddenly remembered he had urgent business with a saddle strap. Humanity’s noblest survival instinct: looking extremely occupied.

Elunaeth clasped her hands before her and rocked once on her heels.

“You may be terrible. Terrible is useful. Terrible has its place. I have loved terrible things.”

Her eyes flicked to Rhovanion.

“Or will.”

Rhovanion’s expression did not change.

For him, this was nearly a ballad.

Elunaeth turned back to the grugach.

“But you will be terrible where you are pointed. You will not cut the tether because rope offends you. You will not frighten the horse because its eyes are stupid. You will not test Orin’s men because they smell soft and sleep too loudly.”

A young grugach bared his teeth.

Elunaeth looked at him.

The teeth disappeared.

“You will not make him spend strength disciplining allies when enemies are generous enough to deserve all of it.”

Her voice gentled.

“When he says hold, hold.”

The grugach listened.

“When he says wait, wait.”

Even the horses seemed quieter.

“When he says kill…”

She smiled again.

This time the grugach smiled with her.

“…then be quick, and do not make poetry of it unless the hour is very dull.”

That earned a few breathy, nervous laughs.

Not from the humans.

The humans were not suicidal.

Elunaeth lifted one hand and touched two fingers to her brow, then to the air between them.

“You are seen,” she said. “Not tamed. Not shamed. Seen.”

The old scarred grugach sank to one knee.

After a moment, the others followed.

Not all gracefully.

Not all willingly.

But they did.

Elunaeth turned back to Rhovanion and took his arm again.

As they walked away, one grugach whispered something in their own tongue.

Rhovanion glanced back.

Elunaeth did not.

She only said, softly enough that perhaps only he heard:

“Yes. I know. But Orin is prettier when they obey him.”
