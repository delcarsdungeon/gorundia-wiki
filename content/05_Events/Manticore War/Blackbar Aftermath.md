10 Coldeven 1231

# Blackbar Post-Battle Report

The first thing anyone remembers is the axe being handed back.

Not the gate breaking.

Not the shouting.

Not the Pig Lantern sign swinging with a crossbow bolt through the painted pig’s eye.

The axe.

A woodsman’s axe, plain and work-worn, its haft darkened by sweat and weather. It belongs to a quiet levy soldier named **Corin Mereholt**, who had carried it because no one told him not to and because men who know wood bring tools when sent to break a place made of posts, gates, and lies.

Corin stands frozen in the mud, both hands out, as **Sir Beringer Amadis** gives the axe back to him.

Beringer’s gloves are streaked with old blood and fresh splinters.

The axe head is notched.

Behind him, one of the whipping poles lies broken across the yard.

Another leans at an angle, its base hacked half through.

The third is already down.

The fourth is still standing.

Not for long.

No one had ordered it.

No herald had announced it.

There was no speech.

Beringer had simply stopped in front of the poles, looked at them, and turned to the nearest man.

> “BRING ME AN AXE!”

Corin had blinked.

Then ran it over.

Because when the Lord Marshal asks to borrow your axe in the middle of a captured slave-yard, there are only so many correct answers, and most of them involve shutting up.

Beringer took the axe in both hands.

The first blow bit deep.

The yard went quiet.

Not all at once. Silence had to travel. Men at the Pig Lantern stopped shouting. A horse tossed its head in the stable lane. One of the Black Spur prisoners looked up. One of the rescued women looked away, then looked back.

The second blow struck lower.

The third split old wood.

The fourth sent a spray of pale chips into the mud.

**Dame Katherine Kendal** stood at Beringer’s shoulder the whole time.

Not behind him.

Not watching from the safe edge of the yard.

At his shoulder.

Helmet off. Shield on her arm. Mud up her greaves. Face pale with fury held so tightly it had become discipline.

She looked too young to be standing there.

That made it worse.

The prisoners knew her colors. Some of them did, anyway. Southmere. Kendal. Vogel vassal blood, whether she liked the shape of it or not.

And there she was, standing beside Beringer while he cut down the whipping poles one by one.

A Black Spur prisoner muttered something under his breath.

Kat turned her head.

Slowly.

The prisoner stopped.

Kat said:

> “You should save your breath. You may need it for naming men.”

That reached the kneeling rows.

It was meant to.

Beringer did not look back.

He chopped.

The first pole groaned.

Beringer hit it again.

It broke.

It came down badly, half-falling, half-tearing free, dragging its iron ring through the mud with a wet scrape.

No one cheered.

That would have made it smaller.

Beringer moved to the next pole.

The second pole fell faster.

The third took longer. Its base had been sunk deep, its iron ring newer than the others. Someone had maintained it. Someone had cared enough to keep this particular cruelty in working order.

Beringer’s jaw tightened.

He said nothing.

That was worse.

Another blow.

Another.

The wood cracked.

The pole fell.

Only then did **Lyosha Ohotnik** speak from near the cookhouse wall, where he stood with a water bucket in one hand and blood on the cuff of his sleeve.

> “That one needed saying.”

Beringer glanced toward him, breathing hard.

> “It was badly built.”

Lyosha looked at the shattered post.

> “Terrible craftsmanship.”

That got one short laugh from a soldier who immediately looked ashamed of it.

Beringer moved to the last pole.

This one had a strip of cloth caught on the iron ring.

Not much.

Just a torn scrap, grey-white once, now rain-dark and rust-stained.

Lyosha saw it before the axe fell.

> “Wait.”

Beringer stopped.

So did the yard.

Lyosha stepped closer.

He did not touch the cloth at first.

He looked at it.

Then at the iron ring.

Then at the mud below.

The scrap was not tavern linen.

Not a work shirt.

Not a woman’s shift.

It had a narrow line of faded yellow stitching at the edge.

Pelorite.

Old travel-robe cloth.

Lyosha’s face did not change much.

That was how Beringer knew it mattered.

Kat saw it too.

Her grip tightened on her shield strap.

She looked at the prisoners.

Not broadly.

Specifically.

The Black Spur men.

The toll clerks.

Mother Salla.

The kind of look that made denial feel premature.

Lyosha carefully worked the cloth free.

He held it in his palm.

Beringer did not ask what it was.

He knew enough.

The yard held itself still.

Then a runner came from the clay pit.

A boy, no more than sixteen, one of the baggage squires. **Nollis Fen**, muddy to the knees, pale under the dirt, with both hands cupped around something wrapped in a rag.

He nearly ran into Ser Caldus Wren, caught himself, then looked toward Beringer and Lyosha as if hoping someone else would become responsible for the world.

Ser Caldus took one look at his face.

> “Bring it.”

The boy swallowed and came forward.

He unwrapped the rag.

Inside lay two medallions and several scraps of cloth.

One medallion was a copper sun-disk, bent almost double.

The other was brass or low gold, blackened along one edge, its face still clear enough to show the rays of Pelor around a worn central boss.

Not pilgrim tokens.

Not roadside charms.

Priest medallions.

Issued pieces.

Marked pieces.

The sort worn by traveling clerics, not pious farmers hoping the road would be kinder than usual.

The cloth scraps were worse.

Grey-white robe wool.

Yellow stitching.

One strip still had dark thread where a temple mark had been torn away.

Another was stiff with old blood and clay.

Nollis Fen said, very softly:

> “There’s more in the pit, my lord.”

No one moved.

Even Mother Salla stopped performing.

Lyosha took the medallions.

Not dramatically.

Not with trembling hands.

Carefully.

That made it worse.

Beringer looked at him.

Lyosha said:

> “These were not sold from a market stall.”

Beringer’s answer was light.

Too light.

> “No.”

He looked back at the prisoners.

> “Markets prefer their dead anonymous.”

Kat’s voice was quiet.

> “Then they chose the wrong road.”

That was the moment the yard changed.

Before that, Blackbar had been a captured post.

A filthy one. A corrupt one. A place of unlawful restraint, debt bondage, false writs, and private cruelty.

But this was different.

Pelorite priest medallions.

Scraps of travel robes.

Blood in the clay.

The dead were no longer rumor.

They had arrived in the yard.

Beringer handed the axe back to Corin Mereholt.

> “Good edge.”

Corin stared at him.

> “My lord?”

Beringer nodded toward the last pole.

> “I will need it again in a moment.”

Then he turned to **Ser Cadran Oathmere**.

> “Witnesses.”

To **Ser Edrast Mournvale**:

> “Names.”

To **Ser Caldus Wren**:

> “The pit sealed and guarded.”

To **Sir Avel Stormcairn**:

> “No one questions the rescued without a priest present.”

To **Dame Katherine Kendal**:

Nothing.

Kat was already where she needed to be.

At his shoulder.

Watching the prisoners.

Watching the pole.

Watching the kingdom decide what it was going to tolerate.

The last whipping pole came down in silence.

# The Yard

Blackbar does not look conquered.

It looks opened.

That is worse.

The outer gate stands wide, one leaf sprung crooked on its hinges where the first push broke the bar and the second push made the point. The yard beyond is churned to black-brown mud: hoofprints, boot tracks, blood, spilled ale, soap water, ash, torn parchment, and the kind of human waste everyone pretends belongs to animals until the fighting stops.

The Pig Lantern sign still swings over the permanent building at the rear of the vice yard.

A painted pig with a lantern in its mouth.

Someone has put a crossbow bolt through the pig’s eye.

No one admits to it.

No one is disciplined for it.

By the time Beringer and Lyosha see the yard properly, the violence has already become procedure. Prisoners are down in rows. Evidence is being guarded. Fires are being beaten out. The wounded are being dragged toward priests, water, or whatever patch of ground has not yet been made worse by human ambition.

The captured post is not quiet.

It is listening.

Every man in the yard has now seen the poles fall.

Every prisoner has now seen the priest medallions carried into the open.

Every knight has now been reminded that rage is not command.

Every rescued person has now seen, with their own eyes, that the things built to hurt them can be cut down.

That matters.

Not enough.

But enough to begin.

# Beringer's First View

Beringer sees order first.

He has to.

Not mercy.

Not rage.

Order.

The prisoners are down in rough categories, because the first mercy after battle is sorting. Men who stood under arms are not the same as men who used arms to hide crimes. Men who wrote the crimes into ledgers are not the same as men who slept above them and pretended paper made everything clean.

A fine distinction.

Naturally, half the kingdom will try to pretend it is impossible because thinking is tiring.

The ordinary garrison men kneel nearest the stable wall. Eighteen of them, perhaps twenty if the two wounded in the cookhouse live. They are filthy, soaked, and exhausted. Most are older than the boys who stormed them. Gate men. Stable guards. Wall men. Men with the look of professionals who knew the place was rotten and decided survival was a virtue.

They keep their heads up.

That matters.

At their front kneels **Cadric Vane**, acting post-captain.

Cadric has a split lip, one eye closing, and both hands tied before him rather than behind him. He yielded his sword properly. One of Beringer’s knights remembered manners before violence swallowed the whole plate.

Cadric says nothing.

Beringer respects that more than he wants to.

The Black Spur men are not with the ordinary garrison.

Beringer made certain of that.

They kneel in a separate patch of mud beside the holding cage, guarded by men who do not bother hiding their disgust. These prisoners look different. Younger, mostly. Hard-eyed. Quick to flinch when someone walks behind them. One has oil under his nails. Another has a fresh burn across the back of his hand. Another has a broken nose and keeps trying to spit blood without seeming afraid.

He is afraid.

He is simply the kind of man who thinks fear becomes courage if he makes it ugly enough.

Beringer looks at them and sees no soldiers.

Not yet.

Maybe not ever.

He says, low enough for the officers nearest him:

> “Those men are not to be called prisoners of war until I say so.”

That travels.

Fast.

# Lyosha's First View

Lyosha sees the wounded first.

He cannot help it.

There are three kinds of silence in the yard.

The first is the silence of soldiers trying not to groan.

The second is the silence of guilty men realizing their friends have begun naming them.

The third is the silence from the back rooms of the Pig Lantern.

That is the one Lyosha hears most.

A few women and two boys have been brought out wrapped in horse blankets, cloaks, and whatever clean cloth could be found. Some sit in the lee of the cookhouse. Some refuse to sit with their backs to a door. One girl keeps her hands tucked under her arms because she has not yet understood that no one is going to grab her wrists again.

An older woman has taken a cup of water and holds it with both hands.

She has not drunk from it.

She seems afraid that drinking will make the world real.

Lyosha sees bruises hidden under powder. Ankles rubbed raw. A burn scar on one shoulder. A ribbon tied too tightly around a wrist to hide the mark beneath it.

He sees **Tansy Reed**, the camp cook, standing near the rescued people with a pot ladle still in her hand as if it might serve as a mace. She is crying and furious and pretending both are indigestion.

She tells one of Beringer’s guards:

> “I fed them when I could.”

The guard says nothing.

Tansy snaps:

> “That was not a confession, you armored bucket.”

Lyosha believes her.

Mostly.

That is the miserable part.

Blackbar is full of people who did one human thing and nine cowardly ones.

Very inconvenient for clean judgment.

The gods, naturally, outsourced the paperwork.

# Mother Salla Greaves

**Mother Salla Greaves** kneels in the mud with the other market prisoners.

She is the only one acting as if she has granted permission for it.

Her hands are bound behind her. Her nose is bleeding. Not broken, probably, but struck hard enough that both nostrils are dark and wet. Mud streaks the side of her face where she went down. Her hair has come loose from its pins, though not enough to ruin the impression that she is still taking inventory.

She looks offended.

Not frightened.

Offended.

The report from the storming party is simple.

When the Pig Lantern was breached, Salla stood in the common room and told the knights that if they wanted to play at rescuers, they might start by asking how many of their own sort had warmed their boots in her house before finding religion.

That got attention.

Then she called **Ser Rion Kelbray** “a clean little hero, arriving just in time to rescue women already ruined by better-paying men.”

That got her punched.

Ser Rion admits it without hesitation.

> “I struck her, my lord.”

Beringer looks at Salla’s bloody face, then back at him.

> “With a gauntlet?”

> “No, my lord. Bare hand.”

A pause.

> “She was fortunate.”

That is **Ser Edrast Mournvale**, not helping.

Beringer does not raise his voice.

> “Ser Rion. You will not strike a bound prisoner.”

Rion’s jaw tightens.

> “She was not bound when I struck her.”

Beringer gives him the look.

The one that says the next sentence had better arrive dressed for its own funeral.

Rion bows his head.

> “No, my lord. I will not strike a bound prisoner.”

Mother Salla smiles through the blood.

> “There. See? Law arrives late, but it does arrive dressed nicely.”

The Heironean paladin steps forward.

**Sir Avel Stormcairn**, Ordained Knight of Heironeous, rides with the host but is not one of Beringer’s household favorites. That makes him useful. He is not part of the inner circle. He has no need to flatter the Lord Marshal’s mood. He wears a white cloak gone grey at the hem, battered plate, and the expression of a man who has spent years making peace with the fact that righteous violence is still violence.

He looks down at Salla.

> “Woman, you have mistaken restraint for uncertainty.”

Salla stops smiling.

# The Knights' Reports

The first useful reports come in angry.

Beringer prefers reports angry rather than embellished. Anger can be managed. 

## Ser Edrast Mournvale

**Ser Edrast Mournvale** comes from the writ office with ash on his sleeves and murder filed neatly behind his eyes. He is usually courtly in that old-fashioned way, like a man raised by portraits and disapproving furniture.

Not now.

He holds up a scorched bundle of pages wrapped in wet cloak wool.

> “They tried to burn the names. Not the coin. Not the toll marks. The names.”

He looks toward the clerks.

> “That tells us what they feared.”

The pages are wet, torn, and burned along one edge.

Still readable.

Still damning.

## Ser Rion Kelbray

**Ser Rion Kelbray** reports from the Pig Lantern rear rooms.

He is young, polished, furious, and still too idealistic to hide how badly Blackbar has offended him. He keeps his voice steady because he knows Beringer is watching him.

> “Locks on the outside. Shutters hooked from the outside. Three rooms dressed as lodging, two as storage, one as a sickroom. None were meant to let anyone leave.”

His eyes flick once toward Mother Salla.

> “I should have used the gauntlet.”

Beringer does not dignify that with an answer.

Sir Avel does.

> “No. You should have remembered who you were before she reminded you what she is.”

That lands harder than a reprimand.

Rion accepts it.

Barely.

## Ser Caldus Wren

**Ser Caldus Wren** led the stable search. He is practical, plain-spoken, and more disturbed than the men who found the back rooms.

> “Fourteen horses left. Bad ones, mostly. Alric took the fit stock.”

He checks the tally in his hand.

> “Best remounts gone. Two pack mules gone. Three patrol saddles gone. Surgeon’s chest gone. Good harness gone. Some feed missing. Not a panic strip. Chosen.”

That lands hard.

Not because it is surprising.

Because it proves intent.

Blackbar was not abandoned by accident.

It was stripped and left to rot loudly enough to delay pursuit.

## Ser Marten Vey

**Ser Marten Vey**, younger and still carrying too much polish on him, comes from Harl Brunt’s tent with a wrapped bundle.

He unwraps it once.

Then wraps it again immediately.

> “Small irons.”

His voice catches, but only slightly.

> “Marked as foal hobbles.”

No one laughs.

Not even the bastards.

## Ser Cadran Oathmere

**Ser Cadran Oathmere** brings the blackmail ledger.

He is stern, literate, and absolutely livid in that quiet knightly way where one can hear the violence being alphabetized.

He carries the book under one arm like a live snake that studied law.

> “This names patrolmen, toll guards, two clerks, merchants, and at least six men from Alric’s road party as customers or debtors. Not all crimes. Some are payments. Some are worse.”

Beringer’s face does not change.

That is how Lyosha knows the report hurt him.

Because Beringer has already decided Alric’s road detachment receives soldier treatment unless named.

And now some of them are named.

## Sir Avel Stormcairn

Sir Avel gives the shortest report.

> “The yard is secured. The prisoners are not calm. The knights are worse.”

Beringer looks at him.

Avel continues:

> “Give angry men tasks before they start mistaking anger for command.”

Beringer nods once.

# Brennart Soot

**Brennart Soot** is gone.

Not missing in the chaos.

Gone before it began.

That is what makes the report turn cold.

By the time the outer yard is taken and the Black Spur men are separated, no one has seen Soot since the night before. His cot was stripped. His tack was gone. His best cloak was gone. Three hooks in the patrol room stood empty: one for his riding bow, one for his sword belt, one for the black oilskin satchel he kept close and let no clerk touch.

Two of his men vanished with him.

**Garric Tallow**, the dog-man.

Thin, fox-faced, good with hounds, bad with people. Known for finding runaways who thought they were clever.

**Merrit Churn**, the quiet brute.

Former kennel-hand turned patrol hardcase. Broad, slow-eyed, and obedient in the way men are when they have replaced conscience with habit.

The stable tally confirms three horses taken after moonrise.

Not the best horses. Alric had already stripped those.

But good enough.

Fresh enough.

Chosen by a man who needed distance more than pride.

Old **Jorren Pitch**, the farrier, says Soot took the north ditch road.

> “He used it when he didn’t want the gate book troubled.”

Ser Caldus Wren asks what sort of business required that.

Jorren looks toward the Pig Lantern.

> “The kind that didn’t come back to complain.”

That changes the yard.

The storming did not flush Soot out.

It arrived after he had already fled.

Beringer says nothing for a long moment.

Then:

> “Who warned him?”

No one answers.

That is answer enough.

# What Was Found

The findings come in clusters.

Not as one dramatic revelation.

That would be too merciful.

Blackbar is not one crime.

It is a working system.

A toll post. A patrol stable. A vice yard. A serf trap. A false-law engine. A place where every man can say he only did his part and hope nobody notices that all the parts assembled into a monster.

## In the Writ Office

The writ office is the evil paperwork chapel.

Ser Edrast’s men recover:

- Lists of travelers detained
- Names of serfs returned
- Names of serfs transferred
- Names with no return mark
- Debt lodging forms
- Transport fee tallies
- Protective custody papers
- Parish transfer notes
- False surety forms
- Toll records with coded additions
- Burned scraps from the night before

The coin records are mostly intact.

The names were targeted.

That tells Beringer more than any confession.

## In Osric Writhe's Materials

**Osric Writhe**, scrivener and bondman, is found pale, ink-stained, and trying to become furniture.

He has three ledgers.

One clean.

One working.

One filthy enough to hang a county with, provided the right noble can be made to read.

Recovered from his table, stool, and satchel:

- Clean public ledger of toll forms and copied writs
- Working ledger of patrol deliveries and Pig Lantern assignments
- Blackmail ledger naming soldiers, merchants, clerks, toll guards, and private favors
- Loose slips hidden under the writing table
- Rolled pages inside a hollow sand shaker
- One sealed packet already prepared for flight
- Wax tabs with private marks matching several market accounts

Osric keeps saying:

> “I write what men tell me. Ink has no conscience, my lord.”

Ser Cadran answers:

> “Good. Then you can tell us who gave it orders.”

Osric does not like that.

No one cares.

## In Harl Brunt's Stall

**Harl Brunt** is conscious when they bring him in.

He had to be put down after reaching for a hammer.

He is now missing two teeth and several illusions about being respected.

His stall contains:

- Human-sized ankle cuffs labeled as foal hobbles
- Collar rivets
- Repair ticket for three girl irons and one boy collar
- Chain links stamped with Brunt’s mark
- Broken shackle with blood in the hinge
- Pig Lantern hinge pins made to lock from the outside
- A custom order from Brennart Soot
- Small lock blanks hidden beneath horse tack

Harl says:

> “I sell iron. Men choose what to fasten with it.”

Ser Marten Vey looks at him with open disgust.

> “You marked children’s irons as stable goods.”

Harl says nothing after that.

A rare improvement.

## In Cuthred Scarth's Stall

**Cuthred Scarth**, salvage merchant, sits bound beside crates of goods that no honest road should have produced.

Recovered:

- Boots cut down to fit smaller feet
- Bundles tagged with village marks
- Prayer beads sorted by shrine
- Ribbons, rings, family charms
- A child’s cloak
- Walking sticks with initials shaved off
- Pouches of tokens kept separate from common stock
- Finder’s fee slips from Black Spur patrolmen

Cuthred insists:

> “Road takes. I sell what’s left.”

Lyosha hears that and almost laughs.

Not because it is funny.

Because there are only so many ways a soul can rot before language starts limping.

## In Mara Quill's Book

**Mara Quill** is not brave.

That is useful.

She looks at the yard, looks at Mother Salla, looks at the knights, and decides survival has acquired a new shape.

Her measuring book contains:

- Name used
- Real name if known
- Height
- Hair
- Marks
- Temper
- Size
- Replacement clothes
- Lantern room
- Assigned status
- Notes on who needed to look respectable for inspections

One page has the word assigned written beside three names.

Another lists prices for replacing torn dresses after rough custom.

Mara says:

> “People need clothes, my lord. Even unfortunate people.”

Sir Avel replies:

> “You dressed captivity as employment.”

Mara has no answer.

This may be the wisest thing she does all day.

## In the Pig Lantern

The Pig Lantern itself is worse than the market.

The market lies.

The Pig Lantern makes the lies profitable.

Found inside:

- Rear rooms locked from the outside
- Shutters hooked from the outside
- A sickroom with restraints hidden beneath bedding
- Purses and tokens kept in a locked cabinet
- Gambling debts pinned behind the bar
- Two names scratched into the underside of a bedframe
- A blood-washed drain in the back hall
- A narrow hidden way to the refuse yard
- Pelorite and Yondallan tokens hidden behind stacked linen
- A ledger of room assignments disguised as laundry rotation

That last shelf breaks Lyosha more than the drain.

The hidden holy tokens.

Even here, people tried to smuggle in decency.

Of course they did.

People are disgusting.

People are also impossible to give up on.

## In the Clay Pit

At the clay pit behind the post, the searchers find:

- Burned paper scraps
- Broken shackle links
- Old ash
- Animal bones
- Bones not yet identified
- Cloth fragments
- A rusted collar plate
- One small copper sun-disk pressed into the mud
- Additional scraps of grey-white priestly robe wool
- Yellow stitching consistent with Pelorite travel vestments
- Blood-stiff cloth embedded in clay near the pit edge

No one says the worst part loudly.

Lyosha hears it anyway.

# Prisoner Categories

Beringer’s central order is simple:

> “Men who stood under command will be treated as soldiers. Men who hid crimes behind command will be treated as criminals.”

That becomes the spine of the whole handling.

## Ordinary Garrison Soldiers

The ordinary garrison men are ashamed, defensive, and afraid of being lumped in with Soot’s filth.

A few start talking once they understand Beringer means to separate soldiers from criminals.

They give practical information:

- Which rooms were used by Black Spur
- Which clerks kept copies
- Which patrolmen rode with Soot
- Which men were assigned to the Pig Lantern doors
- Which nights the yard was worse than usual
- Which market people handled payments
- Which gate books were honest and which were theatre

One wall guard says:

> “I didn’t touch the girls. I didn’t.”

No one asked him yet.

That is written down.

Another says:

> “Soot had his own book.”

That is written down faster.

## Black Spur Patrolmen

The Black Spur men stay quiet longer.

They look at each other too much.

They try to measure which names are already known.

One mutters that he was under orders.

Ser Edrast answers before Beringer can:

> “Then you will have the comfort of naming the officer who ordered you to sell a bound woman.”

That shuts him up.

For a while.

A Black Spur man named **Tovin Grale** breaks first.

Not from guilt.

From arithmetic.

He names Garric Tallow.

He names Merrit Churn.

He confirms Soot took the north ditch road before dawn.

He says there was a warning.

He says he does not know from whom.

No one believes the last part.

## Clerks and Toll Men

The clerks fold fastest.

This is one of the few dependable mercies of civilization: the men who ruin lives with ink often discover their spines are decorative.

**Pell Orwick**, called Three Tabs, insists he only kept tallies.

Then he insists Soot controlled the patrol books.

Then he insists Alric stripped the post.

Then he asks whether cooperation will be remembered.

Beringer says:

> “Yes.”

Pell relaxes.

Beringer finishes:

> “So will delay.”

Pell starts talking properly.

## Market Prisoners

The market toughs try to become civilians instantly.

A fascinating transformation.

One moment they are hard men defending the yard, and the next they are humble ostlers, cousins, hired hands, washer-boys, and “just here for wages.”

Nature is amazing.

Cowardice, doubly so.

Rook Marran pretends to be too stupid to understand his own coded drink board.

Cuthred Scarth pretends salvage has no source.

Harl Brunt pretends iron has no purpose.

Mara Quill pretends clothing is charity.

Mother Salla gives no names.

Not yet.

She watches who talks.

That is more useful than speech.

# Vianola Steps In

Vianola Erinleaf comes to Lyosha while he is still working near the cookhouse wall.

She does not rush.

That is the first thing he notices.

No fluttering panic. No noble outrage. No useless hand-wringing dressed up as compassion. She walks through the mud with her skirts lifted just enough to keep from tripping, face composed, eyes moving.

Counting.

Sorting.

Reading the yard the way a battlefield mage reads angles of fire.

Then she looks toward the prisoners.

Only for a moment.

Her face goes pale.

Not frightened.

Worse.

Controlled.

Terrible.

As if some cold, precise part of her has just begun assigning every kneeling man a place in a ledger no court will ever see.

Then she turns back to Lyosha.

The mask settles.

> “Father.”

Lyosha glances up from binding a torn wrist.

> “Professor Erinleaf.”

She looks at the rescued people, then at the priests, then at the soldiers hovering too close because they want to help and have no idea how. That, somehow, makes them more dangerous.

> “I can assist.”

A beat.

> “Not command. Assist.”

That matters.

Lyosha hears it.

He nods once toward the people under blankets.

> “Water first. Then heat. Then distance.”

Vianola looks toward the guards.

> “How much distance?”

Lyosha says:

> “Enough that no one has to look up and see armor unless they choose to.”

Vianola turns immediately.

Her voice stays calm.

> “You three. Back ten paces. No, not there. You are still in the doorway. If you can see both exits, so can they. Move.”

The guards move.

One starts to object.

Vianola looks at him.

He decides retirement sounds nice and moves faster.

Lyosha almost smiles.

> “You’ve done this before?”

> “No.”

She looks over the gathered victims again.

> “But I have seen rooms after powerful men finished using them.”

That sits between them.

Neither dresses it up.

Vianola kneels beside Lyosha, careful not to crowd the woman he is treating.

> “Tell me how you want them sorted.”

Lyosha answers softly.

> “Not by usefulness.”

Vianola’s jaw tightens.

> “No.”

Then, just as softly:

> “By harm.”

That is close enough.

Together they begin.

Not names.

Not testimony.

Not yet.

First, who cannot be touched.

Who cannot be left alone.

Who keeps looking at the door.

Who is bleeding under the blanket and hiding it.

Who is trying to help because if they stop moving they will break.

Who can carry water.

Who should be given a task.

Who should be given silence.

Vianola is very good at that last part.

She can look at someone and know whether a question would open them or ruin them.

She points lightly, never with command in her voice when speaking to the rescued.

> “She can sit with the older woman. Do not separate them.”

> “That boy needs a wall behind him.”

> “The red-haired girl is listening to everything. She may know names. Not now.”

> “That one should not be asked anything today.”

Lyosha gives her a look.

Vianola accepts it.

> “Tomorrow, then.”

Lyosha says:

> “Maybe.”

Vianola inclines her head.

> “Maybe.”

That is her deference.

Not weakness.

Choice.

A little later, one of the rescued women starts shaking so hard the cup in her hands rattles against her teeth.

Vianola takes the cup without touching her fingers.

> “You do not have to drink because he gave it to you.”

The woman looks up.

Vianola holds the cup where she can see it.

> “You may drink because you are thirsty.”

The woman stares at her.

Then drinks.

Lyosha watches Vianola for half a breath.

She does not look back.

She is already scanning the yard again.

The prisoners.

The clerks.

Mother Salla.

Her face goes pale once more.

Then calm.

That is somehow worse.

# What Lyosha Does Not Let Happen

Lyosha does not contradict Beringer in the yard.

But later, when Beringer has a moment, Lyosha says:

> “Do not let them turn the rescued into evidence before they are people.”

Beringer is silent for a long breath.

Then:

> “I know.”

Lyosha looks toward the Pig Lantern.

> “No. You know it in command. I am asking you to know it in practice.”

That gets through.

Beringer calls Sir Avel over.

> “No questioning the rescued without Lyosha or another priest present. Food first. Water. Blankets. Names only if they offer them.”

Avel nods once.

> “Good.”

Then, because he is still a paladin and therefore legally required to be intense at breakfast, he asks:

> “And after?”

Beringer looks over the yard.

> “After, we take the place apart plank by plank.”

# Immediate Orders

Beringer gives the main orders in the middle of the yard, where prisoners, knights, clerks, and rescued captives can all hear the shape of them.

> “All soldiers are to remain counted by unit.”

> “Black Spur men are to be held apart.”

> “Clerks, toll men, and quartermaster staff are under guard.”

> “No one enters the writ office, Pig Lantern office, Brunt’s stall, or the clay pit without escort.”

> “No fire is lit without leave.”

> “Any man who strikes a bound prisoner answers to me.”

A pause.

> “Any man who harms a captive, witness, or civilian answers as a criminal.”

He looks at the kneeling rows.

> “Men who stood under command will be treated as soldiers.”

Then his eyes shift to the Black Spur men.

> “Men who hid crimes behind command will be treated as criminals.”

That is the line.

Everyone hears it.

Some men are relieved.

Some men are doomed.

Some men are smart enough to know they do not yet know which.

# Orders Concerning Brennart Soot

Soot’s flight changes the military problem.

He is not a loose end from the battle.

He is a man who had warning.

Beringer’s orders are narrow and cold:

> “Light riders. No banners.”

> “Find the north ditch road.”

> “Find where three horses passed after moonrise.”

> “Find who fed them, hid them, ferried them, or lied about seeing them.”

> “Do not chase blindly into prepared ground.”

Ser Caldus Wren asks:

> “If he has gone to ground?”

Beringer looks toward the Pig Lantern.

> “Then we dig.”

Sir Avel adds:

> “Alive, if possible.”

Beringer nods.

> “Alive, if possible.”

No one pretends that is mercy.

It is utility.

Soot knows names.

Soot knows who warned him.

Soot knows whether Alric stripped Blackbar and left it to burn, or whether someone higher wanted the smoke seen from miles away.

Dead men confess badly.

Annoying, but true.

---

# Alric's Shadow

Alric DeSalle is not present in the yard.

That does not mean he is absent.

His shadow is everywhere.

In the missing horses.

In the stripped surgeon’s chest.

In the absent remounts.

In the soldiers left behind who still look toward the road as if rescue might arrive late and apologetic.

In Cadric Vane’s silence.

In the fact that the post was left strong enough to delay pursuit and weak enough to be expendable.

Beringer does not say this aloud in front of the prisoners.

Not yet.

But Ser Caldus Wren says it plainly enough when the officers gather near the stable wall:

> “Alric took the teeth and left the gums to bleed.”

No one corrects him.

Ser Edrast adds:

> “If he knew what this place was, he abandoned witnesses.”

Ser Cadran says:

> “If he did not know, he abandoned command.”

That is the shape of it.

Neither answer is clean.

Beringer looks at the post.

> “Then we will learn which failure belongs to him.”

# Mother Salla Under Guard

Mother Salla remains kneeling longer than most.

Not because she is forgotten.

Because no one sensible wants her moved without purpose.

When she is finally hauled up, she sways once but catches herself.

She looks at Beringer.

> “Do you imagine this place ends because you took my door?”

Beringer answers:

> “No.”

That surprises her.

Only a little.

He continues:

> “I imagine it ends because men who thought themselves hidden discover they were counted.”

Salla looks toward Osric.

Then toward Rook.

Then toward the Black Spur men.

Then back to Beringer.

> “You’ll need me.”

Lyosha, standing near enough to hear, says:

> “That may be the saddest true thing anyone says today.”

Salla almost smiles.

Almost.

# Sir Avel's Judgment

Sir Avel Stormcairn walks the yard after the first sorting.

He does not preach.

That would be easier.

Instead, he watches.

At the holding cage, he removes one gauntlet and touches the iron ring set into the post.

There is dried blood in the join.

He studies it for a long moment.

Ser Rion Kelbray says:

> “This is not war.”

Avel answers:

> “No.”

Rion waits.

Avel gives him the rest.

> “War is cleaner when men wish to excuse it. This is law made into a knife and handed to cowards.”

Rion looks toward Mother Salla.

> “And what do we do with cowards?”

Avel turns.

> “We do not become them.”

That should sound simple.

It does not.

# Lyosha Among the Rescued

Lyosha does not make speeches.

He passes water.

He asks permission before touching wounds.

He lets one girl keep the blanket over her head.

He lets another curse him for being a priest.

He tells the guards to move farther away.

He tells Tansy Reed to keep the soup warm and thin.

He tells Old Bessa Marn she can sit.

Bessa says:

> “If I sit, I won’t get back up.”

Lyosha says:

> “Then sit where you can stay awhile.”

She does.

Eventually, she talks.

Not dramatically.

Not all at once.

One name.

Then another.

Then another.

Like pulling hooks out of cloth.

> “He wore blue laces.”

> “The red-haired one paid extra.”

> “Soot came on rain nights.”

> “Mara knew their real names.”

> “Osric wrote them different after.”

> “There was a girl with a blue sleeve.”

Bessa’s hands twist in her apron.

> “I kept it. I don’t know why.”

Lyosha does not ask why.

He knows why.

# Ser Odran Blackreed's Report

Beringer is still near the stable wall when the Draal Moor-ranger appears.

No one sees him arrive.

One moment there is only Beringer, Kat at his shoulder, Ser Caldus with the horse tally, and two guards arguing quietly over whether a broken gate hinge counts as evidence or carpentry.

The next moment there is a bearded man standing just behind Beringer’s right shoulder.

Hood up.

Cloak wet.

Mail dark under the mud.

A longbow across his back and a short sword at his hip.

His beard is black streaked with grey, full enough to hide half his face, but not his eyes.

Nothing hides those.

Draal eyes.

Moor-ranger eyes.

The kind that make a man feel he has been tracked since breakfast.

Kat notices him first.

Her shield hand tightens.

The ranger inclines his head by half an inch.

> “Dame Kendal.”

Beringer closes his eyes for the smallest possible moment.

Not surprise.

Recognition of a familiar nuisance.

> “Ser Odran.”

The man is **Ser Odran Blackreed**, knight of Drakemoor and one of the Draal Moor-rangers. Technically a knight, because Gorundia insists on putting titles on men who would clearly prefer to be mistaken for weather.

He looks toward the palisade.

> “Two went through a gap west of the refuse cut.”

Beringer turns.

> “Soot?”

> “No.”

Odran says it flatly.

No apology.

No drama.

> “Smaller fish.”

Ser Caldus exhales through his nose.

> “Alive?”

Odran considers that.

> “One.”

Beringer waits.

Odran continues:

> “First made open ground. Bad choice. Hussars saw him.”

A beat.

> “He became sport.”

No one asks for details.

This is because everyone present has, at some point, met hussars.

Kat’s expression does not change, but she looks briefly toward the road.

Lyosha, passing close enough to hear, says mildly:

> “I assume that means dead.”

Odran looks at him.

> “Eventually.”

Lyosha keeps walking.

> “Helpful clarification.”

Beringer’s mouth almost moves.

Almost.

> “And the second?”

Odran gestures with two fingers toward the holding cage.

> “In irons. Name given as **Hewit Nale**. Black Spur hanger-on. Claims he ran because he was frightened.”

Ser Caldus mutters:

> “Refreshing honesty.”

Odran says:

> “He is lying.”

Beringer looks back to him.

> “About which part?”

> “All of it, likely.”

That earns the ranger a brief silence.

Odran reaches into his cloak and produces a strip of torn leather, a buckle, and a folded scrap of oiled cloth.

He offers them to Beringer.

> “He had this under his shirt.”

Beringer does not take it.

He nods to Ser Cadran Oathmere.

> “Witness and bag it.”

Cadran steps in with cloth and seal-string.

Odran continues:

> “Leather matches a satchel strap. Oiled cloth smells of lamp oil and ink. Not his. Too careful.”

Kat says quietly:

> “Soot’s?”

Odran’s eyes shift to her.

> “Could be.”

Then back to Beringer.

> “Or someone wanted him carrying a piece of Soot.”

That lands unpleasantly.

A tiny little gift from the universe, wrapped in paranoia. Naturally.

Beringer looks toward the Black Spur prisoners.

> “Where is Hewit now?”

Odran says:

> “Separated. Mouth intact. Pride less so.”

Beringer gives him a dry look.

> “You questioned him?”

> “No.”

A beat.

> “I discouraged further travel.”

Kat looks like she might approve and is trying not to.

Beringer says:

> “Bring him to Cadran. No one speaks to him alone.”

Odran nods.

Then, because apparently the moors produce men who consider full conversations a moral failing, he steps back.

Not far.

Just enough that when Beringer looks away and looks back, Ser Odran Blackreed is somehow already beside the palisade gap, speaking to no one and seeing everything.

Ser Caldus watches him go.

> “Does he always do that?”

Kat says:

> “Appear?”

Beringer says:

> “Report.”

Lyosha, from the cookhouse wall:

> “Brood.”

No one argues.

# Final Yard Status at Sunset

By sunset, Blackbar is held.

Not pacified.

Held.

There is a difference.

Anyone who ignores that difference should be made a duke, since apparently that is where kingdoms store that kind of thinking.

The ordinary soldiers are under guard by unit.

The Black Spur prisoners are in irons.

The clerks are isolated.

The market suspects are bound separately.

Mother Salla is under direct watch, still bleeding, still upright, still dangerous.

Osric Writhe is alive, protected from everyone who wants him dead, which currently includes almost everyone with a conscience and several without.

Harl Brunt has stopped talking.

Cuthred Scarth has started sweating.

Mara Quill has asked twice whether cooperation protects her.

Rook Marran has offered names, coin, and drink in that order.

Old Bessa Marn is asleep sitting upright beside the cookhouse wall.

Tansy Reed is making soup and insulting anyone who tries to help badly.

The rescued people are inside a guarded storehouse with blankets, water, priests, and no questions unless they choose to answer.

Brennart Soot remains at large.

Garric Tallow and Merrit Churn remain with him.

Someone warned them.

The knights are angry enough to do something stupid, which is why Beringer keeps giving them useful tasks.

Lyosha keeps finding people who need water.

That does more for the Crown’s case than any speech.

Because when the story of Blackbar travels, it will not be:

> They stormed a fort.

It will be:

> They opened the rooms.

And that is worse for the rebels than any battlefield defeat.
