# The Bridge Toll

The bridge was narrow, old, and perfectly serviceable.

Which meant someone had naturally decided it required a knight.

He waited at the center in polished mail beneath a blue surcoat, one gauntleted hand resting on the pommel of his sword. Behind him stood two bored men-at-arms, a skinny squire, and a painted board announcing:

> **TOLL BY RIGHT OF SIR ALDREN MANDOR**  
> **HORSE: TWO SILVER**  
> **CART: FIVE SILVER**  
> **NOBLE TRAFFIC: AS ASSESSED**

Aylassa reined in.

Jessa stopped beside her.

The knight raised one hand.

“Halt.”

Aylassa looked at the bridge.

Then at the sign.

Then at the knight.

“Marvelous,” she said. “A talking obstruction.”

Sir Aldren’s expression tightened.

“This bridge is held under my protection.”

“Is it in danger?”

“It is maintained by my men.”

Jessa looked down at the planks.

One had been replaced recently.

The others had not.

Aylassa leaned slightly in the saddle.

“How many men?”

“That is not your concern.”

“I ask because I can see four, and only one appears acquainted with work.”

The squire looked away quickly.

Sir Aldren drew himself straighter.

“You will pay the toll.”

“Certainly.”

Jessa turned her head.

Only slightly.

Aylassa smiled brightly.

“Receipt, please.”

The knight paused.

“What?”

“A receipt. With your seal, the authority under which the toll is collected, the duration of that authority, and the office to which disputes may be submitted.”

Sir Aldren stared at her.

Jessa said, very quietly, “My lady.”

“Yes?”

“He has no seal pouch.”

Aylassa’s smile widened.

“Oh.”

Sir Aldren’s hand dropped more firmly onto his sword.

“You question my honor?”

“No,” Aylassa said. “I question your paperwork. Honor is much harder to verify and usually less interesting.”

“You stand on lands held by House Mandor.”

“Do I?”

“Yes.”

Jessa looked toward the weathered boundary stone beside the road.

The carved mark had been chiseled partly away.

Underneath the fresh blue paint, another device remained visible.

A sheaf of wheat.

Not Mandor.

Jessa inclined her head toward it.

Aylassa followed her glance.

Sir Aldren noticed too late.

“Interesting,” Aylassa said.

The knight drew his sword.

His men-at-arms did not.

This was the first sensible decision anyone had made.

Aylassa looked at the blade.

Then at Jessa.

“Would you say drawing steel constitutes an alteration in the toll schedule?”

“Yes, my lady.”

“A surcharge?”

“Possibly.”

Sir Aldren stepped forward.

“I will not be mocked by some painted elf whore playing noble.”

Silence.

The squire closed his eyes.

One of the men-at-arms muttered, “Sir.”

Jessa’s hands unfolded.

Aylassa dismounted.

Not hurriedly.

She handed Jessa her reins.

“Hold these.”

“Yes, my lady.”

Sir Aldren lifted his sword.

Aylassa moved.

There was a short metallic sound, a twist of steel, and then Sir Aldren’s sword was lying several yards away.

He staggered.

Aylassa did not.

Her rapier rested lightly against the hollow of his throat.

Sir Aldren froze.

Aylassa smiled.

“Let us begin again.”

He breathed carefully.

Aylassa tapped the painted sign with the toe of one boot.

“You have no posted charter, no visible seal, no recognized boundary, and no receipt book.”

Sir Aldren said nothing.

“Your men do not believe in you.”

One of the men-at-arms coughed into his fist.

“Your squire appears embarrassed on behalf of the entire institution of knighthood.”

The boy looked down.

“And your bridge,” Aylassa continued, “was repaired by someone who knows how to use a hammer, which excludes everyone currently standing on it.”

Jessa examined the replacement plank.

“There is a carpenter’s mark, my lady. Sweetbarrow work.”

Aylassa’s expression brightened.

“Oh, wonderful. Guaco may have relatives.”

Sir Aldren swallowed against the point at his throat.

“What do you want?”

“A receipt.”

“I have none.”

“Yes. We established that.”

Her smile sharpened.

“So instead, you will refund every traveler you can still find, remove this sign, and spend the next week repairing the bridge under the direction of whoever actually owns the carpenter’s mark.”

His face reddened.

“I am a knight.”

“Then this will be an excellent opportunity to encounter service.”

“You cannot order me.”

Aylassa considered that.

Then she lowered her rapier.

Sir Aldren mistook this for improvement.

He straightened.

“You have assaulted a knight of House Mandor on his own road. Whatever petty title you imagine yourself to possess, you will answer for—”

Aylassa caught his right wrist.

He barely had time to look down.

Her dagger flashed.

Something small struck the wooden toll box with a wet tap.

Sir Aldren screamed.

His sword-hand jerked back against his chest.

The first finger was gone.

Aylassa looked into the toll box.

Then at him.

“Two silver per horse seemed excessive.”

She tilted her head.

“One digit feels more proportionate.”

Sir Aldren stared at her, white-faced.

Jessa looked into the box.

“My lady.”

“Yes?”

“I do not believe that is legal tender.”

“Neither was his toll.”

One of the men-at-arms turned away very suddenly.

The other discovered an urgent need to inspect the river.

The squire had gone completely still.

Aylassa wiped the dagger on Sir Aldren’s blue surcoat.

“Now.”

Her voice remained cheerful.

“The refunds.”

Sir Aldren clutched his hand.

“You mad bitch.”

Aylassa’s smile became radiant.

“Careful. You are running out of tolls.”

That shut him up.

Jessa stepped closer.

“My lady.”

“Yes?”

“The sign should remain.”

Aylassa glanced at her.

“Why?”

“For correction.”

Jessa took the paint pot from beside the board, turned it over, and found a half-dry brush.

She handed both to the squire.

The boy looked at her.

Then at Sir Aldren.

Then, after the smallest imaginable hesitation, began painting over the old words.

Aylassa watched with interest.

“What are we writing?”

Jessa answered without expression.

> **BRIDGE REPAIR BY ORDER OF LOCAL AUTHORITY**  
> **NO TOLL**  
> **SIR ALDREN MANDOR CURRENTLY LEARNING THE DIFFERENCE**

The squire’s mouth twitched.

Sir Aldren made a strangled noise.

Aylassa beamed.

“Oh, Jessa.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“You are becoming funny.”

“No, my lady.”

“No?”

“I am becoming tired.”

They crossed the bridge twenty minutes later.

Behind them, Sir Aldren Mandor sat on an overturned toll box with his hand bandaged while his squire held a replacement plank in place.

One of the men-at-arms was hammering.

Badly.

Aylassa looked back once.

“Do you think he will improve?”

“No, my lady.”

“Do you think the bridge will?”

“Yes, my lady.”

Aylassa nodded.

“Then justice remains achievable.”

She rode another few yards.

Then glanced at Jessa.

“Do remind me to ask Beringer whether compulsory bridge repair counts as public service.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“And the finger?”

Jessa looked straight ahead.

“I suspect Your Ladyship has already classified that separately.”

Aylassa smiled.

“Excellent. You really are learning.”
