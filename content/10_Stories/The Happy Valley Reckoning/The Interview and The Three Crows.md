

## The Interview

The footman vanished.

Aylassa watched the door close.

“You already knew whom to ask.”

“I knew whom not to waste time asking,” Dwalric said.

“That is nearly an answer.”

“It is the useful part of one.”

Before she could reply, the door opened again.

Jessa Ardell entered without hurry.

She wore dark service green, plain enough for work and fitted well enough to prove someone in the household understood that dignity was also tailoring. Her hair was bound neatly back. Her collar was correct. Her apron was clean. She crossed the room with the controlled quiet of a woman trained not to disturb a house that heard everything.

Another noble might have seen broad shoulders, lean arms, and practical hands, then settled the matter comfortably in his own mind: peasant stock, farm-raised, polished later by better people.

Aylassa did not settle matters comfortably.

She watched the way Jessa moved.

No wasted step. No decorative softness. No wandering gaze. The woman entered as a servant, but her eyes had already measured the table, the door, the covered mirror, Dwalric’s cane, the knife beside Aylassa’s plate, and the distance between herself and every man in the room.

Interesting.

Then Aylassa’s attention dropped to Jessa’s hands.

They were folded before her in the proper household manner. Modest. Still. Serviceable.

Almost.

The pose was correct enough to pass before a countess, a priest, or a sleepy merchant with too much wine in him. But beneath the discipline, beneath the polish, there was an older habit. The wrists sat too near each other. The fingers rested as if they remembered being denied their own reach. Not frightened. Not broken.

Remembering.

And the hands themselves were not servant’s hands, though they had done servant’s work. The knuckles were thickened. The fingers had been broken and healed around usefulness. The tendons stood clean beneath the skin. These were hands that could fold linen, pour wine, button a gown, and close around a throat with the same terrible economy.

Aylassa smiled.

Not brightly. Not falsely. Not with pity, which would have been insulting and therefore probably dangerous.

It was a small, genuine smile. The sort given when one professional recognizes another and chooses not to announce it to the room.

Jessa saw it.

Of course she did.

Her expression did not change.

But her eyes paused on Aylassa half a heartbeat longer than service required.

Dwalric stood.

“Jessa Ardell.”

“Master Stonefoot.”

“Lady Aylassa requires a travelling maid for a private matter outside the house. The work may involve danger, deception, distressed persons, and men who have mistaken power for permission.”

Jessa’s gaze moved to Aylassa fully now.

No flinch.

No eagerness either.

Good.

Dwalric continued.

“You are not commanded.”

“I understand.”

“No servant of this house is ordered into private danger.”

“I understand that as well.”

Aylassa leaned back slightly, still studying her.

“I do not know exactly what I am riding into.”

“No, my lady?”

“No. Which is inconsiderate of it. But I suspect ugly rooms, frightened people, bad law, worse men, and enough lies to make the walls sweat.”

Jessa absorbed this.

“Children?”

Aylassa’s smile faded.

“I do not know.”

That answer mattered more than a quicker one would have.

Jessa lowered her eyes for a moment. Not submission. Thought.

Then she looked back up.

“When does my lady intend to leave?”

Dwalric did not move.

Aylassa did not answer immediately.

Instead, she looked once more at Jessa’s folded hands.

Then at her face.

“After I have paid proper respects,” Aylassa said. “And after you decide whether you wish to come.”

Jessa’s hands remained folded.

Perfectly maid-like.

Perfectly dangerous.

“Yes, my lady.”

Aylassa tilted her head.

“Is that agreement or acknowledgment?”

For the first time, something almost like dry humor touched Jessa’s mouth.

“Both, unless my lady prefers them separate.”

Aylassa laughed once.

Soft. Delighted.

“Oh, I am going to like you.”

Jessa inclined her head.

“If my lady says so.”

Dwalric’s expression remained untouched by amusement, which fooled absolutely no one worth fooling.

## The Three Crows

By the time the lights of the coach inn appeared through the evening damp, Jessa had learned three things about Lady Aylassa.

First, she could ride well.

Second, she could notice everything while appearing to notice nothing.

Third, she could talk without any visible need for breath.

This was not complaint. Complaint required surprise, and Jessa had stopped being surprised somewhere around the second hill, when Aylassa had moved from court gossip to inheritance law to the failings of poets who compared women to moons without once considering how many things the moon had done badly.

Jessa had answered when required.

“Yes, my lady.”

“No, my lady.”

“If my lady says so.”

“As my lady wishes.”

These had carried her safely through three hours, two anecdotes about duels, one story involving a bishop, a chandelier, and an escaped goat, and a brief but intense lecture on why mediocre men should not be allowed metaphors.

Aylassa did not seem offended by the spare replies.

If anything, she seemed encouraged.

The inn was called The Three Crows, because apparently one crow had not sounded unlucky enough and two lacked ambition. It sat at a bend in the road with yellow light in the windows, wet mud in the yard, and the tired, sour smell of horses, smoke, beer, old wool, and men who had been traveling just long enough to believe themselves interesting.

A stable boy came out before they dismounted. Thin, quick, wary. Too wary.

Jessa noticed the bruise fading along his jaw.

Aylassa noticed him noticing her horse.

“Good evening,” Aylassa said brightly. “This noble beast is exhausted, judgemental, and deeply convinced she deserved a better class of road. See that she is rubbed down, fed, and told at least one flattering lie.”

The boy blinked.

“Yes, my lady.”

“Not too flattering. She has vanity enough already.”

Jessa took the saddlebag herself.

The boy glanced at her hands.

Only once.

Good boy.

Still learning.

Aylassa swept toward the door, already speaking again.

“Now, Jessa, if the supper is turnip-based, I shall attempt bravery, but if someone has boiled cabbage in public I may have to reconsider civilization altogether.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“You disapprove of cabbage?”

“No, my lady.”

“Ah. You disapprove of public failure.”

Jessa opened the inn door.

“If my lady says so.”

“I very often do.”

“I have observed that, my lady.”

Aylassa laughed and entered first.

Of course she did.

She stepped over the threshold already talking, one hand lifted in mid-gesture, hood pushed back enough to show pale braids, sharp ears, and that impossible mismatched gaze.

“—and I told him, very politely, that if he wished to compare me to a moonlit blade again, he might first learn which end of a blade is customarily held.”

Jessa followed half a step behind, hands folded before her, chin lowered just enough for service and not enough for fear.

The room noticed Aylassa.

Rooms usually did.

Conversation dipped. Not stopped. Stopping would have admitted interest, and men in inns were very committed to pretending they had not just become audience members.

The common room took shape in Jessa’s mind by layers.

Hearth.

Bar.

Stairs.

Kitchen door.

Back hall.

Four men gambling badly near the left wall.

Two merchants by the window.

A drover with a red nose and a loose belt.

A knife under one sleeve, badly hidden.

A better knife in a boot.

The innkeeper, broad through the shoulders, with a napkin over one arm and the wary eyes of a man who had seen nobility damage furniture.

Aylassa was still smiling.

She had seen most of it too.

That was irritating.

“My lady?” the innkeeper asked, already bowing.

“Rooms, supper, hot water, and something for the horses that does not suggest revenge.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“And if supper involves cabbage, lie to me gently.”

The innkeeper’s expression twitched.

“Stew tonight, my lady.”

“An evasive answer. I respect it.”

The room warmed by half a degree. Aylassa did that. She made attention feel like amusement before anyone noticed it had become obedience.

Near the left table, the drover shifted.

Not much.

Enough.

His gaze had found Aylassa’s belt purse first, then her wrist, then the line of her throat. Men like that rarely arrived at a good idea by accident.

Jessa moved one step.

Only one.

She did not look at him directly.

She did not need to.

Her folded hands separated by the width of a breath, and for an instant the servant’s pose failed. Not visibly to the room. Not enough for alarm. Just enough for a man who had spent his life around rough hands to understand what sort of hands he was looking at.

The drover looked.

Then he looked away.

His mug became fascinating.

Aylassa, still smiling at the innkeeper, said, “Two rooms, if you please. Near enough to be respectable, far enough to discourage interpretation.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“And a private corner for supper, if you have one.”

The innkeeper glanced once past her, toward the drover who had suddenly developed a serious relationship with his ale.

“We have a small side room.”

“A wise establishment.”

“We try, my lady.”

“Trying is the first step toward either excellence or disaster. I look forward to discovering which.”

Jessa folded her hands again.

Properly.

Aylassa did not turn.

But her smile changed.

The innkeeper led them toward the stairs and side passage. Aylassa moved as though she owned the floor without needing to prove she owned the room. Jessa followed with the bag, eyes lowered, seeing everything beneath her lashes.

The serving girl near the kitchen door watched them pass.

Young.

Tired.

Left hand kept close to her apron.

Not injured from kitchen work. Too careful. Too hidden.

Jessa filed it away.

Aylassa glanced toward the girl and kept walking.

She had seen that too.

Naturally.

At the foot of the stairs, Aylassa paused.

“You moved,” she said.

Jessa stopped beside her.

“Yes, my lady.”

“Very little.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“And he decided to remain alive.”

“If my lady says so.”

Aylassa looked back, eyes bright with amusement.

“Oh, don’t be dull with me. I have been talking for half a county, and you have not once complained. I refuse to believe your mind is empty enough to be that patient.”

Jessa kept her hands folded.

“My lady has a great many observations.”

“That was almost an insult.”

“No, my lady.”

“A compliment?”

“No, my lady.”

“A field report?”

“Yes, my lady.”

Aylassa’s smile widened.

“There you are.”

Jessa said nothing.

That was usually safest.

Aylassa leaned slightly closer, lowering her voice.

“You saw the drover.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“And the serving girl.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“And the knife in the boot?”

“The sleeve first, my lady. The boot second.”

“Good. I missed the sleeve.”

“No, my lady.”

Aylassa’s brows lifted.

Jessa looked at her properly then.

“My lady chose not to look at it.”

For a moment, Aylassa was silent.

Then she laughed, softly and with real pleasure.

“Oh, Jessa.”

The maid’s expression remained perfectly correct.

Aylassa tilted her head toward the common room below.

“You stopped him without making a scene.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“Why?”

“He had not yet done anything worth bleeding over.”

“Practical.”

“And my lady did not require assistance.”

“Also practical.”

“And if he had touched me?”

Jessa’s hands remained folded.

“Then I would have waited half a breath to see whether my lady had permitted it.”

Aylassa’s smile sharpened.

“Only half?”

“Yes, my lady.”

“How flattering.”

“It was not intended as flattery.”

“Better still.”

The innkeeper waited above them, pretending not to hear.

He was very good at it.

Jessa noticed.

So did Aylassa.

They resumed walking.

Aylassa’s voice brightened again before they reached the landing.

“Now, about this stew. If there is cabbage in it, we shall need courage, diplomacy, and possibly a priest.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“And if there is no cabbage?”

“Then my lady will find another matter requiring courage.”

Aylassa looked back over her shoulder, delighted.

“You are learning.”

“No, my lady.”

“No?”

“I am surviving.”

Aylassa laughed all the way to the side room.

Behind them, in the common room, the drover kept both hands around his mug and did not look up again.

A wise man, briefly.

## A Trial Run

Later, after supper proved edible and the stew proved innocent of cabbage, the innkeeper showed them to their rooms.

Two rooms, as requested. Near enough to be respectable. Far enough to discourage interpretation.

Jessa checked both locks, both windows, both shutters, and the space beneath each bed without being asked.

Aylassa watched from the doorway.

“You are very thorough.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“Dwalric must be unbearable with doors.”

“Master Stonefoot is exacting.”

“That was a yes dressed for church.”

“It was, my lady.”

Below, The Three Crows settled into its night rhythm: mugs scraping, chairs dragging, men pretending they were not drunk, and the serving girl moving through it all with her left sleeve pulled low and her eyes trained carefully on the floor.

Aylassa looked toward the hall.

“I suppose I should sleep.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“That was not agreement.”

“No, my lady.”

Aylassa smiled. “Good night, Jessa.”

“Good night, my lady.”

Jessa stepped into her room and closed the door.

Aylassa waited.

One breath.

Two.

Three.

Then she drew her cloak around her shoulders and opened her door without a sound.

Jessa was already in the hall.

Barely a step behind.

Aylassa stopped with one hand still on the latch.

“You were awake.”

“No, my lady.”

Aylassa turned her head.

“I was waiting.”

“For me?”

“For what my lady had noticed.”

The hall was quiet except for the old timber settling and the distant stupidity of men below.

Aylassa released the latch.

“You saw her hand.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“And the jaw.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“And the way she looked toward the kitchen before answering the innkeeper.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“You might have mentioned it.”

“My lady had already seen it.”

Aylassa’s smile became small and genuine.

“There you are.”

They went downstairs together.

---

By morning, The Three Crows was quieter than it had been the night before.

No one had died.

This seemed to disappoint no one sensible.

The common room had been swept. The hearth had been fed. The innkeeper stood too straight behind his counter, pale around the mouth, with the exhausted dignity of a man who had discovered that shame could be itemized.

His brother was not present.

Neither Aylassa nor Jessa remarked on this.

A folded statement lay drying beside the ledger, written in the innkeeper’s own hand and dictated in Aylassa’s pleasantest voice. It named his brother Merrit. It named the keys. It named the threats, the false accusations, the bruises, and the girls who had been made to understand that silence was part of their wages.

The nearest lawful authority had been sent for before dawn.

Tamsin had gone south in a hired cart with a cloak around her shoulders, a bag of coin in her lap, and instructions clear enough that even fear would have difficulty improving on them.

Aylassa paid for the rooms, the supper, the hot water, the horses, and a pot of honeyed tea no one remembered ordering. She placed the coins down one at a time, then set another folded note beside them.

“For the girl,” she said.

The innkeeper looked at the note as though it might bite.

“It will reach her.”

“Yes,” Aylassa replied pleasantly. “It will.”

Near the kitchen passage, the older cook stood with both hands folded in her apron and her jaw set hard enough to crack walnuts. She gave Jessa the smallest nod.

Jessa returned it.

That seemed to matter.

Outside, the horses steamed in the cold damp. The road bent away from the inn, grey and rutted, carrying them onward toward Happy Valley and whatever civilized horrors had learned to use keys instead of chains.

They rode for some time in silence.

Or rather, Jessa rode in silence.

Aylassa permitted nearly seven full minutes of it before saying, “That was handled with admirable restraint.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“I mean by everyone.”

“No, my lady.”

Aylassa glanced over.

Jessa kept her eyes on the road.

“The innkeeper cooperated once he understood the alternatives. The girl spoke once she knew she would not be alone afterward. The brother surrendered his keys when asked.”

“When asked,” Aylassa repeated.

“Yes, my lady.”

“How delicately you phrase things.”

“I was trained in a refined house.”

Aylassa’s mouth curved.

“And were you satisfied?”

Jessa was quiet long enough that the horse’s hooves filled the space between them.

“No, my lady.”

Aylassa nodded.

“Neither was I.”

They rode another stretch beneath wet branches and low morning cloud.

Then Jessa said, “My lady was kinder than she wished to be.”

Aylassa did not answer at once.

When she did, her voice was lighter than her face.

“No. I was angrier than I could afford to be.”

Jessa turned her head then.

For the first time since leaving Taur-Endorath, she smiled.

Not much.

Barely enough to trouble the discipline of her face.

But enough.

Aylassa saw it, of course.

She brightened immediately.

“Oh, good. You do have one.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“Rare?”

“Carefully rationed.”

“How wise. Smiles are wasted on most people.”

“Yes, my lady.”

They rode on.

Behind them, The Three Crows grew smaller in the morning mist.

Ahead, Happy Valley waited with its clean names, quiet houses, and all the little lies people tell when they have learned no one is coming.
