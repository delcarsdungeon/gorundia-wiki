# Breakfast at Larkmeadow

Sir Cadric Vayneholt had disliked Lady Silvershire from the moment she arrived.

This was unfair.

She had been perfectly charming.

That was the problem.

She came up the drive in finery with one maid and no escort, smiling as though Larkmeadow were exactly what she had expected to find. Oswin met her at the steps. Ysabet came down ten minutes later. Malrec watched from the gallery.

Cadric had watched from the breakfast room.

No one had introduced them.

Nobody needed to.

The landlady had come home.

By supper, everyone knew.

Not officially. Larkmeadow did nothing officially unless there was money in it.

But doors had begun closing.

Servants spoke less.

Oswin disappeared into his office with the ledgers.

Ysabet burned something in her room and claimed it was old correspondence.

Perrin drank too much and spent half an hour explaining to anyone who would listen that there was _nothing unlawful_ about private hospitality between persons of rank.

No one had accused him of anything.

That made it worse.

Lady Silvershire herself remained pleasant.

She asked about the orchards.

She complimented dinner.

She asked one guest whether he preferred Brightwake before or after wine.

The gentleman nearly dropped his glass.

Then she laughed.

Softly.

As though she had merely caught him cheating at cards.

Her maid was worse.

Jessa Ardell scarcely spoke at all.

She moved through the house carrying linen, hot water, trays, keys.

Cadric caught her once standing outside the lower servants’ passage.

Not listening.

Just standing.

He had known enough dangerous men in his life to recognize the difference.

That night there were noises.

Not many.

A door closing sharply.

Footsteps overhead where nobody should have been walking.

Something heavy struck wood once.

Then silence.

Cadric had lain awake for perhaps an hour afterward.

He told himself it was Malrec.

Malrec was dealing with it.

Malrec dealt with things.

That was why Malrec was there.

Morning came grey and wet.

No bells rang for breakfast.

That was the first thing Cadric noticed.

The second was the servant outside his room.

An older halfling man stood there with a covered tray.

“Sir Cadric.”

Cadric looked at the tray.

“I didn’t order anything.”

“No, sir.”

The halfling lifted the cover.

There was no breakfast underneath.

Only a folded card.

Heavy cream stock.

Silver edging.

Cadric did not touch it.

“What is that?”

“Invitation, sir.”

“To what?”

The halfling’s expression remained cheerfully empty.

“The south veranda.”

Cadric stared at him.

“From whom?”

That was when the old bastard smiled.

“Her ladyship.”

---

Perrin was already in the corridor when Cadric emerged.

So was Ysabet.

Dame Ysabet Corven had dressed too quickly. Cadric knew because one earring was missing.

She noticed him noticing.

Neither mentioned it.

Perrin adjusted his cuffs.

“Have either of you seen Malrec?”

“No,” Ysabet said.

Too quickly.

Cadric looked at her.

She lowered her voice.

“There was some disturbance last night.”

Perrin swallowed.

“What kind?”

Ysabet’s eyes flicked toward the servant ten paces behind them.

“A household matter.”

Of course.

A household matter.

That was what they called everything until someone needed paying.

They descended together.

Two other guests joined them near the stairs. Lady Rosienne was already ahead, wrapped in a dark morning shawl, her expression unreadable. Mira Vell stood near the receiving room door.

Mira would not look at Cadric.

That frightened him more than anything yet.

At the end of the hall, the doors to the south veranda stood open.

Cold morning air moved through the house.

Perrin stopped.

Nobody told him to continue.

He did anyway.

They went outside.

Sir Malrec Thornby was sitting in a chair.

For one absurd heartbeat Cadric thought he was asleep.

Malrec sat facing the lawns, boots planted squarely before him, sword laid across his knees. Someone had folded his hands neatly over the scabbard.

There was remarkably little blood.

A dark line marked his collar.

His head rested at an angle no sleeping man would tolerate.

Pinned neatly to his coat was one of Larkmeadow’s service cards.

Ysabet made a tiny sound.

Perrin whispered:

“Gods.”

Cadric stepped closer.

He could read the card.

**NO LONGER REQUIRED.**

Nobody moved.

Then Cadric remembered.

“Harl.”

Ysabet looked at him.

“What?”

Cadric could hear his own heartbeat.

“He wasn’t alone.”

Perrin frowned.

“What are you talking about?”

“Malrec.”

Cadric pointed at the corpse.

“He went out last night with Harl Brenn.”

Ysabet’s face changed.

Only slightly.

Enough.

Cadric continued.

“I saw them in the west passage. Malrec had a cudgel under his coat. Harl had his knife.”

Perrin looked toward the open lawn.

“Where is Brenn?”

No one answered.

Cadric looked at the hedges.

The veranda steps.

The roofline.

Nothing.

Somewhere behind them, a woman laughed.

Every person on the veranda turned.

Aylassa stood in the doorway.

She was wearing pale green that morning.

Not black.

That somehow made it worse.

Jessa stood behind her carrying a silver tray.

Aylassa smiled.

“Good morning.”

Nobody answered.

Her mismatched eyes moved to Malrec.

“Oh.”

A little sadness entered her face.

“How dreadful.”

Cadric stared at her.

She looked back.

The sadness vanished.

“Dame Ysabet, would you be terribly kind?”

Jessa crossed the veranda.

On the silver tray lay a sealed letter.

Ysabet did not take it.

Jessa waited.

Eventually, she did.

The wax bore the Silvershire seal.

Ysabet looked at Aylassa.

“You wish me to read this?”

“Aloud, please.”

Perrin said, “What is the meaning of this?”

Aylassa turned her smile toward him.

“Lord Perrin, I promise you will understand considerably more when Dame Ysabet finishes.”

Ysabet broke the seal.

Her hands were shaking.

Cadric had never seen that before.

She unfolded the letter.

Read silently for several seconds.

Then looked up.

Aylassa tilted her head.

“Out loud.”

Ysabet swallowed.

> “To the present guests of Larkmeadow House.
> 
> “Good morning.
> 
> “Certain irregularities concerning the management of my estate have lately come to my attention.
> 
> “These include irregularities of finance, hospitality, household service, private correspondence, alchemical provision, and personal conduct.
> 
> “Some of you are witnesses.
> 
> “Some of you are victims.
> 
> “Some of you are merely fools.
> 
> “And some of you have been exceedingly wicked.”

Perrin's face had gone white.

Ysabet stopped.

Aylassa gestured delicately.

“Please continue.”

> “I have not yet determined which description applies to every person presently enjoying my hospitality.
> 
> “I therefore ask that no guest depart Silvershire until I have had the opportunity to speak with them.
> 
> “This is a request only in the same sense that a locked door requests you not walk through it.”

Cadric looked toward the drive.

Two halfling men were standing near the gate.

They had not been there yesterday.

Ysabet continued.

> “Any person who attempts to flee will oblige me to assume that urgency has overcome innocence.
> 
> “I dislike assumptions.
> 
> “They encourage me to investigate energetically.
> 
> “You will find my less energetic methods considerably more comfortable.”

Someone behind Cadric whispered a prayer.

> “You are free to eat, drink, walk the grounds, attend chapel, confer with counsel, and compose whatever explanation your conscience feels able to survive.
> 
> “You are not free to burn papers.
> 
> “You are not free to threaten servants.
> 
> “You are not free to remove witnesses.
> 
> “You are not free to alter accounts.
> 
> “And, for the immediate future, you are not free to leave.”

Ysabet faltered again.

The last paragraph was written larger.

> “For those worried that remaining at Larkmeadow may prove dangerous, I offer the following reassurance:
> 
> “Sir Malrec Thornby was not killed for staying.
> 
> “He was killed for making himself a problem.”

Nobody looked at the body.

Nobody needed to.

Ysabet reached the signature.

Her voice failed.

Aylassa smiled.

“Go on.”

Ysabet read it.

> “With every expectation of your cooperation,
> 
> “Lady Aylassa Silvershire,
> 
> “your regrettably attentive landlady.”

Below it sprawled an enormous looping signature that consumed nearly a quarter of the page, complete with an absurd little flourish that resembled either a flower or a dagger.

Ysabet lowered the letter.

The veranda was silent.

Then Cadric heard it.

A faint sound from above.

Wood creaking.

He looked toward the roof.

Nothing there.

Aylassa followed his gaze.

Her smile widened by the smallest amount.

Cadric thought of Harl Brenn.

Malrec had taken Harl with him.

Malrec was sitting dead in a chair.

Nobody had seen Harl since.

For the first time that morning, Cadric understood the real purpose of the letter.

It was not to tell them they could not leave.

The gates could do that.

The halflings could do that.

Aylassa could certainly do that.

The letter existed to make each of them wonder what everyone else had done.

Who had spoken?

Who had confessed?

Who was missing?

Who was next?

Aylassa stepped forward and removed the service card from Malrec’s coat.

She examined it critically.

“I think the lettering is crooked.”

Jessa looked at it.

“Very slightly, my lady.”

“Annoying.”

“Yes, my lady.”

Aylassa tucked it away.

Then she looked around the assembled guests.

“Breakfast is ready.”

Nobody moved.

She blinked.

“Oh, come now.”

That little laugh finally surfaced.

“You’ll need your strength.”

Cadric Vayneholt believed her.

That was the worst part.
