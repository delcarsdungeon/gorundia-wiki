## The Gate Was Already Open

Aylassa came alone.

No carriage. No escort. No outriders wearing borrowed importance.

Just one black horse at a hard canter along the moss-walled road, its tack dark and plain, its rider darker still.

Aylassa, as usual, was dressed to kill. Many Gorundian ladies were, but in her case the phrase had stopped being metaphor and started asking for hazard pay. Black leather fitted her from throat to boot, close enough for scandal and practical enough for murder, with enough sharp implements tucked into elegant places to make a halfling cook and an armorer exchange professional concerns while a Pelorite priest quietly began praying for mercy “to whom it may concern.”

She slowed before the outer gate without being challenged.

That was the first courtesy.

The second was that the gate was already open.

Two housemen stood inside the arch, not guards exactly, though both wore swords and neither had the softness of men hired for display. Their livery was Sylvanranth green and gold, though mourning had disciplined both colors: the green darkened toward cypress, the gold dulled beneath black thread and ash-cloth. Black ribbons were tied at their wrists.

They bowed as she passed.

Aylassa gave them a bright little salute with two fingers.

Then, because she was Aylassa, she added, “No ambush in the shrubbery? Standards, gentlemen, standards.”

Neither man smiled.

Neither man looked offended.

One simply inclined his head a fraction deeper, as if she had commented on the weather and the weather had been properly noted.

The manor waited beyond the drive.

Most people would have called it Sylvanranth House, because most people liked their names short and their consequences shorter.

The household did not.

To the staff, to the ledgers, and to any guest corrected with lethal courtesy, it was Taur-Endorath. The Stern Grove House. The House of the Bound Word.

It stood at the edge of the King’s Forest with its green banners veiled and its gold thread dimmed, old elvish grace drawn tight around something harder and less forgiving. The place did not look abandoned. It did not look frantic. It did not even look wounded in any ordinary way.

It looked disciplined.

That made the grief sharper.

At the foot of the main stair, the household had gathered.

Not all of it. Not the kitchens, not the hidden corridors, not whatever quiet knives Theron Sylvanranth had tucked behind the wallpaper and called “staff.” But enough.

Two lines of servants stood arranged with formal precision from the stair to the open doors. Dark green livery. Muted gold trim. Black mourning bands. Still hands. Still faces.

Every mirror visible beyond the threshold had been covered in dark cloth and tied with dull gold cord. No polished tray caught a face. No glass answered the candles. No window reflected her back at herself.

The house had made itself blind until mourning was done.

Dwalric Stonefoot stood at the center.

Small, square, immaculate, and absolutely impossible to mistake for a servant in any ordinary sense. His coat was deep Sylvanranth green, its gold fastenings veiled by a strip of black linen. His beard was bound with iron rings darkened for mourning. One hand rested lightly atop a silver-capped cane.

The limp, when he moved, was present and ignored by everyone.

That told Aylassa more about the household than any banner could have.

He bowed.

Not deeply.

Correctly.

“My lady,” he said. “Taur-Endorath receives you in mourning, under the Master’s final instructions, and in the name of the House of the Bound Word.”

Aylassa’s expression changed.

Not much.

But enough.

For all her brightness, for all her leather and blades and performative lunacy, she understood ceremony when it had teeth. The old elvish name landed on her with unexpected force. She glanced once at the covered mirrors, once at the veiled green-and-gold banners, and then back to Dwalric.

“Well,” she said softly. “That was magnificent and deeply unfair.”

“Custom often is, my lady.”

“I may have to behave now.”

“That would be appreciated, though not presumed.”

Her smile flashed.

There. Gone.

Then she swung down from the saddle before anyone could offer a hand. Not rudely. Efficiently. One boot hit gravel, then the other, and she passed the reins to the nearest footman with the easy confidence of a woman who assumed competence until civilization disappointed her.

Which it frequently did.

“Master Stonefoot.”

Then she curtseyed.

In black leather.

In riding boots.

In the middle of the drive.

It should have been absurd. It was absurd. But she made it beautiful anyway, sinking with the full courtly grace of a woman in silk and jewels, one hand extended as if holding invisible skirts, her spine perfect, her chin angled just so. It was a ballroom gesture with blood under its fingernails, elegant enough for a queen and dangerous enough to frisk the orchestra.

A few of the younger servants would have stared, in another household.

Here, no one did.

Dwalric accepted the curtsey as if it were the most natural thing in the world.

“My lady honors the house.”

Aylassa rose in one smooth motion.

“I had several less honorable options and rejected them out of vanity.”

“Your restraint is appreciated.”

That drew another quick flash of a smile from her.

Then her eyes moved past him, to the open doors. To the covered glass. To the line of silent staff. To the empty place where Theron Sylvanranth should have stood if the world were better arranged and less aggressively stupid.

The brightness in her face did not vanish.

It became thinner.

“I am sorry,” she said.

No flourish. No joke. No performance.

Just that.

Dwalric bowed his head.

“As are we, my lady.”

The staff remained still.

Aylassa looked back to him.

“I respected him greatly.”

“He knew.”

That hit harder than she expected.

Her mouth opened, then closed again.

For one heartbeat, the road, the leather, the jokes, the madcap sparkle all failed her.

Then she recovered, because some people built walls out of stone, and some built them out of movement.

“Well,” she said briskly, removing her gloves finger by finger, “that was unforgivably kind of you to say, so naturally I shall pretend not to have been affected.”

“Of course, my lady.”

“Good. We are already cooperating.”

“The house remains at your service.”

“Dangerous phrase.”

“We use it sparingly.”

Aylassa glanced again at the assembled household.

“Master Stonefoot, I begin to suspect this reception is more formal than my arrival deserves.”

“It is appropriate to a friend of the house.”

That slowed her.

Only slightly.

But enough.

“A friend,” she said.

“Yes, my lady.”

“In mourning, no less. You are determined to make me behave.”

“No, my lady.”

Dwalric’s expression did not change.

“We are determined to receive you properly whether you behave or not.”

Aylassa laughed once.

Too bright.

Too quick.

One of the housemaids lowered her eyes, not in disapproval, but in the perfect discretion of someone declining to witness a private defense mechanism flinging itself bodily across a polished floor.

Aylassa noticed.

Of course she did.

Her smile softened by half an inch.

“Then I shall be unbearable with gratitude.”

“We have made accommodations.”

“Presumptuous.”

“Experienced.”

The horse snorted behind her. The footman holding the reins murmured to it, low and competent. No one asked whether she had ridden hard. No one asked why she had come alone. No one asked why she wore black leather to a grieving house and curtseyed like a duchess at Midwinter Court.

That was the third courtesy.

The greatest one.

Dwalric stepped aside.

“If my lady will enter.”

She looked up at the doorway.

The house waited.

Not welcoming. Not rejecting.

Continuing.

Aylassa took one breath.

“Are all the mirrors covered?”

“All that may catch a face.”

“Even the silver?”

“Dulled until the mourning period is lifted.”

“Thorough.”

“The Master was exact.”

“Yes,” she said quietly. “He was.”

Dwalric did not answer.

He did not need to.

Aylassa moved toward the stair, then paused beside him.

“I did not come only to pay respects.”

“I assumed as much.”

“Because I came armed?”

“My lady, most friends of this house arrive armed.”

“Because I came alone?”

“That suggested urgency.”

“Because I dressed like a scandal with buckles?”

“That suggested personality.”

She turned her head and gave him a look.

For the first time, Dwalric’s mouth moved almost enough to be accused of humor.

Almost.

Aylassa climbed the steps.

The household bowed as she passed, one after another, a quiet ripple of green, gold, and mourning black. No music greeted her. No harp from the gallery. No soft strings to decorate grief into something more comfortable.

Only the low fires.

Only the covered mirrors.

Only the sound of her boots crossing the threshold.

At the door, she stopped once more and looked back over the drive.

The road behind her was empty.

The road ahead, wherever Happy Valley sat waiting in its rot, would not be.

“Master Stonefoot,” she said.

“Yes, my lady.”

“I am going to ask something difficult.”

“Then I am pleased you came to a house that keeps records.”

She breathed out, almost a laugh, almost not.

“Gods help us all.”

“Where appropriate,” Dwalric said, “we will assist them.”

## Dinner, Without Bloodshed

![[Aylassas Dinner with Dwalric.png]]

Aylassa’s arrival was handled with the sort of efficiency that made questions unnecessary.

Her horse was taken. Her tack was logged. A room had already been prepared. Hot water arrived before she asked for it, along with clean towels, lemon water, salt biscuits, and a mourning-dark robe laid where she could reach it without feeling watched.

No one asked why she had come alone.

No one asked why she had ridden hard.

No one asked whether the knives came off before supper.

The answer, as the household correctly assumed, was no.

By the time she entered the private dining room, the road had been removed from her with hot water, clean linen, and terrifying household competence. She had changed into deep Sylvanranth green, her hair dressed with immaculate care, gold threaded through the braids like old sunlight caught in a net. She looked every inch the proper elvish maiden.

Which, on Aylassa, made propriety look less like virtue and more like a concealed blade.

A light supper had been laid: dark bread, soft cheese, winter pears, cold fowl, and wine in cups that did not shine. The silver had been dulled. The mirrors remained covered. The green-and-gold hangings were veiled with black linen.

There was no music.

Dwalric Stonefoot stood beside the sideboard in formal mourning livery, one hand resting on the silver cap of his cane.

“My lady,” he said, “I must ask your pardon. Supper is served without music while the household remains under mourning restriction.”

“No apology is necessary.”

“It is, my lady. Hospitality is not suspended by grief. Only altered.”

She sat.

The chair had been chosen correctly: near enough the fire to warm her, far enough that she would not feel softened by it. The food was simple and excellent, because Taur-Endorath had apparently decided sorrow should still be plated competently.

Dwalric remained standing.

Of course he did.

Aylassa took three bites before she set down her knife.

“Master Stonefoot.”

“My lady?”

“Are you going to stand there for the entire meal looking indispensable?”

“I had intended to stand here only for the necessary portion.”

“That was not a denial.”

“No, my lady.”

“I dislike discussing serious matters with a man arranged like furniture.”

“I assure my lady, the furniture is not so heavily burdened.”

“Sit down.”

A pause.

Not hesitation.

Procedure encountering weather.

“My lady is kind.”

“My lady is impatient.”

“That also has been noted.”

“Then note this: I came alone, I came armed, and I came to ask something difficult. If you stand while I eat, I shall either feel rude or start performing at you, and neither will improve the evening.”

Dwalric inclined his head.

“As steward of the house, then. Not as guest.”

“As whatever permits you to use a chair without summoning a legal crisis.”

A footman appeared with a chair before Dwalric turned his head.

Naturally.

Dwalric sat with care, cane placed beside him, grief buttoned into every seam.

Aylassa lifted her wine.

“There. Civilization narrowly survives.”

“For now,” Dwalric said.

She smiled despite herself.

Then the smile thinned.

“I am sorry for him.”

Dwalric’s gaze moved briefly toward the covered mirror above the hearth.

“As are we.”

“I respected him.”

“He knew.”

That struck deeper than courtesy had any right to.

For a moment, the poise, the knives, the bright little madness all failed her.

Then she recovered, because some people built walls from stone and some built them from motion.

“Well,” she said briskly, “that was unforgivably kind of you. I shall pretend it missed.”

“Of course, my lady.”

“You are very good at this.”

“Service?”

“Not commenting.”

“In this house, my lady, not commenting is often the service.”

She laughed once.

Softly this time.

Then she reached for the bread.

“Good. Then let us continue to be useful to one another.”

Aylassa tore a small piece from the bread and turned it once between her fingers.

“I am going to Happy Valley.”

Dwalric did not react.

That was comforting.

Most people reacted to names. Wise people waited to hear what the name had done.

“It is mine,” she said. “In the way such things are mine. Ink, inheritance, obligation, old men congratulating themselves across a table. That sort of ownership.”

“A durable kind,” Dwalric said.

“Unfortunately.”

She ate the bread, mostly to give herself something to do.

“I do not know what I am riding into.”

“No?”

“No. Which is rude of it.”

“Very.”

“I have suspicions. I have patterns. I have the unpleasant certainty that wherever power has been left alone too long, someone has discovered a way to make suffering administratively convenient.”

Dwalric’s gaze did not move.

Aylassa smiled thinly.

“You see? This is why I like you. You do not make encouraging noises.”

“They are rarely useful.”

“No. They are usually just fear wearing perfume.”

She leaned back slightly.

“If I took Thranduil, this would be simple. He and I could ride in, frighten every guilty man within ten miles, and have the whole place confessing by sunset. Or dead. Depending on how irritating they were.”

“A direct method.”

“A satisfying method.”

“Not always the same thing.”

Her smile sharpened.

“There you are again, being useful.”

“I apologize.”

“Do not. I would only forgive you.”

She looked toward the covered mirror above the hearth.

“No. I am not going in as a blade. Not first. I am going in as the lady of the place. Noble. Polite. Mildly inconvenienced. Deeply concerned. All the usual weapons, just cleaner.”

“A slower method.”

“A better one, if I want them to show me who they are before I decide how murder-gremlin I intend to become.”

Dwalric paused.

Only just.

“Murder-gremlin, my lady?”

“A technical term.”

“I shall update the index.”

“Please do.”

Aylassa leaned back, one foot angled beneath the table as though she might spring from the chair or start dancing. With her, both seemed equally plausible and equally dangerous.

“If I arrive with soldiers, they hide the bruises, burn the papers, coach the servants, and send the worst men into the trees until I leave.”

Dwalric did not move except to lower his eyes for the space of one breath.

“If you arrive alone?”

Aylassa’s smile thinned.

“Then I arrive as accusation, not lady.”

“Yes.”

“So I arrive properly.” She lifted one hand, counting the pieces in the air. “One noblewoman. One maid. No bodyguards.”

Dwalric’s gaze returned to her face.

“And when they ask after them?”

“I left them at the border.”

“Why?”

Aylassa spread her hands, all innocence and knives.

“Because why would I bring bodyguards to my own house?”

Dwalric considered that.

“It is a useful answer.”

“It is a trap with manners.”

“Those are often the best kind.”

“I thought you might appreciate it.”

“I do.”

The humor thinned.

Not vanished.

Only stepped aside, like a servant making room for a coffin.

Aylassa set her cup down.

“But the maid cannot be decorative.”

“No.”

“She must be able to hear a lie from behind a closed door. She must know when a frightened woman is asking for help without using the words. She must keep her face still if dinner becomes an indictment.”

“That is a particular request.”

“I know.”

“And dangerous.”

“I know that too.”

“Does she need to fight?”

Aylassa looked down at her hands.

“Only if everything has already gone wrong.”

“In my experience, my lady, everything has usually gone wrong earlier than people admit.”

“That is also my experience.”

A quiet passed between them.

This one was not grief.

This one was business.

Then Dwalric’s hand tightened on the head of his cane.

Only slightly.

Enough.

“No servant of this house will be ordered into private danger.”

Aylassa looked up.

“I would not ask it.”

“No,” he said. “You would not.”

His voice remained level, but the room had changed around it.

“The Master gathered this household from ruin, debt, blood, hunger, disgrace, and foolishness. Some were saved from enemies. Some from patrons. Some from themselves.”

His gaze did not leave hers.

“They are not pieces to be moved because a lady of rank requires a cleaner silhouette.”

Aylassa’s expression softened by a dangerous fraction.

“I know.”

“I believe you do.”

“I need someone willing.”

“Then that is what you may have.”

“May?”

“If such a person chooses it.”

There was iron in the correction.

Not insult.

Boundary.

Aylassa inclined her head.

A real inclination, this time. Not courtly display. Not performance. Recognition.

“Of course.”

Dwalric held her gaze one moment longer, as if weighing whether the word had landed.

Then he nodded.

“Good.”

The room breathed again.

Aylassa picked up her wine, but did not drink.

“Master Stonefoot.”

“My lady?”

“I liked you before this.”

“That was generous.”

“No. Premature. Now I like you properly.”

“I shall endeavor to survive the distinction.”

“You may even deserve it.”

“That would be inconvenient, but I will make a note.”

The corner of her mouth moved.

“Please do.”

Dwalric turned his head slightly.

The footman by the door straightened.

“Ask Jessa Ardell whether she will attend us.”
