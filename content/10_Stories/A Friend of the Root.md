# A Friend of the Root

*Late-night scene at the Great Tree of Bard’s Gate*

## In-Game Date

Late one night, sometime in early Coldeven, 1231

## Summary

Late at night, [[Countess Zara Vallytrex]] comes quietly to the [[Great Tree of Bard's Gate]] in [[Glimmer Green]] and gives [[Tovan Reedmere]] a heavy bag of money for grove restoration.

The gift is to remain anonymous, recorded only as coming from:

**A Friend of the Root**

Tovan is overwhelmed, stammers badly, accidentally calls Zara beautiful with complete childish innocence, and is spared only because Zara finds it unexpectedly sincere rather than courtly fawning.

She gives him instructions.

He promises to keep it quiet.

He will not keep it quiet.

## Key Ledger Route

Zara does not make the donation through any official noble channel.

The money is routed through **Master Pellen Grimsprocket**, a gnome factor of the Merchant’s Guild operating from a discreet counting office on [[Stonehollow Road]].

Pellen’s public role is boring enough to be useful:

- merchant factor
- invoice witness
- contract copyist
- guild fee processor
- quiet handler of maintenance trusts

He forwards the payment through Galibano-connected accounts without naming them directly.

The [[Galibano Family]] is **not officially in town**.

There is no Galibano office on the civic rolls. No open seal. No family placard. No registered representative.

Naturally, this means everyone with sense knows they are involved anyway, because plausible deniability is just theater for accountants.

The money appears in the grove accounts as a private maintenance trust under the name:

**A Friend of the Root**

No plaque.

No heraldry.

No formal thanks.

No temple breakfast mention.

No grateful speeches.

Especially no grateful speeches.

## Scene

The grove was not empty at night.

[[Tovan Reedmere]] knew that better than most.

People thought of night as absence. No sunlight, no visitors, no children chasing each other down the root-paths, no old women sitting with their knees wrapped in blankets and their hands folded around cups of bitter tea.

But the [[Great Tree of Bard's Gate]] was never empty.

At night, it simply stopped pretending to belong to the city.

The leaves shifted without wind.

Small animals moved through the dark in soft, busy lines.

Water whispered in the root channels.

Somewhere above, hidden among branches older than several city offices and most of their bad decisions, [[Auraleth of the Great Tree]] watched the moonlight gather on her bark.

Tovan knelt near the western root-bed with a lantern hooded low beside him, sleeves rolled up, fingers muddy to the second knuckle. He was pressing wet clay into a cracked runnel stone and muttering to himself.

> “No, no, you can sulk tomorrow. Tonight we hold shape.”

The stone did not answer.

That was usually best.

He smoothed the clay with his thumb, leaned back, and frowned at the line of the channel.

> “Too shallow.”

A leaf drifted down and landed on his shoulder.

Tovan looked up.

> “I know.”

Another leaf fell.

> “I said I know.”

A third leaf.

He sighed.

> “My lady, if you wish to criticize the angle, you may simply say so.”

The branches above creaked softly.

Not speech.

Not quite.

But amused.

Tovan sat back on his heels and reached for the small wooden measure beside him.

Then the grove gate opened.

Very softly.

Tovan froze.

The gate had not creaked.

It always creaked. Even when oiled, even when blessed, even when he specifically asked it not to embarrass him in front of visitors, it creaked.

Tonight, it opened without a sound.

That meant either magic, wealth, or someone with enough confidence to frighten hinges into obedience.

Tovan slowly turned.

A woman stood beneath the arch of the gate, wrapped in a dark traveling cloak trimmed in deep green. The hood shadowed her face, but not enough. The lantern-light caught the edge of red hair, the curve of cheek and mouth, the bright intelligence of eyes that seemed to measure the whole grove in a single glance.

Tovan stood too quickly.

His knee struck the stone channel.

He hissed, staggered, caught himself on a root, then remembered the root was sacred and apologized to it.

> “I’m sorry. Sorry. Not to you. I mean, yes, to you. Also to—”

The woman lowered her hood.

Tovan stopped speaking.

The night seemed to stop with him.

She was not dressed for court. No plate. No jewels meant to command a room. No retinue. No herald. No visible weapon.

She did not need any of them.

[[Countess Zara Vallytrex]] stood in the grove as if she had stepped out of a scandal whispered by firelight and decided, for reasons known only to herself, to visit a tree.

Tovan’s mouth opened.

Closed.

Opened again.

> “You’re…”

Zara regarded him with calm amusement.

He swallowed.

> “You’re…”

A smile touched her mouth.

> “Yes,” she said gently. “Countess Zara.”

Tovan stared at her.

Then said, with the doomed sincerity of a man whose mind had slipped its leash:

> “Beautiful.”

Silence.

The word hung there.

Tovan’s eyes widened.

His face drained, then flushed so hard it was visible even in the low lantern-light.

> “I mean—no. I mean yes. I mean not that you are not. You are. Obviously. I mean not obviously in a rude way. I was not observing you. Except I was. With eyes. Because that is how seeing works. I meant—”

Zara blinked.

For one rare instant, she looked genuinely taken aback.

Not offended.

Not flattered in the practiced way men usually tried to flatter her, with polished phrases and hungry eyes and calculation sweating through the perfume.

This had not been that.

There had been no angle in it. No reach. No appetite. No little courtly hook hidden under silk.

The poor man had simply seen her and reported the disaster honestly.

Zara laughed.

It was not her court laugh. Not the bright blade she used in halls where everyone was armed with manners.

This was softer.

Almost startled.

Tovan looked as if he might throw himself into the nearest shrub and ask it for asylum.

> “Oh, gods,” he whispered. “I’ve begun badly.”

> “You have begun unusually,” Zara said.

> “That may be worse.”

> “It is not.”

She stepped farther into the grove. Only then did Tovan notice the bag in her hand.

It was small enough to carry easily, but heavy enough that the leather pulled taut around its contents. The clasp was plain. The stitching was expensive. The whole thing had the air of something deliberately made to look less important than it was.

Zara held it out.

Tovan stared at it.

Then at her.

Then at the bag again.

> “I should not take mysterious bags from noblewomen at night.”

> “No,” Zara said. “As a general rule, that is excellent policy.”

He made no move to accept it.

Her smile sharpened slightly.

> “This is an exception.”

Tovan took the bag with both hands.

His arms dipped.

> “Oh.”

> “Yes.”

> “This is very heavy.”

> “Yes.”

> “This is not seeds.”

> “No.”

He looked at the bag as if it might begin explaining itself.

Zara folded her hands before her.

> “The Great Tree requires repair.”

Tovan’s nervousness shifted. Not gone, but redirected. His eyes flicked instinctively to the western root-bed, the cracked channel, the old warding post leaning a finger’s width too far from true.

> “Yes,” he said. “It does.”

> “The western watercourse is wrong.”

He stared at her.

Zara’s gaze moved past him, not to the canopy, but to the low stone channels half-hidden beneath moss and soil.

> “The fall is too shallow there. The water slows, gathers, and sits after rain. Stone that holds water too long holds cold with it.”

Tovan looked toward the channel.

> “You can tell that?”

Zara’s eyes returned to him.

> “I have people who know stone.”

That was not quite an answer.

It was also not quite a lie.

Zara continued.

> “There is iron contamination near the western gate. Not much. Enough to matter if left alone.”

Tovan’s mouth fell slightly open.

> “No one told me that.”

> “Then someone should have.”

She looked toward the exposed roots, then back to him.

> “As for the tree itself, I will not pretend expertise. I know stonework, watercourses, poor drainage, and poisons. Roots are another language.”

Tovan blinked.

That humility, or whatever stood close enough to it in a countess’s cloak, seemed to unsettle him more than arrogance would have.

> “The roots are strained,” he said carefully. “Not dying. Not yet. But the deep bed is thirsty after summer heat, and drowned after hard rain. It cannot decide what season it is being punished by.”

Zara nodded once.

> “Then the watercourse feeding the deep root-bed must be restored before summer. The soil should be replaced in layers, not turned all at once.”

She glanced toward the damp clay packed beside his work tools.

> “Clay has its use. It holds shape. But too much, packed low, will choke the bed. Araisal loam would breathe better.”

Tovan clutched the bag closer.

> “You know about clay and loam.”

> “I know enough to hire people who do.”

> “That is not the same as knowing trees.”

> “No,” Zara said. “That is why I am giving the money to you.”

He looked down at the bag.

Then at the cracked channel.

Then, reluctantly, at the old roots.

> “That is… probably wise.”

> “Probably?”

Honesty wrestled with diplomacy.

Honesty won, because it had a knife.

> “Definitely wise. But also alarming.”

> “That is fair.”

> “That is true.”

> “I am not here to accuse you.”

> “Oh. Good. Thank you. I would have accepted, but badly.”

> “I am here to help.”

The words were simple.

That made them stranger.

Tovan looked down at the bag again.

> “How much is this?”

> “Enough.”

> “That is not a number.”

> “No.”

> “I keep accounts.”

> “I am sure you do.”

> “Not elegant accounts. But accounts.”

> “Then write this as a gift from a friend.”

> “A friend.”

> “Of the root.”

Tovan’s brow furrowed.

> “A Friend of the Root?”

> “That will do.”

He looked at her, trying to understand the shape of it.

> “You do not want your name recorded.”

> “No.”

> “No plaque?”

> “No plaque.”

> “No heraldry?”

> “No.”

> “No formal thanks?”

> “No.”

> “No temple breakfast mention?”

Zara’s smile returned.

> “Especially no temple breakfast mention.”

Tovan nodded very seriously.

> “Yes. Those can get out of hand. Sister Ammel once thanked a man for donating barley cakes and by noon everyone knew he was courting the miller’s widow. He was not. Well. He was, but privately.”

> “I trust you see the danger.”

> “I do. Completely.”

Zara studied him.

That seemed to make him remember who he was speaking to. He straightened so abruptly that one of the coins inside the bag shifted with a soft, expensive sound.

> “I mean. Yes, my lady. Of course. No mention. No plaque. No heraldry. No breakfast. A Friend of the Root.”

> “Good.”

She stepped closer.

Tovan held very still, like a rabbit that had realized the fox was being polite.

> “You will hire proper workers,” she said. “Quietly. Pay old debts first. Laborers remember being cheated, and roots hear curses spoken over them.”

Tovan nodded.

> “Repair the channels before the visible paths. The grove must live before it looks pretty.”

> “Yes.”

> “If city officials ask, the funds came through a private maintenance trust.”

He blinked.

> “Did they?”

> “They do now.”

> “Right. Yes. Trust. Maintenance. Private.”

> “There is a gnome factor on [[Stonehollow Road]]. Master Pellen Grimsprocket. You will take any payment orders through him.”

> “Master Grimsprocket.”

> “He will know which accounts to touch.”

> “That sounds alarming.”

> “It is meant to sound boring.”

> “That may be more alarming.”

> “Good. Then you are listening.”

Tovan nodded again.

Too many instructions were stacking behind his eyes like frightened geese.

Zara noticed.

Her expression softened by half a degree.

> “Breathe, Master Reedmere.”

He breathed.

Too much.

Coughed.

Recovered badly.

> “I am breathing.”

> “Convincingly.”

> “Thank you.”

For a moment, neither spoke.

The Great Tree rustled above them.

Zara looked up.

Something changed in her face.

It was subtle. Tovan might have missed it if he had not spent his life watching leaves and bark for small truths. The countess was still composed. Still elegant. Still dangerous in the way a drawn blade is dangerous even when laid gently on silk.

But beneath it, something had gone quiet.

Not weak.

Not ashamed, exactly.

Listening.

The leaves stirred again.

Auraleth did not appear, but the grove leaned toward Zara.

Tovan felt it.

Zara did too.

Her eyes lowered back to the roots.

> “She accepts it?” Zara asked.

Tovan swallowed.

It was not really a question one lied about.

> “Yes,” he said.

For the first time since she entered, Countess Zara Vallytrex seemed to exhale fully.

> “Good.”

The word was almost too quiet to hear.

Tovan’s fingers tightened around the bag.

> “My lady…”

Zara looked at him.

He should have stopped there.

He did not.

> “Why?”

The grove became very still.

Tovan instantly regretted every life choice leading to that syllable.

> “I do not mean to pry. I mean, I did pry. That was prying. I apologize. The question escaped. I do not know who taught it the latch.”

Zara watched him for a long moment.

Then she said:

> “Because it was needed.”

Tovan nodded.

That answer should have been enough.

But he was Tovan Reedmere, and enough was apparently a country he had never visited.

> “And because of Jinwing?”

Zara’s eyes sharpened.

Tovan’s soul left his body, looked back, and judged him.

> “I am dead,” he whispered.

> “No,” Zara said.

> “Not yet?”

> “Not tonight.”

> “That is kind.”

> “It is restrained.”

> “Yes. Thank you for your restraint.”

Zara held his gaze.

Then, to his astonishment, the edge faded.

> “A little because of [[Jinwing]],” she said.

Tovan said nothing.

This was unusual enough that even the tree seemed interested.

Zara looked toward the Great Tree again.

> “She has a way of making mercy feel less like defeat.”

Tovan considered this with the terrible seriousness of a man being trusted with a fragile thing.

Then he nodded.

> “She does.”

Zara returned her attention to him.

> “You will not repeat that.”

> “No.”

> “You will not repeat any of this.”

> “No.”

> “You will repair the grove.”

> “Yes.”

> “You will keep the gift quiet.”

> “Yes.”

> “You will remember that anonymous means anonymous.”

> “Yes.”

> “You will not explain the anonymity in detail to the first person who asks.”

Tovan hesitated.

Zara raised one eyebrow.

He swallowed.

> “I will make a sincere effort.”

> “That is not the same thing.”

> “No.”

> “You understand why this matters?”

> “Yes.”

> “Do you?”

Tovan hugged the bag like a shield.

> “You are trying to do a good thing without making it belong to you.”

That answer landed.

Zara’s expression shifted again, not much, but enough.

> “Yes,” she said.

Tovan nodded.

> “I will keep it quiet.”

He meant it.

That was the tragic part.

Zara stepped close enough that he forgot how to stand.

Then she leaned forward and kissed him lightly on the forehead.

Tovan went completely rigid.

> “Thank you, Master Reedmere,” she said. “For the tree. For your care. And for your discretion.”

His voice emerged as a faint squeak.

> “Yes.”

She drew back.

Her smile curved, warmer now, though still carrying that warning all beautiful dangerous things carry.

> “And do try to remember,” she said. “Anonymous.”

> “Anonymous,” he repeated.

> “Completely anonymous.”

> “Completely anonymous.”

> “No songs.”

> “No songs.”

> “No breakfast.”

> “No breakfast.”

> “No grateful speeches.”

His mouth opened.

She touched one finger lightly beneath his chin, closing it.

> “Especially no grateful speeches.”

Tovan nodded.

Zara turned, drew up her hood, and walked back toward the gate.

This time, when it opened, it creaked.

Just a little.

As if the grove itself had waited until she was leaving to breathe again.

At the arch, Zara paused.

Without turning, she said:

> “Master Reedmere?”

> “Yes, my lady?”

> “If anyone asks, you were working late.”

> “I was.”

> “And I was never here.”

Tovan looked at the bag in his arms.

Then at the repaired clay, the cracked channels, the leaning warding posts, the old roots listening under the soil.

> “Right,” he said. “Never here.”

Zara passed through the gate.

The latch settled.

The grove returned to night.

For several long breaths, Tovan stood completely still.

Then the branches above him shifted.

Auraleth’s voice came softly from the leaves.

> “You are glowing, little reed.”

> “I am not.”

> “You are.”

> “I have experienced a civic event.”

> “You have experienced a beautiful woman.”

Tovan closed his eyes.

> “That too.”

The leaves laughed.

He looked down at the bag.

> “A Friend of the Root,” he whispered.

The Great Tree rustled.

Tovan nodded to himself, trying to arrange the instructions in his mind.

No plaque.

No heraldry.

No formal thanks.

No temple breakfast.

No songs.

No grateful speeches.

Keep it quiet.

Very down low.

He could do that.

Absolutely.

Probably.

Several days later, standing in front of witnesses with a watering can in his hand, Tovan Reedmere would discover that he could not, in fact, do that.

## Master Pellen Grimsprocket

**Race:** Gnome  
**Role:** Merchant’s Guild factor  
**Office:** Stonehollow Road counting office  
**Public Work:** Guild fee processing, maintenance trusts, contract witnessing, invoice forwarding  
**Private Utility:** Can pass money through channels tied to the Galibano Family without any Galibano name appearing on city records

Pellen Grimsprocket is a neat, careful gnome with ink-stained cuffs, polished spectacles, and the weary moral posture of a man who has learned that clean paperwork is not the same as clean money.

He is not a Galibano agent.

Officially.

He has never represented the Galibano Family.

Officially.

No Galibano office operates in Bard’s Gate.

Officially.

Pellen simply knows which invoices go where, which stone-and-water trusts are legitimate enough to survive an audit, and which names should never appear beside each other on the same page unless one is trying to start a very expensive fire.

He is useful because he does not ask why money is moving.

He asks whether it has the correct seal, the correct witness, and the correct excuse.

### Pellen’s Line

> “Master Reedmere, I do not know who funds this trust. I know only that the trust exists, the work is lawful, the invoices are payable, and the ink is dry. Let us not ruin a tidy thing with curiosity.”

## Aftermath

The donation repairs more than appearance.

The funds allow the grove to address old structural problems:

- failing western water channels
- cracked runnel stones
- poor drainage around the deep root-bed
- iron contamination near the western gate
- neglected labor debts
- controlled replacement of compacted clay with breathable loam
- weakened boundary runes

Zara’s knowledge appears strongest where stonework, water, drainage, runoff, and contamination are concerned.

She does not explain how she knows what she knows.

Officially, she has people who know stone.

Unofficially, no one sensible presses the question.

She does not fully understand the Great Tree itself.

That matters.

The actual judgment of the roots remains Tovan’s work, and beyond him, Auraleth’s.

The work matters because the Great Tree accepts the gift.

That does not absolve Zara.

It does mean the living grove takes the offering.

The difference is important, and exactly the sort of thing people will argue about until everyone involved wishes druids came with simpler paperwork.

## Tovan’s Failure of Discretion

Tovan does not mean to expose Zara.

He is not bribed, pressured, or tricked.

He is proud.

He is grateful.

He is bad at secrets.

When asked why the repairs are suddenly moving so quickly, he begins with the correct answer:

> “A private friend of the grove helped us.”

Then he improves the answer, which is where everything goes directly into the ditch.

> “No plaque, though. She was very clear about that. Very clear. No heraldry, no formal thanks, no temple breakfast mention. Which I thought was very humble of Countess Zara, really. I mean, for someone with a dragon on her coat of arms, that’s restraint.”

Then he realizes.

Then he looks down at the watering can like it has betrayed him.
