## Vignette — The Patent

9 Coldeven 1231

The first man made the mistake in public.

Not loudly. Not bravely. Just clearly enough that the nearest squires heard it and went still.

Ser Garran Rook had been checking a saddle strap near the horse-lines. He stopped, turned, and looked at the young knight who had spoken.

“Name your house.”

The young knight was bright-buckled, wine-flushed, and surrounded by enough friendly faces to mistake himself for safe.

“Why? Looking to marry better?”

The laughter faltered.

Rook stepped away from the horse.

“Name your house. Name your second. Say it again as charge.”

The young knight’s smile thinned.

“I made no charge.”

“Then retract.”

By then, men had begun to gather. Knights, squires, two mounted sergeants, a groom still holding a brush. The young knight looked around, saw the eyes on him, and chose pride.

“I retract nothing.”

Rook nodded once.

“Then draw.”

It was a proper duel.

That mattered later.

Witnesses stood. Seconds were named. Space was cleared in the yard. The insult had been heard, the retraction refused, and the challenge accepted.

The young knight fought well enough to have been admired in a training hall. Quick wrist, clean guard, good feet. He had been taught by competent men.

Rook fought like Sir Aldos Verran had spent twenty years stripping ceremony down to function.

The first exchange was steel.

The second closed too fast.

The young knight cut. Rook caught it on his shield, stepped in, and drove the shield-rim into the man’s breastplate. The blow folded the knight’s breath out of him. Rook hooked a boot behind his ankle and put him into the mud.

The young man rolled and came up fast.

Rook gave him that.

Then the knight went for Rook’s sword arm.

Rook let him take the line, bound the blade, shoved the hilt aside, and hit him in the mouth with the pommel.

It was lawful.

It was not elegant.

Several witnesses winced.

The young knight staggered, spat blood, and came forward angry.

That ended it.

Rook took the next cut on his shield, stepped through it, caught the man by the sword-belt, and threw him down hard enough that the mud took a print of him. Then Rook set one knee between the man’s shoulder blades and laid his sword edge against the back of his neck.

“Yield.”

The knight tried to shift.

Rook pressed the blade a fraction lower.

“Yield, or finish being foolish.”

The answer came through mud and blood.

“I yield.”

Rook stood.

He did not offer a hand. He did not salute. He did not speak of honor, ladies, or virtue.

He only looked down at the man and said:

“Next time, speak charge or silence.”

By supper, the story had already been cleaned up.

A rough knight had defended Dame Katherine Kendal’s honor in proper form. Some called it unexpected chivalry. Some called it overdue.

Those who had watched closely were less certain.

It had been proper, yes.

But not courtly.

Rook had used the shield edge, the bind, the boot, the pommel, the mud, and every ugly inch of lawful space between duel and beating.
The second incident was harder to explain, not because it was unlawful, but because it was not.

Two days later, a man from the lower camp came to the healers with two broken fingers, a split brow, and a shoulder sitting wrong beneath the skin.

He was not a knight. Not noble. A retainer or hired guard from someone’s baggage train, depending on who was asked and how carefully they wanted the answer recorded.

Brother Anselm Crowther set the shoulder himself.

The sound carried.

So did the name.

“Rook.”

The explanation came after, in pieces. The man had repeated the rumor about Dame Katherine where he thought only other small men would hear it.

Rook had heard.

There had been no challenge. No second. No cleared ground.

There did not need to be.

A common man did not get a knightly duel for slandering a noblewoman.

He got corrected.

That was the part Sophia noticed.

Not because it was illegal.

Because it was not.

Sophia found the name first on a casualty note.

**Ser Garran Rook.**

Then on a duty roll.

Then in a pay ledger.

Then, because the first three documents answered only the dull questions, she went looking for the patents.

By midmorning she had three folios open across a narrow table near the records chest. A wax tablet rested beside her hand. Her hair had begun the day pinned back and had since escaped in several places. There was ink along one finger.

She was reading the same paragraph for the third time when Kat entered.

“Lady Sophia.”

Sophia looked up.

“Dame Katherine.”

The greeting was correct on both sides.

No warmth followed it.

They did not dislike each other cleanly. That would have been simpler. Kat found Sophia too sharp, too casual, too willing to dissect pain while it was still bleeding. Sophia found Kat guarded, formal, and difficult to reach without making her flinch. Neither of them was wrong enough to make the problem easy.

Kat glanced at the folios.

“Are you working?”

“Yes.”

Sophia touched one page to keep her place.

“And not only on assigned work, before you ask. This is related.”

“To what?”

“To Ser Garran Rook.”

Kat’s face stilled.

“I know the name.”

Sophia noted the answer, and the restraint behind it.

“That is a narrow answer.”

“It is an honest one.”

“Good. Narrow answers usually are.”

Kat folded her hands behind her back.

“Why are you looking into him?”

“Because he has now been involved in two incidents tied to the same rumor. The first was a lawful duel. The second was not.”

Kat’s mouth tightened.

“What happened?”

“The first incident says a rough knight defended a lady’s honor within form. The second says lower men are being corrected for repeating what higher men began. A third will say the camp knows something and is afraid to let it be spoken.”

Kat said nothing.

Sophia’s tone softened, not much, but enough to show effort.

“They said something about you.”

Kat’s eyes lowered to the documents.

“Then it is beneath record.”

“No.”

The answer was immediate.

Kat looked up.

Sophia turned the folio so Kat could see the seal impression.

“That is how these things survive. They are kept beneath record until everyone treats them as known.”

Kat’s expression did not change, but her shoulders tightened.

Sophia saw it and chose her next words carefully.

“I am not interested in preserving what he said. I am interested in the shape it is taking.”

“The shape.”

“Yes.”

Sophia tapped the folio.

“The first incident says a rough knight defended a lady’s honor within form. The second says someone is making a habit of correction. A third will say men are being silenced because the rumor frightens someone powerful.”

Kat’s voice was low.

“There is nothing to hide.”

“I know.”

That stopped Kat more than disagreement would have.

Sophia looked down at the patent again.

“I know that. But a rumor does not need truth. It needs repetition, fear, and people willing to mistake smoke for proof.”

She slid one of the folios forward.

“Ser Rook is more interesting than the camp thinks.”

Kat’s eyes flicked to the page.

Southmere witness marks.

Kendal confirmation.

Aldos Verran’s name.

For a moment, Kat looked younger.

Then she drew herself back into stillness.

“You found his patents.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because men who appear in a host and begin breaking other men near politically useful slander are rarely random.”

Sophia glanced at the page.

“Formerly Garran Rook. No house. No lawful trade listed. Convicted in Southmere jurisdiction after a grain-road robbery. Sentence stayed by Baron Cadaras Kendal. Bound into service under Sir Aldos Verran.”

Kat’s jaw moved once.

Sophia continued.

“Years of road duty. Granary fire commendation. Lychford caravan defense. Later grant of arms. Confirmed knightly status.”

She paused.

“Your father did not only spare him.”

“No.”

“He made him useful.”

Kat’s answer came sharper than before.

“He made him answer.”

Sophia looked up.

The correction landed. Sophia accepted it.

“That is better.”

Kat did not relax.

“Ser Garran’s past is not camp entertainment.”

“Neither is your honor.”

That reply left them both quiet.

Sophia closed one folio, then stopped herself from closing the rest. She was thinking too visibly. Kat watched her do it.

At last Sophia said:

“Beringer should have noticed sooner.”

Kat looked at her.

The room changed.

Sophia caught it a half-second late.

She was used to saying such things about Beringer in the casual shorthand of family. Exasperation, affection, complaint, old trust. With Kat, the words had landed somewhere else entirely.

Kat’s voice became formal.

“You should not speak of the Lord Marshal that way.”

Sophia blinked.

Then she understood.

At least partly.

“I mean Beringer.”

“He is the Lord Marshal.”

“He is also my brother.”

“Adopted.”

The word came out too quickly.

Kat knew it. Sophia knew it. The scribe at the far table suddenly became deeply invested in not existing.

Sophia’s face did not harden, exactly. It went still while she sorted the wound from the fact.

“Yes,” she said. “Adopted.”

Kat inhaled.

“Lady Sophia, I did not mean—”

“You did.”

Sophia said it quietly, not cruelly.

Kat had no answer.

Sophia rested one hand on the folio.

“If we are sorting by blood and paper, then yes, my claim is more recent than yours would consider proper.”

Kat flinched.

Sophia noticed and seemed to regret the precision of it even as she continued.

“But I have earned the right to be exasperated by him. That is what I meant.”

Kat looked away.

Sophia let the silence settle before she spoke again.

“His being foolish does not mean I think him small.”

Kat’s eyes returned to her.

Sophia looked uncomfortable now. Not cold. Not triumphant. Just a young woman trying to translate private affection into public language and hating the imprecision of it.

“It means I think him mine enough to say it.”

Kat’s hand twitched once at her side.

“I do not have that luxury.”

“No.”

Sophia’s answer was immediate.

Then, softer:

“I know.”

For a moment, they almost met there.

Almost.

Then Sophia looked back at the documents, because paper was easier than mercy.

“Rook should be spoken to.”

“By the Lord Marshal?”

“No.”

Sophia shook her head.

“If Beringer involves himself, the story becomes Beringer. It always does, especially when people are already looking for him in it.”

Kat’s mouth tightened, but she did not argue.

Sophia continued.

“Your father’s man is answering an insult to your house. Let your house answer him.”

“You mean my father.”

“I mean you.”

Kat stared at her.

Sophia gathered the folios, aligning the edges with careful hands.

“You are Dame Katherine Kendal. If Ser Garran Rook is acting for Kendal honor, then Kendal honor may command him.”

Kat’s hand moved to the small iron portcullis at her throat.

Sophia saw it, but did not comment.

Kat said:

“You think I should tell him to stop.”

“I think you should tell him what he is permitted to do in your name.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“No.”

Sophia held her gaze.

“It is more important.”

The answer stayed in the air between them.

Outside, the host moved around them: canvas snapping in the wind, horses shifting, steel ringing somewhere in the yard.

Kat looked toward the lower camp.

“Why tell me?”

Sophia’s face changed, just slightly.

“Because I do not like what they are doing to you.”

That was too plain for either of them.

Sophia looked down first this time.

“And because if no one thinks about the third version of a story, the worst men get to write it.”

Kat absorbed that.

Then she nodded once.

“Thank you.”

Sophia seemed almost startled by it.

“You are welcome.”

The courtesy was still awkward.

But this time it was not empty.

Sophia tucked the folios under her arm.

“The patents are sound. Rook is real. Not clean. Real.”

Kat looked at the seal again.

“Yes.”

Sophia started to leave, then paused at the door.

“For what it is worth, when I called Beringer foolish, I was teasing him.”

Kat did not answer immediately.

Then:

“I know.”

Sophia studied her.

“No. I do not think you did.”

Kat looked down.

“No,” she said. “I suppose I did not.”

Sophia nodded, once, as if that was a conclusion both of them could live with.

Then she left.

Kat remained beside the records chest, surrounded by dust, wax, and the weight of old decisions written in careful hands.

Outside, men were still talking.

Quieter now.

Not silent.

Kat straightened.

If Ser Garran Rook was acting in her name, then she would speak to him herself.
