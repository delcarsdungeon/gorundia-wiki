## Lóminquëurë (Root-Bound Servitors)
*Scholarly Summary and Mechanical Notes*

The **Lóminquëurë** are post-mortem servitors developed within Wood Elf druidic traditions during the late Emerald Throne period. Unlike [[Ancestor Golems]], which rely on fragmented soul residues bound to inert vessels, Lóminquëurë preserve a near-complete soul and bind it to an external locus of authority rather than the host body.

The transformation is performed at the threshold of natural death. The subject’s body undergoes total transmutation: flesh to wood, blood to sap, structure reinforced through treated bone and root lattice. The resulting form is stable, unliving, and highly resistant to conventional trauma.

Of particular note is the outer construction: beneath ceremonial wrappings lies a loosely packed matrix of dried leaves, fibers, and bark shavings. This layer disperses kinetic force and renders most piercing and impaling attacks functionally ineffective unless sufficient force penetrates to the structural core.

---

### Arcane Structure

A Lóminquëurë is defined by a three-part binding system:

- **Vessel (Body):** Transformed wooden construct. Does not bleed, fatigue, or suffer organ failure.
- **Animus (Soul):** Near-intact soul of the subject. Memory and identity persist, but autonomy is suppressed.
- **Locus (Emerald Throne):** Centralized control authority. The soul is bound to the Throne, not the body.

This separation of animus and vessel is the defining feature—and primary vulnerability—of the system.

---

### Command and Control

Lóminquëurë possess no independent agency while bound. All directive function is mediated through the [[Emerald Throne]].

The system was designed to ensure loyalty to the institution rather than to any individual ruler. However, it contains no intrinsic authentication safeguards. Control is determined solely by access to the Throne.

Observed control states include:

- **Legitimate Command:** Issued by a recognized ruler of the Throne  
- **Seized Authority:** Control obtained through external interference  
- **Direct Inhabitation:** Full override of the system, using the vessel as a proxy body (see: [[Kursk]])

---

### Combat Characteristics

- **Projectile Resistance:** Outer leaf matrix disperses arrows and similar attacks. Most such strikes fail to reach structural components.
- **Structural Core:** Bone and root lattice provide moderate durability once exposed.
- **Immunities:** No blood loss, no vital organs, no fatigue.
- **Vulnerabilities:** Fire, crushing force, and heavy cutting trauma are effective.

---

### Failure Modes

1. **Locus Loss:** Absence of stable control renders the system vulnerable to seizure  
2. **Hostile Control:** Any entity capable of interfacing with the Throne may issue commands  
3. **Animus Persistence:** Destruction of the vessel does not guarantee release of the soul

> “We do not defile the dead. We honor the willing.  
>  
> Those who become Lóminquëurë do so at the end of their lives, in clarity and purpose. Their bodies are returned to the forest in a new form, their spirits preserved in wholeness rather than broken into fragments.  
>  
> They do not serve a tyrant. They serve the Throne—an institution, a continuity, a promise that the People endure beyond any single ruler.  
>

## Norwin’s Commentary

Norwin had spent enough time among the elves to recognize a pattern: brilliance untempered by restraint. Nowhere was that more evident than in the constructs they so casually justified.

He had also spent enough time watching them fight.

Which made the design impossible to ignore.

The finest archers in the world—bar none—had built themselves a soldiery that rendered arrows nearly irrelevant. Layered in leaves, wrapped in ritual cloth, structured around bone and root, these things did not resist arrows. They *discarded* them. Shots passed through with little consequence, or lodged harmlessly in dead matter.

And the obvious counter?

Fire.

The one weapon Wood Elves are culturally, tactically, and spiritually reluctant to employ at scale.

Norwin did not consider that coincidence.

> “So the greatest archers alive crafted a force that shrugs off arrows… and can be reliably stopped by the one tool they refuse to use.  
>  
> That is not a soldier’s solution.  
> That is a ruler’s safeguard.”

He did not call it clever.

He called it what it was.

> “These are not the weapons of a people defending themselves.  
>  
> These are the weapons of a throne ensuring it cannot be opposed.”

---

Norwin had spent enough time among the elves to recognize a pattern: brilliance untempered by restraint. Nowhere was that more evident than in the constructs they so casually justified.

The High Elves, for all their refinement, had devised what they called Ancestor Golems—small wooden totems, innocuous at a glance, each one a repository of knowledge. Recipes, histories, spellcraft… even the forbidden and profane. A tidy solution to the problem of memory.

But the method betrayed the truth of it.

Each totem was not merely enchanted—it was inhabited. A soul, taken and pared down, reduced to fragments and bound within the wood. Not destroyed, not wholly consumed, but mutilated. What remained after the carving might pass on to whatever afterlife awaited it… but only after being stripped, diminished, and used.

Norwin found the practice abhorrent.

---

It was, at the very least, a crime committed with restraint. There was an end to it. A boundary. A fragment of the soul endured, and in time, it moved on.

The Wood Elves, however, had gone much further.

They called their creations Lóminquëurë.

These were not constructs fashioned from the dead, but from the dying. Loyal servants of the Emerald Throne, Wood Elves who, at the end of their natural lives, were subjected to a transformation. Flesh to wood. Blood to sap. Breath to dust and leaves. The body remade into something enduring.

And the soul?

Bound.

Not to the body—but to the Throne itself.

Eternal obedience. Absolute. Unquestioning. Not to a person, not to a people—but to the seat of power. To whoever might claim it.

The arrogance of it was staggering.

---

They had believed the Throne would never fall. That it would always rest beneath elven rule, guided by wisdom, safeguarded by tradition. So they built themselves an undying host, never once asking what would happen if that certainty proved false.

Now the Throne is lost.

And in the hands of Warlord Kursk, the Lóminquëurë have become instruments of devastation. He does not command them—he inhabits them, seizing their forms, turning the honored dead into weapons against the very forest they once swore to protect.

It is not merely a corruption of the body.

It is the enslavement of the soul—indefinite, transferable, and absolute.

Norwin could tolerate many things in the pursuit of magic. Compromise was inevitable. Lines blurred. Costs paid.

But this?

This was not necessity. It was vanity. Control. A refusal to accept the natural end of life, dressed up as legacy.

The elves should not take pride in these creations.

They should be ashamed.

## Addendum — Commentary by Lord Faelar Moonshade  
*Keeper of Ancient Texts, resident in the lands of Duke Allaric*

The preceding commentary, attributed to Norwin, is not without merit.

It is, however, incomplete.

I write not to refute him outright, but to place his conclusions within a broader and, I hope, more temperate frame.

---

### On the Matter of Comparison

It has become fashionable—particularly among younger scholars and human practitioners—to compare the Wood Elf Lóminquëurë with our own ancestral constructs, commonly termed [[Ancestor Golems]].

The comparison is not unreasonable.

But it is often made imprecisely.

The High Elven method, for all its discomfort, is fundamentally reductive. A soul is diminished, partitioned, and impressed upon a vessel for the preservation of knowledge. The injury is real, and I will not pretend otherwise—but it is bounded. The animating principle weakens over time, and what remains is eventually released.

The Wood Elf method is of an entirely different philosophy.

They do not reduce.

They preserve.

And in so doing, they exchange a lesser violation for a far more enduring one.

---

### On Consent and Intention

The Wood Elves are correct in one respect that Norwin does not sufficiently credit:

Participation in the Lóminquëurë rite is, by all credible accounts, voluntary.

This matters.

It does not absolve the practice—but neither may it be dismissed.

Elven cultures, particularly those of the deep forests, place great emphasis on continuity of duty. To stand watch beyond one’s life is not, to them, a horror but an honor. It is easy for those outside that tradition to mistake conviction for coercion.

We should be cautious in doing so.

---

### On the Emerald Throne

Here, I find myself in partial agreement with Norwin.

The decision to bind not to the vessel, but to the :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}, is the critical point of failure.

It is, from a purely arcane perspective, an elegant solution. It allows for centralized coordination, eliminates degradation of command, and ensures perfect continuity of purpose.

It is also catastrophically brittle.

The Wood Elves assumed—quite sincerely—that the Throne would remain within wise hands. That it would be guided, as it always had been, by those shaped by their traditions.

History does not reward such assumptions.

---

### On Military Design

Norwin’s observation regarding battlefield behavior is… uncomfortable.

It is true that the Lóminquëurë are constructed in such a way as to negate the primary strengths of Wood Elf warfare. Arrows, even expertly placed, are frequently insufficient to disable them. Their outer composition renders such attacks inefficient at best.

That this is paired with a marked cultural reluctance among Wood Elves to employ fire at scale is likewise accurate.

Whether this constitutes deliberate internal control, as Norwin suggests, is less certain.

I would propose a more charitable interpretation:

The Wood Elves designed a guardian that could not be easily turned against them by conventional means. In doing so, they inadvertently ensured that the most effective countermeasures would be those they themselves are least inclined to use.

Intent, in this case, is difficult to disentangle from outcome.

---

### On the Present Crisis

The actions of [[Kursk]] render much of this discussion academic.

The system has failed.

Not partially. Not in edge cases.

Completely.

The Lóminquëurë now serve a will entirely divorced from the traditions that justified their creation. Worse, they do so with a fidelity and efficiency that speak to the underlying strength of the original design.

This is the true indictment.

Not that the Wood Elves sought continuity.

But that they built a mechanism in which continuity could be so easily appropriated.

---

### Concluding Remarks

Norwin calls this vanity.

The Wood Elves call it duty.

As is often the case, the truth lies somewhere less satisfying.

It is, I think, best understood as a failure of imagination.

They envisioned eternity.

They did not adequately envision loss.

And so they prepared perfectly for one future—

—and disastrously for all others.

## Addendum — Field Commentary by Aphtheldorthon  
*Captain of the Emerald Guard; Archer of the Reclamation*

I’ve read the scholar’s take.

And Norwin’s.

They’re both right.

And neither of them has had to stand in a tree line and watch these things keep walking.

---

### On What They Are

They don’t look like monsters.

Not at first.

You see the wrappings. The shape. The way they carry themselves.

You think: *old guardians.*

Then you shoot one.

Good shot. Clean line. Center mass.

And the arrow just… goes through.

Not deflected. Not stopped.

It passes through like you missed something that isn’t there.

You didn’t miss.

There’s just nothing where it needs to matter.

---

### On Arrows

I’ve trained my whole life to end fights before they start.

Distance is safety. Distance is control.

That’s what we’re taught.

These things take that away.

Not because they’re stronger.

Because they’re *built* so what I do doesn’t count.

You can still hit them. You will hit them.

You just won’t stop them.

---

### On Design

Norwin thinks this was deliberate.

Faelar thinks it was unintended.

I don’t know which is worse.

What I do know is this:

> Whoever made them understood archers.

Not in theory.

In practice.

They knew what shots look like. What angles matter. What kills.

And they built something that ignores it.

---

### On Fire

Yes. Fire works.

Everyone says that.

They say it like it’s an answer.

It isn’t.

It’s a cost.

You don’t just burn them.

You burn the ground they’re standing on. The trees behind them. The things you’re trying to protect.

You burn your own position if the wind shifts.

You burn fast, or you burn wrong.

And if you hesitate—even a little—they close.

---

### On Kursk

The first time I saw one move *wrong*, I didn’t understand what I was looking at.

They’re supposed to be steady. Deliberate.

These aren’t.

They move like someone alive inside something dead.

Faster. Sharper.

Like the hesitation’s gone.

That’s when you know he’s in there.

And that’s when you stop thinking of them as soldiers.

They’re just bodies now.

---

### On the Old Stories

I grew up on the stories.

We all did.

Guardians. Last watch. Honor carried forward.

I believed them.

Still want to, some days.

But stories don’t bleed.

People do.

---

### On What They Are Now

Maybe they were something else once.

I won’t argue that.

But that’s not what they are now.

Now they’re weapons that don’t care who’s holding them.

And they’re very good at what they do.

---

### Final

You can argue what they were meant to be.

You can argue whether it was right.

That doesn’t change what happens when they’re in front of you.

> You shoot.  
> They keep coming.  
>  
> And then it’s not a question of philosophy anymore.
