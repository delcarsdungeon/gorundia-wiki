# Church of the Triad in Gorundia

> [!quote] Sorek Redmarch
> “The Triad teaches that courage, mercy, and truth stand equal before the gods.  
> The nobility agrees, provided courage gets the largest chair.”  


The **Church of the Triad** is the dominant religious structure of the Kingdom of Gorundia, built upon the formal cooperation of three major faiths:

- [[Heironeous]]
- [[Pelor]]
- [[Pholtus]]

In doctrine, the three gods are honored as equal pillars of righteous civilization. In practice, Gorundia being Gorundia, the arrangement is more complicated, more political, and therefore more likely to have a committee.

The Triad is not a single merged faith. It is a legal, spiritual, and cultural compact between three churches that agreed long ago that the kingdom could not survive if its defenders, healers, judges, and moral authorities spent more time fighting each other than defending the realm. The result is a cooperative religious order with shared ceremonies, recognized ranks, mutual obligations, and carefully negotiated precedence.

For formal titles and clerical hierarchy, see [[Triad Ranks]].

## The Three Pillars

The Triad is commonly explained through three sacred duties:

- **[[Heironeous]] guards the realm through courage, honor, discipline, and righteous battle.**
- **[[Pelor]] preserves the realm through mercy, healing, renewal, and protection of the common folk.**
- **[[Pholtus]] orders the realm through law, truth, judgment, and moral clarity.**

The simple village version is:

**Heironeous holds the shield.  
Pelor tends the wounded.  
Pholtus records what happened and decides whose fault it was.**

This is crude, but annoyingly accurate, which is how most useful theology survives contact with farmers.

## Founding of the Triad Compact

The Church of the Triad arose during Gorundia’s early consolidation, when local cults, knightly chapels, healing shrines, and law-priests were brought into a common royal framework.

The kingdom needed a religious structure that could support war, law, and recovery at the same time. A realm on the frontier could not afford three rival priesthoods competing for influence while border raids, plagues, succession disputes, and monster incursions politely waited their turn.

The original compact was built around three principles:

1. **No pillar may claim sole ownership of righteousness.**
2. **No royal law is complete without courage, mercy, and truth.**
3. **No temple of the Triad may deny the sanctity of the other two gods.**

This did not erase rivalry. It domesticated it. Like putting a collar on a wolf and then acting surprised when it still looks at the sheep funny.

## Shared Worship

Most major Gorundian settlements have a **Triad temple**, though the balance of emphasis varies by region.

A typical Triad temple contains:

- A martial chapel or sword-altar of [[Heironeous]]
- A sun-facing shrine or healing hall of [[Pelor]]
- A law court, record chamber, or white-lit sanctuary of [[Pholtus]]

In larger towns and cities, each god may have a separate temple while still participating in joint holy days, civic rites, and royal ceremonies.

In smaller settlements, one priest may serve as the local representative of the Triad as a whole, assisted by lay brothers, shrine-keepers, militia chaplains, healers, or traveling confessors.

## Public Doctrine of Equality

Officially, the three churches stand as equals.

No pillar is declared superior. No god is “first” in salvation, law, war, or royal blessing. During major ceremonies, prayers are usually arranged in a threefold pattern so no faith appears slighted.

A coronation, muster, noble oath, or military blessing will often include:

- A vow before [[Heironeous]] to defend the realm honorably
- A blessing before [[Pelor]] to preserve life and protect the innocent
- A declaration before [[Pholtus]] that the oath is lawful, witnessed, and binding

This equality matters. It prevents the kingdom from becoming a theocracy of one god. It also keeps ambitious priests from turning every royal crisis into a doctrinal knife fight in nice robes.

Mostly.

## The Heironean Tilt

Despite the formal equality of the Triad, Gorundian noble culture strongly favors [[Heironeous]].

This is not usually stated aloud in official settings, because even nobles can occasionally detect a rake on the floor before stepping on it. But everyone knows it.

Gorundia is a knightly kingdom. Its ruling class defines itself through land, oath, arms, courage, honor, lineage, and battlefield service. That makes [[Heironeous]] the natural patron of noble identity.

A noble house may honor all three gods, but its banners, sword-oaths, battlefield chapels, and family legends are usually Heironean in tone. A young knight is more likely to be taught the hymns of valor before the legal commentaries of [[Pholtus]] or the mercy litanies of [[Pelor]].

This creates a quiet imbalance:

- [[Heironeous]] dominates noble ceremony and military culture.
- [[Pelor]] commands deep affection among commoners, healers, soldiers, and frontier folk.
- [[Pholtus]] holds great institutional power through courts, records, investigations, and law.

The official line is that all three are equal.

The lived reality is that [[Heironeous]] gets the balcony, [[Pelor]] gets the hospital, and [[Pholtus]] gets the paperwork that can ruin your grandchildren.

## Pelor’s Role

[[Pelor]] is the most beloved of the three among the common folk. His shrines are the places people go when they are sick, frightened, grieving, hungry, or ashamed.

Pelorite priests and lay healers are often the first clergy peasants actually trust. They bless births, tend plague victims, sanctify fields, shelter refugees, and bury those who die without noble names or heraldic shields.

This gives Pelor enormous moral authority, especially in rural Gorundia and along troubled borders.

The Pelorites are often the conscience of the Triad. They remind the other two pillars that justice without mercy becomes cruelty, and valor without compassion becomes vanity in armor.

This is useful. It is also deeply irritating to people who were enjoying their righteous certainty.

## Pholtus’ Role

[[Pholtus]] is the most institutionally uncomfortable member of the Triad, which is another way of saying useful.

Pholtine clergy maintain records, oversee oaths, examine testimony, conduct investigations, and provide legal and doctrinal clarity. They are heavily involved in courts, contracts, inquisitorial procedure, and civic order.

In Gorundia, Pholtus is not merely the god of smug lantern enthusiasts, though the temptation is clearly there. His church provides the kingdom with a vital framework for law, evidence, truth, and accountability.

This also makes the Pholtines feared.

A Heironean knight may kill you in a duel.  
A Pelorite may forgive you while looking disappointed.  
A Pholtine may prove you lied in 1217 and have the document notarized.

Their role within the Triad is essential because noble honor is not always the same thing as truth, and mercy can be exploited by predators. Pholtus provides the hard edge of lawful scrutiny.

## Cooperation in Practice

The Triad functions best when each church respects the limits of the others.

In military campaigns, Heironean chaplains may bless troops, Pelorites tend the wounded, and Pholtines record oaths, crimes, battlefield claims, and accusations of misconduct.

In plague or corruption crises, Pelorites usually lead relief efforts, while Pholtines enforce quarantines and investigate cause. Heironeans provide guards, escorts, and protection against panic, raiders, or worse.

In noble disputes, Heironeans frame honor and legitimate challenge, Pholtines judge legality and testimony, and Pelorites urge restraint, reconciliation, or at least fewer corpses in the chapel aisle.

The best Triad councils feel like a balanced table.

The worst feel like three priests trying to steer one horse in different directions while the horse considers apostasy.

## Common Tensions

The Church of the Triad is stable, but not frictionless.

Common points of pressure include:

- **Noble favoritism toward [[Heironeous]]**, especially in court appointments and military honors
- **Pelorite discomfort with harsh justice**, forced resettlement, punitive labor, and noble indifference
- **Pholtine suspicion of informal mercy**, local compromise, and “honorable” noble lies
- **Heironean impatience with legal process**, especially during war or rebellion
- **Competition over royal attention**, especially during crises
- **Disputes over precedence in ceremonies**, because apparently even holy people need seating arrangements to feel alive

These tensions rarely break the Triad, but they do shape its politics.

A powerful Heironean may push for decisive punishment.  
A Pelorite may ask who will care for the children afterward.  
A Pholtine may ask whether the accused has actually been lawfully convicted.

None of them are wrong.

That is the problem.

## The Triad and the Crown

The Crown relies heavily on the Triad for legitimacy.

Royal decrees, trials, noble successions, military musters, executions, pardons, marriages, and emergency powers are often witnessed or sanctified by Triad clergy.

The Triad does not rule Gorundia, but it helps define what lawful rule looks like.

A king who loses the confidence of all three pillars is in serious danger. A king opposed by one pillar can survive. A king opposed by two has a crisis. A king opposed by all three has either become a tyrant or has hired truly terrible advisors, which is often just tyranny with extra steps.

The Crown therefore works carefully to keep the Triad unified in public, even when individual priests, bishops, magistrates, or militant orders disagree behind closed doors.

## The Triad and the Nobility

Among the nobility, association with the Triad is both sincere and political.

Most noble houses maintain chapels, sponsor priests, endow shrines, and send sons or daughters into religious service when convenient, pious, or embarrassing.

Heironean devotion is especially fashionable among martial houses. A noble line with a strong battlefield history will often claim particular favor from [[Heironeous]], sometimes with more confidence than evidence.

Pelorite patronage is common among nobles who want to appear benevolent, reformist, or genuinely care about their people, an alarming possibility but not unheard of.

Pholtine patronage is more complicated. A noble who publicly favors [[Pholtus]] may be seen as lawful, severe, ambitious, or preparing to sue someone into the grave.

## The Triad and the Common Folk

For most commoners, the Triad is not an abstract theological structure. It is local and practical.

They pray to:

- [[Pelor]] when children are sick, crops need sun, or plague comes near
- [[Heironeous]] when sons march, monsters raid, or soldiers stand at the gate
- [[Pholtus]] when contracts are broken, crimes are hidden, or officials lie

Commoners may speak of the Triad as one church, but their devotion usually shifts by need.

A farmer may curse Pholtine tax records on Moonday, pray to Pelor over a fever on Godsday, and cheer a Heironean knight by Freeday without seeing any contradiction.

This is normal Gorundian religion: practical, layered, and only tidy when written by someone who has never met peasants.

## Other Recognized Faiths

The Church of the Triad is the official state religion of Gorundia, but it is not the only religion present in the kingdom.

Gorundia has always contained older rural cults, imported faiths, household rites, professional patron gods, regional shrines, and city-specific traditions. The Crown does not pretend these do not exist. That would be stupid, expensive, and very difficult to enforce outside a capital where people can be made to stand in neat lines.

Instead, the kingdom distinguishes between **official primacy** and **lawful presence**.

The Triad receives royal recognition, precedence at state ceremonies, and institutional authority in matters of law, war, coronation, military blessing, and public moral order. Other lawful faiths may maintain temples, shrines, cult houses, monasteries, or guild-linked chapels, provided they do not challenge the Crown, subvert the Triad, traffic with forbidden powers, or start doing the sort of thing that makes knights reach for torches.

### Wee Jas

[[Wee Jas]] is known, active, and maintains temples in Gorundia.

Her clergy are respected by some scholars, nobles, mages, magistrates, and those who understand the value of orderly death rites. They are also feared, avoided, or quietly disliked by much of the common population.

There are two reasons for this.

First, Wee Jas is strongly associated with Suel religious tradition. In Gorundia, anything visibly Suel carries a faint political odor because of the Dominion. This does not make Wee Jas illegal or her worship suspect by default, but it does mean her temples are often watched more carefully than most. Fairly or unfairly, some Gorundians hear “Suel goddess” and immediately smell Dominion perfume over a corpse.

Second, her priestesses are death clergy. However refined their theology may be, most commoners do not enjoy being reminded that death has paperwork, etiquette, and professional women in ruby-black vestments who know exactly where the body should go.

Wee Jas is therefore present, legitimate, and important, but hardly popular.

Her temples are most likely to be found in cities, noble courts, mage circles, burial centers, and places where law, inheritance, magic, and death overlap.

### The Velari

The [[Velari]] are honored widely among commoners, especially farmers, village elders, herders, millers, and rural families whose lives depend less on court doctrine and more on whether the rain comes before everyone starves.

Their worship is older, softer, and more practical than the great state rites of the Triad. The Velari are tied to agriculture, fertility of land, seasonal rhythm, planting, harvest, and rural continuity.

They maintain shrines in many villages, often alongside or near Triad shrines rather than in opposition to them. A village may have a Pelorite healing shrine, a Heironean militia chapel, a Pholtine oath-stone, and a Velari field shrine without anyone thinking this is strange.

The Velari also maintain the great monastery known as the [[Abbey of the Celestial Veil]], a major religious site with broad rural influence.

Among the common folk, the Velari are not rivals to the Triad so much as the gods and goddesses people still whisper to when the priest has gone home and the lambing goes badly.

### Rudd

[[Rudd]] has a presence in Bard’s Gate, where her “temple” is functionally a casino.

This is exactly the sort of thing that makes theologians sigh into their sleeves and gamblers feel deeply validated.

Her worship is strongest among gamblers, risk-takers, adventurers, duelists, professional opportunists, and people who insist they have a system right before losing rent money. In Bard’s Gate, where luck, money, vice, and survival are often indistinguishable in dim lighting, Rudd’s cult fits naturally.

The Triad tolerates Rudd’s presence so long as her temple remains within the law, or at least within the sort of law Bard’s Gate can be bothered to enforce before lunch.

### Olidammara

[[Olidammara]] was historically the patron god of Bard’s Gate itself.

His presence lingers in the city’s older traditions, bardic houses, taverns, festivals, criminal folklore, theatrical rites, and general civic habit of treating respectability as something that happens to other people.

Even where formal worship has declined or been folded into broader custom, Olidammara remains part of Bard’s Gate’s identity. The city was never going to be purely Heironean, Pelorite, or Pholtine. Anyone claiming otherwise has either never been there or has been robbed there and chosen denial as a lifestyle.

Bard’s Gate honors the Triad because the Crown does.

Bard’s Gate remembers Olidammara because Bard’s Gate is Bard’s Gate.

### Other Oeridian Gods

Other mostly Oeridian gods also have lawful presence in Gorundia, especially through family shrines, trade cults, martial brotherhoods, bardic circles, rural rites, imported chapels, and regional patronage.

These faiths are generally tolerated when they remain compatible with royal law and public order.

The Triad’s official status does not erase them. It simply outranks them.
## Regional Variation

The balance of the Triad shifts across Gorundia.

Borderlands and knightly baronies tend to favor [[Heironeous]].  
Agrarian regions and refugee corridors often lean toward [[Pelor]].  
Cities, courts, administrative centers, and legal towns give greater influence to [[Pholtus]].

Older settlements may preserve local customs beneath Triad worship, especially harvest rites, oath stones, saint cults, ancestor chapels, or battlefield shrines.

The Triad generally tolerates these customs so long as they do not cross into demon worship, necromancy, treason, or the usual exciting hobbies of doomed villages.

## Current Pressures

In recent years, the Triad has faced increasing strain.

War, treason, infernal influence, noble corruption, refugee crises, succession disputes, and supernatural corruption have forced the three churches into closer cooperation while also sharpening their disagreements.

Key current pressures include:

- The Crown’s growing reliance on religious legitimacy during emergency rule
- Noble houses invoking [[Heironeous]] to justify private violence or revenge
- Pelorite concern over refugees, half-blood settlements, and punitive “charity”
- Pholtine investigations into treason, corruption, and unlawful magical influence
- The need to confront infernal contracts and undead threats without tearing apart jurisdictional boundaries
- The increasing visibility of powerful clerics, paladins, magistrates, and holy witnesses in royal politics

The Triad remains intact, but it is being tested.

That may be its purpose.

## Summary

The Church of the Triad is the official state religion of Gorundia and one of the kingdom’s great stabilizing institutions. It binds together the ideals of courage, mercy, and truth, even when those ideals argue like old campaigners over bad wine.

It gives the Crown legitimacy, the nobility moral language, the common folk protection, and the law a sacred witness.

Yet Gorundia is not religiously simple. [[Wee Jas]] has temples and influence, though her Suel associations and death rites make her unpopular. The [[Velari]] remain beloved among commoners and rural communities. [[Rudd]] laughs from the gaming tables of Bard’s Gate, and [[Olidammara]] still haunts the city’s old bones as its former patron.

Other gods are present.

The Triad is official.

That distinction matters.

The Triad is equal in doctrine.

It is unequal in social gravity.

It is cooperative by necessity.

It is surrounded by older, stranger, and more local devotions.

And somehow, through war, corruption, pride, plague, politics, and nobles being nobles, it still mostly works.
