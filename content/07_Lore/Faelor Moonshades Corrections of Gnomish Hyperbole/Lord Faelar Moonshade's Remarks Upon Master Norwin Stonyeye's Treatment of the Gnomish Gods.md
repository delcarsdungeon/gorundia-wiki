# Lord Faelar Moonshade's Remarks Upon Master Norwin Stonyeye's Treatment of the Gnomish Gods, Being a Theological Correction, Archival Addendum, and Patient Rebuke Concerning Divine Multiplicity, Woodland Reverence, Stone-Memory, Guild Piety, and the Perils of Mistaking Irritation for Clarity

> [!quote] Lord Faelar Moonshade  
> A people’s gods are not arranged for the convenience of outsiders. Nor, despite Master Stonyeye’s heroic disappointment, do they submit themselves to tidy shelf-labels before answering prayer.

## Preliminary Reverence

Before I begin, let the record show that my criticism is not directed toward the gods of the gnomes.

Nor toward their priests.

Nor toward household elders, charm-keepers, burrow-singers, shrine-tenders, tunnel-watchers, guild chaplains, road-blessers, mourning-stone keepers, or any of the countless humble servants by whom divine life actually persists while scholars exhaust themselves naming it.

The gods are not on trial.

Master Norwin Stonyeye’s prose is.

A lesser distinction, perhaps, but a necessary one.

Master Stonyeye has performed a useful service. He has given Gorundian readers a reasonably clear, engaging, and often accurate summary of gnomish worship as practiced among rock gnomes, forest gnomes, mixed settlements, guild circles, and Gorundian civic life.

He has also, with the confidence of a man stepping into a dark pantry while insulting the architecture, kicked several theological stools he did not know were load-bearing.

We must therefore discuss the furniture.

## On the Opening Complaint

Master Stonyeye begins defensively, protesting that he included a religious section in a prior cultural survey and was then questioned about it, as though the desire to understand a people’s gods were some exotic reader failure.

This is charming in the way a locked door is charming when one has the key.

Religion is not an appendix to culture.

It is not a decorative candle placed near the serious machinery of trade, bloodline, migration, and law. It is the grammar by which a people teaches hunger to become feast, fear to become watchfulness, craft to become offering, memory to become ancestor, and death to become something other than silence.

If readers asked questions after Master Stonyeye’s earlier remarks, they were not necessarily being careless.

They may have been paying attention.

I appreciate that this possibility may wound him.

## On “The Gnome Pantheon in Brief”

Master Stonyeye says a proper treatment would require three volumes, twelve genealogical charts, a comparative liturgical table, and a locked cabinet for Urdlen.

This is the first truly sensible sentence in the article.

Unfortunately, he then proceeds to summarize anyway.

This is not a sin. All scholarship is betrayal by compression. The error lies not in brevity, but in allowing brevity to masquerade as sufficiency.

The gnome gods cannot be properly understood as a row of assigned functions: wit, travel, illusion, smithing, vigilance, earth, invention, speech, hidden deep places, horror beneath the boards.

Such lists are useful.

They are also the theological equivalent of describing a person by inventorying pockets, teeth, and hat.

Accurate, perhaps.

Insufficient, certainly.

## Garl Glittergold

Master Stonyeye treats Garl Glittergold with evident affection and care. He rightly resists the crude outsider reduction of Garl to “mere prankster,” arguing instead for laughter as resistance, correction, survival, and social pressure.

On this point, he is very nearly eloquent.

I say “nearly” because he then explains the matter in terms of an arrogant lord falling into a dung cart, which is vivid, useful, and exactly the sort of image that will now haunt several serious theological discussions. This is what happens when one lets wizards near metaphor unsupervised.

Still, he is correct.

Garl’s laughter is not trivial. It is liturgical deflation. It is the godly refusal to let power become too solemn to be mocked. Among forest gnomes, Garl’s laughter often has a softer edge: the laugh that draws a frightened child out from under roots, the laugh that tells a patrol it has been seen but not yet threatened, the laugh shared with a fox because both parties escaped blame.

Among rock gnomes, the same laughter becomes sharper, more civic, more corrective: the humiliating device that teaches apprentices not to bypass safety locks, the public story that reduces a boastful guildmaster to proper size, the legal joke so perfect that everyone remembers the rule thereafter.

Thus Garl is not merely chief by rank.

He is chief because he preserves proportion.

And without proportion, small peoples do not survive among large ones.

## Baervan Wildwanderer

Master Stonyeye’s description of Baervan is amiable, if predictably impatient with woodland practice. He calls him patron of travel, forests, roads, hospitality, hidden paths, and the wandering that returns with maps, treaties, and cheese.

I concede the cheese.

What he misses is that Baervan is not simply a god of wandering.

He is a god of return.

The path matters because someone must come home, or find a new home, or guide another safely between homes. Forest gnome Baervan rites are filled with departure tokens, road-bread, tree marks, guest knots, and small offerings left where a path becomes uncertain. Rock gnome Baervan worship is rarer but not absent, especially among traders, couriers, tinkers, surveyors, and those unfortunate souls asked to travel with experimental equipment.

An Araisal woodland fragment, the _Waybread Verses of Little Thorn_, preserves this line:

> “Bless the foot that leaves, the hand that marks, the eye that remembers, and the door that forgives return.”

This is Baervan entire.

Not wandering for its own sake.

Movement held inside belonging.

## Baravar Cloakshadow

Master Stonyeye treats Baravar with admirable seriousness, correctly noting that concealment and illusion may protect the vulnerable rather than merely deceive the foolish.

He then uses the opportunity to insult Pholtans.

This is, theologically speaking, unnecessary.

Socially speaking, understandable.

Baravar is among the most misunderstood of the gnomish gods by non-gnomes. Elven sources tend to render him as a moon-shadow trickster, which is imprecise but not useless. Pholtine commentators often grow visibly rigid when forced to discuss him, as though the mere notion of righteous concealment places a wrinkle in the fabric of law.

Yet Baravar’s domain is not falsehood. It is sheltering uncertainty.

The hidden refugee.

The masked resistance.

The false trail.

The illusion that preserves life long enough for truth to matter later.

Forest gnomes often honor Baravar beside Baervan, because a path found must sometimes also be a path hidden. Rock gnomes honor him through concealed mechanisms, misdirection locks, decoy ledgers, false-depth vaults, and the sacred doctrine that a thief should learn humility explosively.

In the _Cloakleaf Dialogues_, a forest gnome speaker tells an elven ranger:

> “The shadow is not against the sun. It is where the small thing survives noon.”

This is better theology than many temple lectures.

## Flandal Steelskin

Master Stonyeye’s account of Flandal is strong, rooted in craft, heat, metal, endurance, and the dignity of work.

Here, his rock gnome bias serves him well.

He knows the forge-prayer because he knows the tool. He understands that liturgy may be spoken with hands, bellows, and burns. He recognizes that a hinge holding true can be a religious event, though I suspect he would rather swallow a rivet than say so plainly.

Flandal among rock gnomes is obvious: smiths, foundries, metalworkers, jewelers, pressure-fitters, clockwrights, boiler-makers, chain-makers, ward-plate engravers, and every apprentice who has been told not to touch something glowing and touched it anyway.

But forest gnomes know Flandal too.

Not as forge-lord alone, but as the ember carried in a covered pot, the knife kept clean, the cooking-hook that never bends, the bronze bell that warns the grove, the heated awl used to mark living wood without killing it.

There are forest gnome Flandal rites in which no anvil appears.

Master Stonyeye would likely find this suspicious.

The god, one presumes, manages.

## Gaerdal Ironhand

Master Stonyeye’s Gaerdal is stern, watchful, disciplined, and underappreciated.

Fair.

But incomplete.

Gaerdal is not merely the god of those who stand watch while others sleep. He is the god who makes sleep possible.

This distinction matters.

Among rock gnomes, Gaerdal guards gates, burrows, militias, caravan watches, alarm strings, fallback tunnels, emergency lamps, and whatever small but catastrophic device Uncle Vemmik is no longer permitted to store near children.

Among forest gnomes, Gaerdal is invoked less formally but no less sincerely: in thorn-wards, night circles, silent hand-signs, hidden whistles, watch-squirrels of disputed reliability, and old tree platforms from which patient eyes count danger before it arrives.

One Araisal charm-stick records:

> “Gaerdal at the east root. Baravar in the shadow. Baervan on the deer path. Let the child sleep.”

That is not a temple hymn.

It is better than one.

## Segojan Earthcaller

Here Master Stonyeye approaches reverence despite himself.

His treatment of Segojan as god of earth, burrows, dead beneath soil, deep memory, roots, tunnels, gems, and the right relation between the living community and the ground that shelters it is among the finest portions of his article.

Then he describes Segojan’s priests as “rocks with opinions and family records.”

A lesser reader may think this disrespectful.

I do not.

I have known several rocks with opinions and family records. They were called noble houses.

Segojan’s importance cannot be overstated. He binds the gnomish relation to ground: not mere mineral ownership, not mere habitation, but reciprocal dwelling. The earth holds the dead, supports the living, hides the frightened, tests the miner, and remembers what the surface forgets.

Rock gnomes tend to understand Segojan through burrow, mine, foundation, craft-stone, ancestry, and burial.

Forest gnomes understand him through root, den, hollow, grave-moss, spring-bank, and the dark below trees.

If deep gnomes exist, Segojan may be to them either ancestor, surface-name, cousin-deity, or merely the sound we make when trying to speak of a god they know better.

This possibility should humble us.

Naturally, it will not humble Master Stonyeye until it has been footnoted.

## Nebelun

Master Stonyeye treats Nebelun as patron of invention, risk, creative madness, experimentation, sudden insight, and instructive craters.

He is right.

He is also too amused by danger.

Nebelun’s worship is not merely the sanctification of irresponsible tinkering. It is the divine recognition that discovery requires transgression of the expected boundary. The first person to make fire portable was almost certainly regarded as a menace. The first person to trap lightning in a coil was probably banned from three rooms and one wedding. The first person to enchant a kettle may have been a visionary or an enemy of civilization, depending on whether it screamed.

Nebelun among rock gnomes is plain enough: artificers, alchemists, lenswrights, arcane engineers, wand-testers, valve-makers, theorem-breakers, and inventors whose eyebrows should be considered temporary.

But forest gnomes also honor him under names and moods Master Stonyeye does not record: the new knot, the untried charm, the first child to ask whether a mushroom ring might be moved, the dangerous joy of “what if?”

In the _Little Smoke Canticles_, a forest gnome charm-worker says:

> “If the old song closes the door, hum badly until it opens.”

Nebelun smiles there.

One hopes from a safe distance.

## Rill Cleverthrush

Master Stonyeye is correct that Rill is too often treated as minor by those who understand neither gnomes nor history.

He gives her speech, wit, music, satire, storytelling, verbal craft, and the ability to make tyrants look smaller without drawing swords.

Excellent.

He does not go far enough.

Rill is the keeper of continuity through voice. She is not merely bardic cleverness. She is how a people with scattered burrows, wandering traders, hidden forest enclaves, and disputed archives remains itself without requiring a central library guarded by self-important elves.

I say this as one of the self-important elves.

Among forest gnomes, Rill’s presence is profound. Children’s songs encode trail law. Insult-poems preserve political memory. Nonsense rhymes name poisonous berries. Festival riddles settle disputes too delicate for open accusation. Laughter becomes archive.

Among rock gnomes, Rill appears in guild songs, coded jokes, apprenticeship chants, mnemonic verses, debate customs, courtroom wit, and the terrifying memory of grandmothers.

One should never underestimate a grandmother favored by Rill.

There are ballads shorter than their grudges.

## Callarduran Smoothhands

Master Stonyeye treats Callarduran primarily as the god associated with deep places, stone, patience, hidden communities, and rumored deep gnomes.

He then immediately braces himself against the question of deep gnome existence.

This is understandable.

It is also a little sad to watch.

Callarduran is difficult because he belongs to the threshold between known religion and hidden possibility. Among surface rock gnomes, he may appear in miners’ invocations, quiet stone rites, and prayers before entering deep tunnels. Among forest gnomes, his presence is rare, though not absent: a god of the lower root, the hollow under the hill, the place below the fox-den where even the trees stop speaking.

In deep gnome speculation, Callarduran becomes guardian, ancestor, hidden hand, stone-palm, or, in one late elven gloss, “the patient god who does not raise his voice because the mountain already heard him.”

Is this reliable?

No.

Is it beautiful?

Yes.

Master Stonyeye may now make the face he makes when beauty becomes evidence-adjacent.

## Additional Gnomish Gods Master Stonyeye Did Not Adequately Address

Master Stonyeye warns that his summary is compressed. This is true. Compression excuses omission, but does not remove consequence.

Several divine or semi-divine figures appear in elven, gnomish, and mixed-border sources often enough to deserve mention.

## Bleredd

Bleredd appears in several rock gnome craft invocations as a patron of mines, metal, underground craft, and the transformation of raw stone into useful work. He is sometimes honored alongside Flandal, though the distinction between them varies by region.

Where Flandal is the heat, Bleredd is often the extraction.

Where Flandal sings at the forge, Bleredd listens in the mine.

In the _Underhammer Notes of Telvessin Ford_, a gnome miner gives this prayer before cutting into a new seam:

> “Flandal for the fire, Bleredd for the vein, Segojan for what we must not wake.”

This is an excellent prayer because it contains both ambition and fear.

All good mining prayers should.

## Ulaa

Ulaa, though often honored by dwarves and other hill peoples, appears in several mixed rock gnome and mountain-border communities as a goddess of hills, gemstones, endurance, and the wealth hidden beneath patient land.

Master Stonyeye omits her, perhaps because she is not strictly or exclusively gnomish.

This is precisely the wrong reason to omit her from a discussion of actual worship.

People do not worship by taxonomic permission.

Rock gnome jewelers, hill traders, and mining families in mixed dwarf-gnome regions may honor Ulaa beside Flandal, Segojan, or Bleredd. She is especially favored where gemstone wealth, hill shrines, and clan marriages tie gnome households into dwarven religious neighborhoods.

This is not dilution.

It is neighborly theology.

## Jascar

Jascar appears in certain hill and mountain traditions as a protector of communities, guardian against monstrous threats, and patron of high rugged places. He is not broadly central to gnomish worship, but in contested borderlands and fortified upland settlements, rock gnome militias and mixed gnome-dwarf households may invoke him.

A disputed Araisal copy of the _Three Hill Watches_ records:

> “Gaerdal kept the gate, Jascar held the ridge, and the little folk slept under both.”

Whether this refers to gnomes, halflings, or mixed refugees is unclear.

Naturally, that makes it more interesting.

## Sheyanna Flaxenstrand

Sheyanna Flaxenstrand appears in several domestic and romantic gnome traditions as a goddess of love, courtship, beauty, marriage, affection, and the weaving of hearts into durable household bonds.

Master Stonyeye’s omission is telling.

He has room for pressure craters, guild clauses, and Urdlen’s teeth, but not love.

One hesitates to overinterpret.

Only briefly.

Sheyanna’s worship among forest gnomes is often tied to seasonal dances, garland exchange, shared songs, and the public teasing of courting couples by everyone old enough to be cruel with accuracy.

Among rock gnomes, her rites may be quieter but no less significant: marriage clasps, braided hair-cords, shared tool-gifts, vow-ledgers, paired workshop lamps, and the charming practice of testing a prospective spouse’s patience by assembling furniture together.

This last rite is severe.

Only strong love survives it.

## The Sisters of the Golden Hills

Some gnome household traditions preserve references to lesser “sisters,” “aunties,” “bright cousins,” or “golden hill mothers,” who may be local saints, demigoddesses, ancestral spirits, or forgotten aspects of better-known gods.

Forest gnomes often treat these figures as local powers. Rock gnomes are more likely to catalogue them, argue over them, and eventually create a shelf.

Examples include:

- The Lady of the First Burrow Lamp.
    
- Aunt Embercap of the Safe Kitchen.
    
- The Laughing Midwife Under the Hill.
    
- The Copper-Eyed One Who Finds Lost Screws.
    
- She Who Counts Children in Storms.
    

Are these gods?

Saints?

Household spirits?

Folk masks for larger divinities?

Yes, depending on who is answering and whether dinner is late.

Master Stonyeye’s article does not have room for them, but gnomish religion does.

## Ehlonna and the Forest Gnomes

Master Stonyeye admits he does not fully understand forest gnome reverence for Ehlonna.

This remains the strongest theological statement he makes.

Ehlonna’s role among forest gnomes is not ornamental. Nor is it simply elven borrowing, though elven contact clearly shaped some forms. Forest gnomes honor Ehlonna because she guards the living gentleness of the world without confusing gentleness for weakness.

Among forest gnomes, Ehlonna may be invoked for:

- safe births in woodland settlements;
    
- protection of children and small animals;
    
- blessing of hidden paths;
    
- mercy after harsh winters;
    
- healing of frightened beasts;
    
- sanctifying berry patches, springs, and glades;
    
- warning against needless cruelty;
    
- the defense of beauty from appetite.
    

Master Stonyeye sees wreaths, antlers, rabbits, whispered prayers, and interpretive difficulty.

A theologian sees covenant.

The rabbit may still be only a rabbit.

But a world in which one pauses before deciding so is already holier than the alternative.

## The Triad of Law and Light

Master Stonyeye treats gnomish engagement with Pelor, Heironeous, and Pholtus as public adaptation layered atop older household and clan worship.

This is correct.

It is also more elegant than he seems willing to admit.

Gorundian gnomes are not abandoning their old gods when they honor the Triad. They are speaking to the divine powers that govern the civic, military, legal, and charitable order of the kingdom in which they live.

This is not hypocrisy.

It is a mature religious instinct.

Pelor warms the public square.

Heironeous guards the sworn road.

Pholtus binds testimony and law.

Garl laughs in the home.

Segojan remembers below it.

Flandal heats the workshop.

Baervan blesses departure.

Baravar hides the hunted.

Rill carries the story.

None of this need conflict unless a priest, scholar, or official insists that a soul must be simpler than the life it inhabits.

Master Stonyeye, to his credit, does not insist.

He merely complains loudly near the altar.

## Zilchus and the Merchants’ Guild

Master Stonyeye is correct that Zilchus is not a gnome god and that the Merchants’ Guild is not an exclusively gnomish operation.

He is also correct that commerce is not inherently wicked.

These are useful points, though he presents them with the air of a man forced to explain that rain is wet to people who brought sieves.

Zilchus is honored wherever trade becomes sacred enough to require oath, scale, witness, and restraint. In healthy practice, Zilchus sanctifies fair exchange, reliability, credit honestly extended, and profit earned without reducing the soul to coinage.

Rock gnome merchants often respect Zilchus because he governs the public trust that allows private cleverness to flourish without becoming predation.

Forest gnomes honor him less often, but traveling peddlers, road factors, herbal brokers, and charm-sellers sometimes make offerings before market crossings.

One forest gnome trader’s charm from the _Hazel Scale Papers_ reads:

> “Zilchus weigh the silver. Garl weigh the smile. Rill weigh the words. Let no one leave poorer than agreed.”

That is nearly perfect mercantile theology.

Master Stonyeye’s concern about moral drift is also valid. Trade requires trust. When trust becomes pressure, when contract becomes collar, when debt becomes appetite, Zilchus has not failed.

His worshippers have.

A god of trade is not honored by fraud any more than a god of swords is honored by murder.

## Urdlen

Master Stonyeye’s handling of Urdlen is severe, and rightly so.

Urdlen is not to be softened into mere “chthonic fear,” nor excused as an unpopular shadow-aspect, nor romanticized by bored scholars who believe forbidden things become wiser when written in red ink.

Urdlen is hunger without reciprocity.

Secrecy without sanctuary.

Burrowing without home.

Treasure without generosity.

Teeth without speech.

Master Stonyeye writes that Urdlen is the darkness that chews through the support beams.

This is good.

I wish I had written it.

I will deny saying so if asked.

Where I would add caution is this: Urdlen’s cult should not be used as a convenient dumping pit for every gnomish crime, nightmare, or social anxiety. Not every corrupt burrow hides Urdlen. Not every cruel gnome is Urdlenite. Not every cellar disappearance is cultic. Evil does not require theological sophistication. Sometimes people are simply vile, and no god deserves the paperwork.

Still, when Urdlen is present, the signs are not subtle to those trained to read them: gnawing iconoclasm, hidden hoards of useless trophies, deliberate community corrosion, trap-cruelty, blood secrecy, and the inversion of all that makes gnomish life endure.

No respectable community tolerates such worship.

On this, Master Stonyeye is entirely correct.

I expect he will find that unsettling.

## The Elder Elemental God

And now we come to a matter Master Stonyeye did not address, perhaps wisely.

There exists, in the worst margins of comparative theology, a recurring subterranean speculation concerning the so-called Elder Elemental God.

Let us proceed carefully, because words are doors and some names are handles.

The Elder Elemental God is not properly a gnomish deity. Nor is it safely described as a god in the common devotional sense. It is an ancient, dreaded, chthonic elemental power associated in various traditions with stone, darkness, pressure, deep time, hunger beneath form, and the dreadful unity of matter before moral distinction.

Most orthodox theologians, of whatever civilized faith, treat the matter with caution or silence.

This is usually the correct response.

However, the _Black Root Concordance_, a deeply suspect elven compilation preserved only in damaged abstract, contains a passage that has troubled several generations of archivists:

> “The Hidden Small beneath the last stone do not name Callarduran when the earth shakes. They press their palms flat and remember the Old Element, whose altar was weight before it was worship.”

A later marginal hand, probably not the original compiler, adds:

> “This may concern the pale gnomes below the sealed roads.”

There.

That is the source.

It does not confirm deep gnomes.

It does not confirm their worship.

It does not prove the Elder Elemental God has any living cult among them.

It does, however, preserve a tradition that some unknown subterranean “Hidden Small” were associated by at least one elven compiler with an archaic elemental power distinct from Callarduran, Segojan, or known dwarven stone gods.

This is exactly the kind of evidence Master Stonyeye hates: too specific to ignore, too corrupted to trust, too dangerous to enjoy.

I sympathize.

Briefly.

If deep gnomes exist, and if any fragment of their old religion touches the Elder Elemental God, that fact need not imply worship in the ordinary sense. It may be propitiation. Fear. Ancient captivity. A taboo-name. A memory of a force their ancestors escaped. A warning against digging past the god-line.

The difference matters.

A people may leave offerings to a horror not because they love it, but because they understand doors.

## Why This Matters

The distinction between Callarduran, Segojan, Urdlen, and the Elder Elemental God is not academic decoration.

Each answers the dark differently.

Segojan says: the earth remembers and shelters.

Callarduran says: the hidden hand endures in silence.

Urdlen says: gnaw, take, and destroy the home from beneath.

The Elder Elemental God says, if the sources may be trusted even a little: before shelter, before hand, before hunger had a name, there was weight.

This is not a comforting theology.

It may not even be theology.

It may be geology having a nightmare.

Still, if the speculative deep gnomes possess myths of such a power, then their silence, caution, and refusal of surface contact may not be mere secrecy.

It may be survival.

Master Stonyeye wants an interview.

The deep may have reasons not to answer.

## On Norwin’s Persistent Irritation With Forest Gnome Worship

Master Stonyeye ends by clarifying that forest gnomes do worship the gnome gods, also honor forest powers, local spirits, fey-touched customs, elven rites, and Ehlonna, and that he does not understand all of it.

Good.

Then he says this makes it irritating.

Also good, though not in the way he intends.

Irritation is often the mind’s protest against being enlarged.

Forest gnome religion makes Master Stonyeye uncomfortable because it refuses his preferred boundaries. It does not keep divine, local, ancestral, fey, practical, seasonal, and social devotion in separate drawers. It sets them together on the same shrine, leaves bread beside polished stone, hangs a bell over the lintel, and insists the fox knows more than it says.

This is not intellectual failure.

It is a theology of relation.

The gods are not diminished because the fox is noticed.

The fox is not deified because the gods are near.

The shrine is not confused because it has more than one guest.

Master Stonyeye should attend a forest gnome spring rite with a notebook, an open mind, and no authority to speak for the first hour.

I realize this may be asking for three miracles.

## On Norwin’s Strengths, Since Fairness Remains a Burden

It would be unjust to leave the impression that Master Stonyeye’s article is poor.

It is not.

It is lively, informed, funny, protective of gnomish dignity, appropriately hostile toward Urdlen, and impressively clear about layered worship in Gorundian society. He resists crude outsider simplifications. He respects the Triad without surrendering gnomish religious identity. He understands Zilchus without confusing commerce for ethnicity. He warns against deep gnome speculation hardening into claim.

These are virtues.

His weakness is not ignorance.

His weakness is impatience before forms of knowing that do not owe him legibility.

He trusts a ledger more readily than a song.

A contract more readily than a rite.

A calibrated spell more readily than a charm.

A categorized god more readily than a shrine with too many offerings.

This does not make him wrong.

It makes him incomplete.

The distinction is important.

Also useful.

Also, I suspect, intolerable to him.

## Final Assessment

Master Norwin Stonyeye’s account of gnomish religion should be preserved, cited, corrected, and occasionally read aloud at gatherings where laughter is permitted.

He honors the gods more than he seems to realize, even when he describes them with the manners of a man filing temple furniture by function.

His Garl is sharp and true.

His Baravar is morally serious.

His Flandal smells properly of heat and work.

His Gaerdal keeps watch.

His Segojan nearly becomes beautiful before the author panics and makes a joke.

His Nebelun is dangerous enough.

His Rill deserves more.

His Callarduran stands at the edge of the unknown.

His Urdlen should remain under lock, prayer, and watchfire.

But he omits neighboring gods and shared cults important to actual worship: Bleredd in mine and metal, Ulaa in hill and gem, Jascar in upland protection, Sheyanna in love and household bond, and the unnamed small powers by whom ordinary life is made bearable.

He treats forest gnome multiplicity as irritating.

He treats rock gnome order as clarity.

He treats deep gnome uncertainty as a problem waiting for evidence, rather than a silence that may itself be evidence of wisdom.

The gods of the gnomes are not a tidy council of assigned offices.

They are a hill full of doors.

Some open into laughter.

Some into craft.

Some into roads.

Some into law.

Some into darkness.

Some should not be opened merely because a scholar brought a lamp.

And some, I suspect, have remained closed precisely because they heard Master Stonyeye approaching with questions.
