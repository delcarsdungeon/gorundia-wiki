# Lord Faelar Moonshade's Second Set of Remarks Upon Master Norwin Stoneyeye's Treatment of the Rock Gnomes, Including Certain Necessary Observations on Bias, Stone, Mechanism, Bureaucracy, Nose-Pride, and the Long Elven Error of Mistaking One Short Bearded People for Another

> [!quote] Lord Faelar Moonshade  
> The rock remembers pressure. The root remembers thirst. The machine remembers neither, unless a gnome has first persuaded it to become troublesome.

## Preliminary Acknowledgment

Master Norwin Stoneyeye’s discussion of rock gnomes is, unsurprisingly, the most detailed portion of his report.

It is also the least impartial.

This is not accusation. It is arithmetic.

A scholar writing of his own people may possess intimate knowledge unavailable to outsiders. He may know childhood habits, inherited jokes, unspoken gestures, family embarrassments, village hierarchies, apprenticeship customs, funeral silences, table manners, and the precise insult implied when an aunt says, “Well, it is certainly ambitious.”

Such knowledge is precious.

It is also dangerous.

One sees too much from within and too little from without. Familiarity disguises assumption. Pride dresses itself as common sense. Irritation becomes principle. Taste becomes taxonomy. The local becomes normal.

Master Stoneyeye calls rock gnomes “the normal ones.”

That sentence alone should have been sealed in glass and used to teach young historians the difference between evidence and comfort.

## On “Normal”

Let us begin where the bias announces itself with charming brass bells.

Rock gnomes are not “normal” gnomes.

They are common within certain Gorundian contexts. They are prominent in Teenkwidar. They are administratively visible, commercially active, and more likely than forest gnomes to produce records in a form humans, dwarves, and guild officers can read without first asking permission from a bird.

This makes them legible.

It does not make them normative.

A people is not made central merely because it keeps ledgers well enough for outsiders to find them.

Indeed, history has suffered greatly from this error. Farmers who wrote nothing became “background.” Nomads became “irregular.” Forest peoples became “remnants.” Those who built in stone became “civilized.” Those who built in memory became “uncertain.” Those who filed charters became real.

Rock gnomes are real.

So are those who do not submit minutes.

Master Stoneyeye knows this, I suspect. He simply forgets it whenever a forest gnome elder answers a question with weather.

## On Appearance

Master Stoneyeye describes rock gnomes as shorter and sturdier than forest gnomes, compact of build, strong of hand, large of eye, and possessed of prominent noses, which he insists should be understood as attractive and distinguished among civilized persons.

Here, unusually, I have little quarrel.

The elven sources agree on the general form. Rock gnomes are consistently described as compact, durable, dexterous, and expressive of feature. Their hands receive special note in nearly every early account: quick, clever, restless, and capable of treating a locked mechanism as a conversational partner.

As for the matter of noses, I will tread carefully.

Elves have not always been wise in our descriptions of gnomish faces. Too many older court texts mention “quaintness,” “sharp little profiles,” or “earth-kindred faces,” phrases which tell us more about the writer’s distance than the subject’s appearance.

Master Stoneyeye is correct to correct this.

Among rock gnomes, a long or prominent nose may signify attractiveness, maturity, wit, family resemblance, sensory refinement, or social confidence. The elven archive contains several references to “well-bridged gnome lords,” a phrase once mistranslated by a junior archivist as “gnomes of excellent bridges.”

The resulting paper was energetic.

It was also wrong.

## Citation from the Ledger of Mirrhos-Lantern

The _Ledger of Mirrhos-Lantern_, a trade-register from the late Nilanthalasi period, includes a description of a gnome negotiator named Brinnik of the Nine Clasps:

> “He came no higher than the treaty table’s lower carving, yet his eye took in every hinge, seal, cup, lock, and lie in the room. His nose was long and finely set, which among his people caused three witnesses to trust him too quickly.”

This is valuable because it records not merely appearance, but social meaning. The nose was not incidental. It was read by his peers.

Master Stoneyeye’s pride in this matter is therefore justified.

Which I admit with only minor discomfort.

## On Clothing and Pockets

Master Stoneyeye asserts that humans see “too many pockets,” while rock gnomes see “nearly enough pockets.”

This is, in my judgment, among the strongest lines of his report.

It is also supported by material evidence.

Rock gnome garments recovered from Araisal-adjacent trade sites, old Teenkwidar exchange houses, and collapsed waystations show an astonishing fondness for hidden compartments, reinforced seams, tool loops, internal clasps, waxed note sleeves, miniature counterweight cords, and button-cavities too small for elven dignity but large enough for lock pins.

The mistake would be to treat such clothing as merely practical.

It is also philosophical.

A rock gnome garment assumes that the world will present a problem suddenly, and that the prepared person should have already hidden the answer somewhere on his body.

This is not fussiness.

It is theology in tailoring.

## Citation from The Thread-Book of Elensir Vale

A fragmentary household inventory from Elensir Vale lists a ceremonial jacket gifted by a rock gnome envoy to an elven steward:

> “Blue-brown wool, copper-threaded at the cuffs, lined with nine secret spaces and one that was declared not secret, though none could find it.”

The steward’s marginal note reads:

> “I asked whether the hidden places were symbolic. The envoy replied that symbols were all very well, but a spare fuse was better in rain.”

A useful reminder.

Rock gnomes are not without poetry.

They simply prefer poetry that can hold a fuse.

## On Temperament

Master Stoneyeye describes rock gnomes as grounded, technical, meticulous, verbal, exacting, and inclined toward systems.

This is largely accurate.

His error lies in presenting these traits as though they arise from superior rationality, rather than from a particular historical ecology.

Rock gnomes became system-builders because systems protect small communities surrounded by larger powers, dangerous tools, uncertain trade partners, underground hazards, and relatives with ideas.

A tunnel requires measurement.

A pressure valve requires calculation.

A trade contract requires memory.

A ward requires repeatability.

A mixed settlement requires rules.

A room full of gnome apprentices requires divine intervention, three exits, and someone sober enough to count sparks.

The rock gnome love of systems is not merely intellectual preference. It is survival culture, shaped by stone, trade, machinery, burrow-density, craft inheritance, and the need to make knowledge transmissible across generations that cannot afford to rediscover every mistake explosively.

When Master Stoneyeye mocks outsiders for living in buildings assembled by “feel,” he is, admittedly, correct.

He is also failing to notice that rock gnomes have their own forms of feeling. They feel tolerances through fingertips. They hear imbalance in gears. They smell sour solder. They read a room by hinge, lock, dust, ink, and posture.

This is not cold reason.

It is embodied craft.

A warmer scholar would have said so.

Unfortunately, Master Stoneyeye is a wizard.

## On Magic

Master Stoneyeye lists rock gnomish magic as tending toward enchantment, designed illusion, alchemy, optics, calibration, construct theory, applied wards, engineered spell matrices, communication devices, magical tools, keys, lenses, locks, traps, and mechanisms that only occasionally become self-aware.

This is thorough.

It is also revealing.

Rock gnome magic, by his own account, is not simply “arcane.” It is artisanal, social, commercial, and architectural. It does not merely ask, “Can this spell be cast?” It asks:

Can it be repeated?

Can it be repaired?

Can it be taught?

Can it be scaled?

Can it be hidden from thieves?

Can it be licensed?

Can it be made smaller?

Can it be made safer?

Can it be made more profitable?

Can it be made to stop screaming?

These are not lesser questions. They are civilizational questions.

High elven magic has often pursued elegance, resonance, symbolic alignment, celestial correspondence, and perfection of form across long spans of time. Rock gnome magic pursues utility refined until it becomes elegance by accident.

An elven mirror may hold moonlight for a century.

A gnome mirror may tell you whether the moonlight has been tampered with, adjust the angle, fold into a case, and include a warranty voided by swamp exposure.

Both are admirable.

Only one will come with a diagram readable by someone who did not personally know the maker’s grandmother.

## Citation from the Copperleaf Exchange Notes

The _Copperleaf Exchange Notes_, preserved in the lesser technical annex of the Allaric Archives, describe a series of meetings between elven lenswrights and gnome optical artificers. The elven scribe writes:

> “They did not ask what the lens wished to reveal. They asked how often it could reveal it before recalibration.”

At first glance this appears dismissive.

It is not.

The same scribe later admits:

> “Their smaller glass showed the flaw in our larger one.”

This is the central lesson of rock gnome craft.

Small does not mean minor.

Practical does not mean shallow.

Repeatable does not mean unimaginative.

## On Illusion “By Design Rather Than Whim”

Master Stoneyeye contrasts rock gnome illusion “by design” with the more intuitive or relational practice of forest gnomes.

Here his bias becomes refined enough to be nearly invisible.

It is true that rock gnome illusionists often favor structure: measured light, repeatable angles, prepared sound-forms, coded triggers, lens-assisted misdirection, patterned glamours, and mechanically stabilized phantasms.

But “design” is not the opposite of “whim.”

Design is merely whim that survived testing.

A rock gnome illusion may appear clean, bounded, and technical, but one should not mistake this for emotional neutrality. Rock gnome illusions often carry jokes, warnings, family signatures, guild marks, hidden insults, social traps, and layered redundancies that reflect a deep delight in making perception do honest work after first lying to it.

The best rock gnome illusions do not merely deceive.

They instruct the viewer in the consequences of carelessness.

This is why so many doors in gnome holdings appear to be somewhere else.

It is also why one should never kick a gnome footstool without confirming it is not load-bearing to a spell matrix.

## On Teenkwidar and Bureaucracy

Master Stoneyeye’s section on Teenkwidar’s guild system is perhaps the most emotionally transparent portion of the report. He defends the system. He praises its standards. He explains its necessity. Then, like a man confessing under moonlight to a crime he intends to repeat, he admits the forms are excessive.

This is welcome.

Teenkwidar’s magical bureaucracy is, by all elven accounts, magnificent, maddening, precise, restrictive, ingenious, self-protective, and almost certainly capable of achieving consciousness if enough forms are stacked in one archive during a thunderstorm.

Master Stoneyeye considers this “good.”

Mostly.

The elven view is more complicated.

Bureaucracy is memory externalized. It is a people admitting that even sharp minds die, tools are misplaced, apprentices lie, and no one should trust a verbal agreement involving alchemical vapor.

In this, Teenkwidar shows wisdom.

Yet bureaucracy is also fear externalized. Every form says: someone once failed here. Every seal says: trust was insufficient. Every category says: the world surprised us, and we intend to be less surprised next time.

In this, Teenkwidar shows injury.

Rock gnome bureaucracy should therefore be studied not merely as administration, but as collective scar tissue.

## Citation from The Sevenfold Petition of Tinkers Beneath Stone

An obscure elven diplomatic copy of a Teenkwidar petition, translated as _The Sevenfold Petition of Tinkers Beneath Stone_, contains the following clause:

> “No device shall be condemned as unsafe until the maker has been permitted to explain whether the smoke was expected, incidental, ceremonial, or hostile.”

The elven ambassador who copied the petition added:

> “Their law is a maze built by people who fear straight corridors because straight corridors allow running.”

This line has often been quoted as insult.

I read it as admiration.

A maze slows the invader.

It also slows the resident.

There is always a cost.

## On Trade

Master Stoneyeye warns the Gorundian Mages’ Guild that Teenkwidar’s reopening will bring requests for reciprocal licensing, patent recognition, component standards, spell safety classifications, and anti-plagiarism clauses enforced by “people who consider a three-page footnote to be a warning shot.”

This is extremely plausible.

It is also an understated diplomatic alarm.

Rock gnomes do not enter trade as supplicants. They enter as negotiators with long memories, small print, and the ability to determine whether your measuring rod has been shaved.

Any Gorundian institution dealing with Teenkwidar must understand that gnomish trade law is not merely a commercial habit. It is cultural defense. A people who cannot win every contest of force must make force expensive, fraud embarrassing, and ambiguity legally fatal.

This is wisdom.

It is also why human merchants find gnomish contracts “hostile.”

They are not hostile.

They are awake.

## The Obscure Matter of Lord Aethril Veyrion’s Misarrival

Because Master Stoneyeye has elsewhere expressed a certain impatience with poetic high elven primary sources, I will now provide one.

Not because it is strictly necessary.

Because it is beautiful, relevant, and likely to annoy him.

The poem concerns Lord Aethril Veyrion of Elenlindë, a minor noble of sufficient grace and insufficient attention, who in the year of the Pale Comet was dispatched to renew an old courtesy with the dwarf hold of Dern Kalduhr. Through a chain of errors involving a damaged road-marker, a mistranslated courier tablet, fog, and what one later witness called “excessive confidence in the angle of mountains,” he instead arrived at a fortified rock gnome holding.

The gnomes, realizing the mistake almost immediately, received him with such perfect courtesy that he did not discover the error for two days.

The incident is preserved in the poem _Lanthir na Gwilith Brannor_, usually rendered as _The Silver Guest at the Brass Door_.

### Original Elvish

> **Lanthir na Gwilith Brannor**  
>
> Anvarad dan ondo ú-beluva,  
> ar hiril neldë nain glavar-gelir.
> 
> “Mae govannen, heru haeron,”  
> linnant i fenn brasson nu galad-cerin.  
> Ú-varn Anga, ú-hamar-hall,  
> ancalim hend tirner, ilya min ar ilya hall.
> 
> Cúvant aranath i ú-norter,  
> laitant orod-ruith mi daer-baran nor.  
> I niphir nernir, ar suilanner lis,  
> ar dhainir gelin beriad na hîr-ethil.
> 
> Na minui anar en attëa aur,  
> na-cennant i thand: râd naugrim vanwa, gûr dramada.  
> Renant se rî, ar renner hain,  
> ar teithant neldë gostad nu mereth-arin.
> 
> A lass celeb na fenn brasson,  
> áva maegil er i vinya-nor.  
> An ondo gar nîr, ar curu gar noss,  
> ar saila gelin nó minna-thandir.
### Common Translation

> **The Silver Guest at the Brass Door**
> 
> Aethril came silver-cloaked, green-eyed, and certain,  
> following the road he believed the mountains owed him.  
> He sought the dwarf anvil beneath the stone,  
> but found instead nine bells and a hall of gears.
> 
> “Welcome, lord of height,”  
> sang the brass-bound doors in candlelight.  
> There were no iron beards, no hammer-vaults,  
> only bright eyes watching from every level.
> 
> He bowed to absent kings,  
> praised mountain smoke in burrow air,  
> and the small folk smiled, poured tea,  
> and concealed their laughter as courtesy requires.
> 
> On the second dawn, the truth was set before him:  
> his road had failed, and his pride had arrived early.  
> He laughed then, and so did they,  
> and three useful agreements were signed before midday.
> 
> Silver leaf at the brass-bound door,  
> do not ask only what came before.  
> Stone has cousins. Craft has kin.  
> Wisdom laughs before it enters.

There are several reasons this poem matters.

First, it demonstrates that elven confusion between dwarven and rock gnome holdings is not new.

Second, it demonstrates that rock gnomes have long understood how to turn outsider error into diplomatic opportunity.

Third, it preserves a rare moment in which an elven noble recognized his own mistake before composing a tragedy about it.

Fourth, it is a rebuke to Master Stoneyeye’s suspicion of poetic sources. No ledger would preserve the emotional truth of the incident so efficiently. No treaty record would capture the gnomes’ courtesy, the elf’s pride, the hidden laughter, and the fact that three agreements followed because everyone involved chose grace over humiliation.

Poetry is not the enemy of scholarship.

Bad reading is.

## Section-by-Section Corrections to Master Stoneyeye

### “Rock Gnomes are the most common gnomes in Gorundia.”

Accepted, with limitation.

Rock gnomes are the most visible and institutionally documented gnomes in Gorundian domains. Commonness in records may reflect actual population, but it also reflects settlement pattern, guild contact, trade integration, and archive-friendly habits.

Forest gnomes are less recorded because they move differently.

Deep gnomes, if they exist, are not recorded because they have had the good taste to avoid us.

### “Rock Gnomes are the dominant people of Teenkwidar.”

Accepted.

However, Teenkwidar should not be mistaken for the totality of rock gnome culture, any more than Enstad should be mistaken for all elves or Bard’s Gate for all bad decisions.

### “We are the normal ones.”

Rejected.

With sympathy.

### “Rock Gnomes are grounded, technical, meticulous, verbal, exacting, and inclined toward systems.”

Accepted.

But incomplete.

They are also humorous, anxious, proud, sensuous in craft, socially layered, materially poetic, and more superstitious than Master Stoneyeye’s tone permits him to admit.

The difference between a “safety custom” and a “superstition” is often whether the person describing it owns a certificate.

### “Rock Gnomes prefer to know why something works.”

Accepted.

Yet we must add: rock gnomes often accept that “why” may include history, prior injury, guild inheritance, social trust, material behavior, and the desire not to repeat Uncle Vemmik’s boiler incident.

Cause is rarely singular.

Explosions, especially, are collaborative.

### “Structure allows knowledge to be shared, repeated, improved, audited, and billed.”

Accepted.

This sentence may be the purest rock gnome theology in the entire document.

I recommend carving it over a guildhall door.

Preferably one that opens only after the reader has paid dues.

### “Teenkwidar’s system is good. Mostly.”

Accepted.

The word “mostly” deserves its own commentary, but I will spare the reader.

Unlike certain gnomes, I know when brevity is merciful.

Occasionally.

## On Rock Gnome Aesthetics

Master Stoneyeye’s defense of rock gnome style is more important than he seems to realize.

Outsiders frequently mistake rock gnome practicality for lack of beauty. This is a failure of perception. Rock gnome beauty lives in precision, clever concealment, durable use, proportional compactness, pleasing mechanisms, secret compartments, accurate balance, responsive tools, and the quiet thrill of something fitting exactly where it should.

Elves have often sought beauty in line, light, resonance, and symbolic continuity.

Dwarves in mass, endurance, and worked strength.

Rock gnomes in exactness.

A perfect latch may be as beautiful as a moonlit arch.

A self-leveling inkstand may contain more grace than a marble colonnade designed by twelve grieving princes.

Admittedly, it is less likely to have songs written about it.

This is our failure, not theirs.

## On Rock Gnomes and Dwarves

Rock gnomes are often compared to dwarves because both peoples work stone, trade in crafted goods, value technical skill, and are short enough that human observers become mentally lazy.

This comparison is useful only until it becomes convenient.

Dwarves seek permanence.

Rock gnomes seek function.

Dwarves ask whether the hall will endure.

Rock gnomes ask whether the hall can be ventilated, defended, lit, escaped, heated, audited, subdivided, and fitted with a lift that does not kill visitors more than once a year.

Dwarven craft is ancestral.

Rock gnome craft is iterative.

Dwarves perfect by deepening tradition.

Rock gnomes perfect by revision.

Both produce wonders.

Only one is likely to label a sacred mechanism with “do not turn unless humming.”

## On Rock Gnomes and Elves

Master Stoneyeye claims rock gnomes admire elven arcane preservation, craftsmanship, and long memory, while being less impressed by our tendency to take two centuries to choose between silver and moon-silver hinge fittings.

This is unfair.

It was one century.

The second century concerned whether the hinge should be visible.

Nevertheless, there is some substance beneath the insult.

Rock gnomes and elves often frustrate one another because both value refinement but disagree on acceptable tempo. Elves preserve possibility. Rock gnomes resolve it. Elves ask whether an object harmonizes with memory. Rock gnomes ask whether it works, whether it can work again, and whether the warranty survives being dropped down stairs.

The wisest collaborations occur when the elf prevents haste from becoming ugliness and the gnome prevents elegance from becoming paralysis.

This is rare.

When it happens, the results are extraordinary.

## On Master Stoneyeye Himself

It would be easy to dismiss Master Stoneyeye’s bias as vanity.

That would be inaccurate.

His bias is not simple vanity. It is loyalty sharpened by exasperation. He is defending a people too often simplified by outsiders, while himself simplifying others. This is a common scholarly flaw, and therefore almost comforting in its predictability.

He knows rock gnomes from within: their guilds, jokes, anxieties, strengths, absurdities, and forms.

Especially forms.

He knows them as one knows family: with love, irritation, embarrassment, and the inability to stop explaining them at length.

Such testimony is invaluable.

It should be preserved.

It should not be left unchallenged.

## Final Assessment

Master Norwin Stoneyeye’s treatment of rock gnomes is factually strong, technically useful, and interpretively compromised by the unbearable burden of being correct too often about his own relatives.

He establishes clearly that rock gnomes are the most visible and institutionally significant gnomish population in Gorundia, especially through Teenkwidar. He gives an excellent account of their appearance, craft culture, magical habits, bureaucracy, and probable diplomatic posture. Any Gorundian mage, merchant, noble, or clerk dealing with rock gnomes would be wiser for reading him.

But he also centers rock gnomes as “normal,” treats structure as superior to relation, and allows his irritation with forest gnome ambiguity and elven poetics to harden into judgment.

The rock gnomes deserve better than flattery disguised as taxonomy.

They are not normal.

They are themselves.

Stone-wise.

Gear-minded.

Ink-stubborn.

Pocket-laden.

Contract-sharp.

Secretly poetic in brass, glass, spring, latch, and measured spark.

A people who learned that the world is dangerous, and answered not by becoming larger, but by becoming prepared.

That is worthy of respect.

Even when they make one sign eleven copies before being permitted to admire the door.
