# Stories of the Grugach: The Wild Elves and the Root-Gnomes

The grugach do not hate the forest gnomes.

That should not be mistaken for fondness.

The wild elves of the Great Forest are a hard people, shaped by loss, exile, war, and the long defense of lands most others abandoned, failed, or misunderstood. They are territorial, disciplined, and slow to trust. Their silence is not peace. Their stillness is not welcome. Their patience is not mercy.

The forest gnomes know this.

Among the root-gnomes, grugach territory is taught as one teaches a child about deep water, winter ice, sleeping bears, or a bow already drawn. It is not evil. It is not to be mocked. It is simply dangerous, and a wise person does not test danger merely to prove it exists.

A forest gnome child learns early that there are places where one may trick a hunter, places where one may bargain with a hunter, and places where the hunter is grugach and the only proper wisdom is to have never crossed the boundary at all.

## The Matter of Slipperiness

The grugach do not distrust stealth.

They are masters of it.

A grugach war-band can pass through wet leaves without sound, lie unseen in fern-shadow for half a day, kill from three angles at once, and vanish before the last body finishes falling. Their hunters do not merely blend into the forest. They become part of its threat.

To the grugach, concealment is not cowardice. Concealment is discipline. Ambush is not trickery. Ambush is warcraft. Silence is not avoidance. Silence is a drawn breath before the arrow flies.

What they distrust in the forest gnomes is something else.

They distrust evasion.

To the wild elves, forest gnomes are forever slipping away from the hard moment. They hide their camps, move their children, leave warnings in feathers and knots, and vanish before the killing starts. They know the paths beneath thorn and root, but they rarely hold those paths with blood.

They survive.

They preserve.

They endure.

The grugach do not deny that this has value.

They simply do not call it courage.

A grugach hunter once said:

> “The root-folk know where to hide. Good. Let them hide. But do not ask me to call hiding courage.”

The forest gnomes remember that line bitterly.

Among the root-gnomes, the answer is older and quieter:

> “A buried seed is not brave. It is alive. In spring, that matters more.”

This is the old wound between them.

The grugach see themselves as the forest’s blade.

The forest gnomes see themselves as the forest’s hidden seed.

The blade despises the seed for not striking.

The seed fears the blade for forgetting that not everything precious can survive in the open.

## How the Grugach See the Forest Gnomes

To many grugach, forest gnomes are old forest kin, but not equals in grief, war, or claim.

They are remembered as part of Nilanthalas’ living web: burrowers, scouts, charm-workers, animal-speakers, seed-keepers, and guides of hidden ways. The grugach do not usually deny their usefulness. A root-gnome may know where water hides under stone, where goblins crossed in spring, which hollow shelters feverfew, or which trail will not bear the weight of armored men.

Such knowledge has value.

But grugach respect is rarely warm.

The wild elves consider forest gnomes clever, necessary, and often afraid. They believe the root-gnomes have made a virtue of retreat. They believe the gnomes have preserved much, but paid for that preservation by refusing too many hard stands.

A harsher grugach saying runs:

> “They love the forest as a den loves darkness. We love it as a blade loves the hand.”

Older hunters sometimes speak with more patience:

> “A hare survives the wolf by running. That is wisdom for hares. It is not wisdom for warriors.”

This judgment is not entirely fair.

It is also not entirely false.

The grugach have buried warriors.

The forest gnomes have hidden children.

Both remember what they saved.

Both remember what the other did not.

## How Forest Gnomes See the Grugach

Forest gnomes view the grugach as sacred, dangerous neighbors.

They are not monsters. They are not casual allies. They are not to be approached lightly. They are remnants of the old forest’s wrath, walking with bows in their hands and the dead behind their eyes.

A forest gnome camp may honor a grugach victory in song and still move three ridges away before dusk.

Among the root-gnomes, the grugach are respected for their courage, memory, tracking, archery, and refusal to let the Great Forest become a story told only by safer people. They are also feared for their severity, suspicion, and habit of treating trespass as a decision already made.

A common forest gnome teaching says:

> “If the trees go quiet, stop walking. If the birds leave, kneel. If the arrow lands before the warning, you have already been shown mercy.”

The forest gnomes also know that grugach rarely enjoy being teased. This is no small matter. Forest gnome humor is a teaching tool, a shield against fear, and a way of easing grief before it hardens. Grugach humor is rarer, sharper, and not always recognizable until after someone has been insulted.

A root-gnome proverb says:

> “Tell a joke to a badger and it may bite your boot. Tell a joke to a grugach and your grandchildren may need to apologize.”

## The Practical Peace

The two peoples get along best by not living too closely.

The arrangement is old and seldom written:

Forest gnomes avoid core grugach territory.

Grugach do not disturb known gnome nests unless oath has been broken.

Druidic messengers may pass between them.

Gnome scouts may warn grugach bands of goblin movement, blight, strangers, or corrupted magic.

Grugach may permit forest gnome passage in times of danger, especially for children, healers, or oath-carried messages.

Neither side enjoys long councils.

Both sides remember old debts.

When cooperation happens, it is usually quiet.

A gnome leaves three owl feathers and a carved thorn at a boundary stone.

A grugach arrow appears beside a safe trail.

A druid says nothing aloud.

The message is understood.

## Border Rules Known to the Root-Gnomes

Do not cross a line of white-barked stakes.

Do not answer unseen voices unless they name your elder correctly.

Do not imitate bird calls in grugach land.

Do not follow wounded game across a marked boundary.

Do not touch a grugach kill.

Do not laugh when challenged.

Do not explain that something was only a joke.

Do not speak of shared forest-blood unless a druid stands witness.

If given one warning, leave.

If given no warning, assume the warning was missed and leave faster.

## The Foxglove Stone

There is one known meeting place where messages may be left for the forest gnomes of the Great Forest.

It is called the Foxglove Stone.

The stone stands in a shallow dell where three old paths nearly meet but never quite touch. It is a waist-high slab of pale gray rock, split down the center by a seam of dark green moss. In spring and summer, foxgloves grow thick around it. In autumn, the flowers die back but the moss remains. In winter, snow does not settle on the stone for long.

No road leads directly to it.

Those who know the way describe it by signs rather than distance: the ash tree with two trunks, the brook that vanishes under roots, the fallen cedar shaped like a sleeping stag, and the place where birdsong stops for three breaths before beginning again.

Messages left at the Foxglove Stone are not spoken aloud.

They are placed.

A written note may be tucked beneath the moss seam, weighted with a clean pebble.

A druid may leave a braided grass cord.

A hunter may leave three feathers.

A healer may leave a strip of white cloth tied around a foxglove stem.

A desperate parent may leave a child’s carved toy.

Coins are considered rude unless wrapped in cloth and clearly offered as trade, not payment.

Weapons should not be placed on the stone unless the message concerns war.

Blood should never be placed there unless the sender wishes to be judged before being answered.

The forest gnomes do not always respond quickly. Sometimes the message is gone by morning. Sometimes it remains for three days before vanishing. Sometimes a reply appears in the same place: a knot of colored thread, a scratched mark, a folded leaf, a mushroom arranged where none grew before, or a note written in tiny, tidy letters.

Sometimes the answer comes by animal.

A crow drops a button.

A fox waits on the path and walks away only when followed.

A badger emerges, stares with grave disapproval, and scratches an arrow in the dirt.

Sometimes no answer comes.

Among those who use the Foxglove Stone properly, no answer is understood to mean one of four things:

The forest gnomes cannot help.

The forest gnomes will not help.

The request was foolish.

Or the answer has already begun, and the sender has not yet noticed.

The druids say the Foxglove Stone predates the Clay Rising.

The grugach say little about it, but they do not disturb it.

Whether this is respect, old oath, or caution is not known.

## On Kursk’s Bio-Mancers

The grugach make little distinction between the kinds of gnomes who serve Kursk.

This has become a source of fear among the forest gnomes.

Most who practice Kursk’s fleshcraft and root-surgery are believed to be renegade rock gnomes: trained hands, sharp tools, careful notes, alchemical baths, grafting frames, and the cold patience required to turn living things into systems.

But there are whispers of forest gnomes among them.

Those few are named root-butchers.

A rock gnome bio-mancer may profane life through method.

A forest gnome root-butcher profanes memory.

Such renegades may have begun as healers, fungus-tenders, animal-speakers, or charm-workers who believed the wounded forest could be restored by stronger means. Kursk offered certainty. He offered method. He offered the lie that violation becomes holy if the result is green.

The forest gnomes fear them because they know the old ways.

The grugach hate them because they prove old suspicions.

The druids grieve them because they are what happens when restoration forgets reverence.

A captured bio-mancer, in grugach hands, should expect no gentle trial. If a druid or elder intercedes, there may be questioning first. Otherwise, the sentence is usually carried by arrow, knife, or boundary tree.

This, too, the forest remembers.
